Temporary Cloudflare accounts for AI agents
⬆ 216 pts 💬 114 HN comments

Cloudflare's launch of temporary accounts marks a significant shift in how AI agents interact with deployment infrastructure. By introducing the `wrangler deploy --temporary` flag, the company removes a major barrier: the browser-based OAuth flow and manual sign-up process that previously forced agents to halt. Now, any agent can deploy a Worker to Cloudflare instantly, with the deployment lasting 60 minutes and optionally claimed permanently. This is not just a convenience but a response to the growing norm of background AI sessions with no human in the loop. The technical implementation leverages Wrangler's existing CLI, and the agent discovers the temporary flag through a prompt in the standard output, enabling seamless integration. This move aligns with Cloudflare's broader strategy to make its platform agent-ready, as seen in partnerships with Stripe and WorkOS for account provisioning.

The implications extend beyond AI agents. As noted in the comments, ephemeral deployments are a boon for PR previews and quick testing, providing a working URL without any upfront commitment. However, concerns about abuse are immediate: ephemeral accounts could become a vector for phishing, malware, and bot farms, as Cloudflare's existing abuse prevention measures are described as vague. The community also flagged the absence of hard billing caps, a persistent pain point for developers who fear runaway costs when using Workers. The article itself emphasizes a tight write-deploy-verify loop for agents, but the reality is that such frictionless access requires robust monitoring to prevent malicious use. Cloudflare's documentation mentions rate limits and abuse checks, but transparency remains a concern. Overall, this feature is a double-edged sword: it lowers barriers for legitimate developers and agents while potentially empowering bad actors.

Any agent can now run wrangler deploy --temporary and deploy a Worker to Cloudflare. This temporary deployment stays live for 60 minutes, during which time you can claim the temporary account, making it permanently your own.

Key Highlights
  • Temporary accounts let agents deploy Workers without signing up, using a --temporary flag in Wrangler.
  • Deployments last 60 minutes; can be claimed into permanent accounts.
  • Primary use case: enabling AI agents to iterate in a tight write-deploy-verify loop.
  • Concerns include abuse for malicious content, phishing, and lack of billing caps.
  • Cloudflare plans additional integrations for agent account provisioning via OAuth and partnerships.

Loupe – A iOS app that raises awareness about what native apps can see
⬆ 318 pts 💬 120 HN comments

Loupe is a newly released open-source iOS app that exposes the vast fingerprinting surface available to any native app without requiring user permissions. Developed by the privacy research group Mysk, the app reads over 100 raw signals from public iOS APIs—including locale, time zone, battery state, pasteboard change count, volume creation date, and even the ability to probe for installed apps via URL schemes and persist identifiers via Keychain across reinstalls. These signals are organized into three tiers: passive (no prompt needed), permission-requiring (triggers iOS prompts), and advanced side-channel techniques. Critically, Loupe itself does not exfiltrate any data; it simply renders the values in clear text, demonstrating how a seemingly innocuous set of readings can coalesce into a unique device fingerprint. The project was built almost entirely by AI coding tools, and the team behind it also develops Psylo, a privacy-first browser that offers anti-fingerprinting protections.

The HN community reaction is a mix of shock, resignation, and calls for systemic change. A recurring lament is that Apple does not inform users about what data apps can silently read, with one commenter noting that 'a random app you install instantly has a list of every app installed on the device'—enabling inferences about dating habits or cheating. Several commenters point out that this situation is worse than the web, where browser-based fingerprinting is at least partially countered by privacy tools. A notable technical detail emerges: while iOS restricts the number of apps an app can query via LSApplicationQueriesSchemes (max 50), data brokers can aggregate signals from many apps to build cross-app profiles. Others express frustration that 'iPhone last setup or erased' dates are exposed, calling for Apple to fudge these values. A minority of commenters view Loupe itself with suspicion, seeing it as a marketing vehicle for the paid Psylo browser, while others argue that the only effective mitigation is to minimize the number of installed apps or use a privacy-focused OS.

"Trackers don't need your name, email, or location to recognize you online. Each reading isn't necessarily unique on its own, but together they form a fingerprint that follows you across apps and websites."

  • The app is fully open-source (MIT license) and was built almost entirely by AI coding tools; it does not share any data off-device.
  • The team behind Loupe also develops Psylo, a privacy-focused browser, leading to some skepticism about the app's true motivation.

The author notes that while tooling ensures opcode faithfulness, it cannot catch data-layout bugs, making human testing essential. The call for testers has resonated: a USAF veteran expressed elation, and many commenters shared nostalgic memories of playing the game. A key technical insight is the multi-step approach: first full reverse to assembler, then conversion to binary-equal compiled C, and finally elimination of all assembly before porting to Linux/Windows. This mirrors broader trends in software preservation, where emulation is insufficient for true moddability. The project also highlights the use of AI in understanding decompiled assembly—a tool that accelerates naming and structuring—but legal questions linger about the boundaries of clean-room reconstruction.

All C code reconstructed for all executables, moving from assembly to C, yet faithful opcode checking cannot catch all bugs—v0.9.1 needs test pilots to fly and report anomalies.

Linux eliminates the strncpy API after six years of work, 360 patches
⬆ 217 pts 💬 204 HN comments

Linux 7.2 has finally eliminated the strncpy API from the kernel after a six-year effort involving 362 patches. The strncpy function, long deprecated due to its counter-intuitive semantics around NUL termination and inefficient zero-padding, was a persistent source of bugs and security vulnerabilities. Its removal marks the culmination of a methodical campaign to replace every kernel usage with safer alternatives such as strscpy (for NUL-terminated destinations), strscpy_pad (with zero-padding), strtomem_pad (for non-NUL-terminated fixed-width fields), memcpy_and_pad, or plain memcpy. This cleanup reduces the risk of buffer overflows and subtle misbehavior that have plagued kernel developers for decades.

The effort underscores the deep-seated problems with C's string handling model. As commenters noted, strncpy was originally designed for copying Unix directory entries (14-byte name fields) and was almost never the correct function for general string copying. The kernel's migration required not only mechanical substitution but also careful semantic review to choose the right replacement for each context. The debate over alternatives like strlcpy versus strscpy highlights ongoing tension between interface clarity and performance. While some lament the lack of a proper string datatype in C, the systematic removal of such a notorious API is a testament to the kernel community's commitment to long-term reliability over flashy features.

After 362 patches over six years, the Linux kernel has finally eradicated the 'persistent source of bugs' that was strncpy.

Key Highlights
  • Linux 7.2 removes strncpy after six years and 362 patches.
  • strncpy was notorious for confusing NUL termination and unnecessary zero-filling.

Marc Brooker's article 'Alice is impatient' dissects a fundamental measurement fallacy in distributed systems: the inspection paradox. While operators measure mean latency per request or mean time to recovery (MTTR) using uniform event counts, users experience a time-weighted mean—each wait is weighted by its duration. Mathematically, if service times follow distribution f(t) with mean E[X], users perceive E[X^2]/E[X] = E[X] + Var(X)/E[X]. This skews perception: a service with median 30ms and p99 600ms may claim 100ms mean, but users feel ~1s mean wait. Brooker illustrates this with a log-normal simulation, showing how heavy tails dominate customer experience. He argues this is critical for recovery times, where retry hiding isn't possible, and critiques trimmed means for discarding tail information.

The article positions tail latency as not just a performance nuance but a perceptual reality. For recovery, a median 30-minute MTTR with a p99 of 10 hours yields a user-experienced mean recovery of ~6 hours—far exceeding the service metric. Brooker references Little's Law and prior work on load balancing. HN comments reflect deep engagement: some appreciate the observer-operator distinction, while others demand more mathematical rigor or question whether per-request metrics are misleading. The debate extends to alternative metrics like per-user experience and the role of queueing theory. Overall, the piece catalyzes discussion on how we measure and communicate system performance to users who live in wall-clock time.
