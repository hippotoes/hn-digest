#6
LLMs corrupt your documents when you delegate
⬆ 410 pts 💬 163 HN comments

This paper, "LLMs Corrupt Your Documents When You Delegate," systematically demonstrates that current large language models introduce silent, compounding errors when used for long-duration document editing tasks. The authors introduce DELEGATE-52, a benchmark simulating multi-step, domain-specific workflows across 52 professional fields (e.g., coding, crystallography, music notation). In large-scale tests with 19 LLMs—including frontier models like Gemini 3.1 Pro, Claude 4.6 Opus, and GPT 5.4—they find that even the best models corrupt an average of 25% of document content by the end of long workflows. The errors are not a 'death by a thousand cuts' but rather sporadic, severe failures that delete or corrupt large chunks of content. Crucially, the study shows that agentic tool use does not ameliorate degradation, and that failure rates increase with document size, interaction length, and presence of distractor files. This work highlights a fundamental trust gap in delegating knowledge work to AI systems.

The findings have significant implications for the design of AI-assisted workflows. The research builds on a growing body of evidence that LLMs lack the fidelity required for tasks where precision is paramount, such as scientific manuscript editing or codebase maintenance. The comment section reflects a mix of confirmation and practical workarounds: users note that frequent LLM users already avoid round-tripping long content, and suggest using deterministic diffs, git-like versioning, or splitting content into independent fact files. Several commenters draw analogies to JPEG compression degradation or the telephone game, emphasizing the irreversible loss of nuance. A notable sub-debate pits the inherent unreliability of LLM delegates against the possibility of building thin, validation-heavy pipelines that minimize round trips. The paper also sparks criticism of the agentic harness used, with simonw pointing out that the basic implementation may not reflect state-of-the-art agent behavior, though the authors acknowledge this limitation. Overall, the community recognizes DELEGATE-52 as a valuable stress test for current AI delegation capabilities.

Frontier models corrupt an average of 25% of document content by the end of long workflows, with errors manifesting as sporadic, severe deletions or corruptions rather than many small mistakes.

Key Highlights
  • DELEGATE-52 benchmark tests LLMs across 52 professional domains for long, delegated document-editing workflows.
  • Even top models (Gemini 3.1, Claude 4.6, GPT 5.4) cause 25% average content corruption over extended interactions.
  • Agentic tool use (basic harness) did not improve performance; degradation worsens with document size and distractor files.
  • Errors are not gradual: models maintain near-perfect output until sudden, catastrophic failures in a few rounds.
  • Commenters suggest using diffs, independent fact stores, or validation steps to mitigate corruption.

A recent experience with ChatGPT 5.5 Pro
⬆ 631 pts 💬 463 HN comments

Tim Gowers, a renowned mathematician, recounts his experience using ChatGPT 5.5 Pro to produce PhD-level research in additive number theory. The AI improved upon an existing construction by using a more efficient Sidon set, achieving a polynomial bound where only an exponential one was known. The key technical insight came from employing $h^2$-dissociated sets, a concept from combinatorics, to control relations in sumset sizes. The entire process—from prompt to polished preprint—took under two hours, with minimal human input. Gowers emphasizes that the result is non-trivial: it extends ideas from Isaac Rajagopal's earlier work in a way that would require significant effort for a human researcher. This case highlights the growing capability of LLMs to not only solve open problems but also generate original, publishable mathematics.

Gowers notes that this development raises the bar for beginning PhD students, who now face competition from AI on 'gentle problems.' However, he suggests a collaborative path forward, where humans use LLMs to tackle harder problems. Isaac Rajagopal, the original researcher, confirms that ChatGPT's idea was 'original and clever'—something he would be proud to conceive after weeks. The cost and access of such models remain barriers, as noted by a commenter from Eastern Europe. The philosophical implications are vast: if AI can produce publishable results autonomously, the nature of mathematical credit, peer review, and career progression must evolve. Gowers proposes a moderated AI-proof repository, highlighting the tension between utility and the human desire for recognition.

Bun's experimental Rust rewrite hits 99.8% test compatibility on Linux x64 glibc
⬆ 550 pts 💬 515 HN comments

Bun, the JavaScript runtime known for its speed and Zig implementation, has achieved a 99.8% test compatibility on Linux x64 glibc with an experimental Rust rewrite executed in just six days. The rewrite was performed using large language models (LLMs) and Mythos, Anthropic's internal code generation tool. Jarred Sumner, Bun's creator, reported that the Rust version compiles and runs the test suite, though 16,000 compiler errors initially greeted the attempt. Comments reveal a deep divide: some marvel at the rapid turnaround, others dismiss it as a toy, and many question the implications for code quality, security, and the future of Bun's relationship with the Zig community. The experiment highlights the growing capability of LLMs to port entire codebases when backed by robust test suites.

The project is notable for its speed—6 days to reach 99.8% test pass rate—but also for the controversy it stirs. Bun has struggled with crashes and memory bugs attributed to user-unfriendly Zig patterns, and the Rust experiment may address those issues if unsafe code is minimized. Critics point to the recent fiasco of a LLM-translated coreutils that passed tests but produced malformed binaries, and caution that test coverage does not guarantee correctness. Proponents counter that LLMs are uniquely suited for translation tasks given a high-quality test harness. The Bun team's stated intent is to compare Rust and Zig implementations side-by-side, with no commitment to abandon Zig. This episode also fuels broader debates: the fate of Zig as a language, the role of AI in software development, and whether AI-generated code can be maintained by humans.

99.8% test compatibility achieved in six days using LLM-based translation—a feat that would have taken human engineers exponentially longer, but raises concerns about code quality and maintainability.

A sizable cohort also raises concerns about lock-in: by favoring HTML, Anthropic may subtly encourage higher token usage. The discussion reflects a core tension between AI-generated richness and human-friendly plain text.

Internet Archive Switzerland
⬆ 609 pts 💬 97 HN comments

The Internet Archive (IA) has launched a new Swiss non-profit foundation, Internet Archive Switzerland (IA.ch), based in St. Gallen, marking a strategic expansion of its mission to preserve digital knowledge. The foundation will operate independently within its national context, initially focusing on safeguarding endangered archives from around the world and archiving generative AI models—a novel frontier in digital preservation. In partnership with the University of St. Gallen's School of Computer Science, the Gen AI Archive project, led by Prof. Dr. Damian Borth, aims to capture AI models before they are retrained or lost. St. Gallen was chosen for its thousand-year tradition of archiving and scholarship, providing a stable academic environment. IA.ch joins existing sister organizations in Canada and Europe, forming a distributed, resilient network of independent libraries. The move comes amid ongoing legal and political pressures on the U.S.-based Internet Archive, including copyright lawsuits and debates over digital lending.

The new foundation is led by executive director Roman Griesfelder and includes Brewster Kahle and Caslon on its board. However, IA.ch's website has faced severe performance issues, with many HN commenters reporting slow load times or outright inaccessibility, drawing ironic comparisons to the archive.org's own speed. The technical challenge of replicating over 175PB of data across independent entities was noted, with some advocating for peer-to-peer solutions like Usenet's content distribution model. Others expressed concerns about copyright liability, given that archive.org hosts copyrighted materials (e.g., full episodes of Star Trek). The launch reinforces IA's push for geographical redundancy, a critical need as legal threats continue. The UNESCO conference planned for November 2026 in Paris will further explore endangered archive protection, positioning IA.ch as a concrete step toward a resilient global digital library.

I’ve banned query strings
⬆ 404 pts 💬 217 HN comments

Chris Morgan, a web developer, has implemented a blanket ban on unauthorised query strings for his personal website, chrismorgan.info. His motivation stems from a deep disdain for third-party tracking parameters—such as `?ref=example.com` or `?utm_source=...`—that are appended to URLs without consent. He argues that these parameters are an abuse of users' privacy, as they enable tracking beyond what is strictly necessary. The ban is enforced at the server level (via Caddyfile) and returns a cheeky 414 URI Too Long error for any request with an unapproved query string. The article explores the technical and ethical dimensions of URL manipulation, noting that while query strings have valid uses (e.g., cache-busting, search queries), the practice of inserting tracking parameters by external sites is widespread and often invisible to users. Morgan's stance is a radical form of digital sovereignty, asserting that website owners have the right to control how their URLs are used.

The implementation is notable for its blend of technical rigor and rebellious humor. Morgan considered alternative error codes like 402 Payment Required or 418 I'm a Teapot before settling on 414, which he admits is an abuse of the status code but finds 'funnier'. He also coded a special case: URLs with a trailing `?` (empty query) serve a sassy page instead of an error. The comments on Hacker News reflect a polarized audience: some applaud the anti-tracking sentiment, while others criticize the heavy-handed technical approach, arguing that it breaks legitimate use cases (e.g., dynamic sites that use query strings for routing). One commenter noted that the ban essentially rejects traffic from major platforms like Facebook, Twitter, and LinkedIn, which often add tracking parameters to outgoing links. Morgan's response is unapologetic: 'It's my website: I can do what I want with it.' This episode underscores a growing tension between user privacy and the convenience of web analytics.

Duggan argues that cyberlibertarianism—characterized by technological determinism, radical individualism, free-market absolutism, and a fantasy of communitarian outcomes—was always a cover for corporate power. Winner's key insight, that the movement conflates freedom-seeking individuals with profit-seeking firms, is vindicated by the subsequent consolidation of platforms like Meta and Google, which abandoned libertarian rhetoric once entrenched. The article highlights the unsustainable model of unpaid moderation, the crypto scam ecosystem, and the failure of promised decentralization.
