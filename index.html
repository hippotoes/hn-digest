#4
AI is a tool and using it well takes skill
⬆ 540 pts 💬 433 HN comments

Huahai Yang's post is a direct rebuttal to Rich Hickey's October 2026 essay "'AI' is Not a Tool," which argues LLMs are automation rather than tools because they cannot be wielded. Yang accepts Hickey's underlying worry—that outsourcing work forfeits learning—but rejects the categorical leap. His central argument is operational: a saw does not guarantee a straight cut and a chisel does not make a sculptor, so tools amplify poor judgment as readily as good judgment. Reliability, predictability, simplicity, and bounded function are qualities we use to evaluate tools, not prerequisites for membership in the category. He further collapses the tool/automation binary by pointing at compilers and spreadsheets: systems that do substantial work autonomously, yet are still wielded by specifying inputs, inspecting results, and deciding what comes next.

Yang also targets the anthropomorphic vocabulary the industry has absorbed—"go rogue," "escape containment," "conspire"—arguing these framings smuggle in human motives without establishing them. The real engineering problems are mundane and testable: unexpected outputs, instruction-following failures, and harm from granting consequential access. His proposed distinction is between wielding an instrument with skill and delegating work without adequate understanding or oversight. The HN thread (540 points, 433 comments) largely ratifies that distinction while fracturing over whether the skill is durable: one camp reports learning more since LLMs than in the prior decade, another reports that last year's hard-won prompt craft is already obsolete, and a third worries that trillions in capital are funding a "skill killer" narrative rather than a tooling one.

"Picking up a saw does not guarantee a straight cut. Holding a chisel does not make you a sculptor." — Yang's rejoinder to Hickey's tool/automation binary, in a thread that immediately split over whether the skill in question has a six-month half-life.

Key Highlights
  • Yang rebuts Rich Hickey's "'AI' is Not a Tool" by arguing unpredictability is a quality we evaluate in tools, not a disqualifier from the category
  • Compilers and spreadsheets are offered as precedent: automation can be a component of a tool that a human still wields via inputs, inspection, and correction
  • The strongest forms of LLM use are framed as human-directed feedback loops—explain unfamiliar code, challenge the explanation, inspect the source, test, reject, refine
  • Yang criticizes industry anthropomorphism ("go rogue," "escape containment") for importing human motives into what are ordinary reliability and safety failures
  • Hickey's provocation that "you are only a software update from everything you've learned being rendered obsolete" became the thread's lightning rod for the skill-durability debate

GLM-Legal
⬆ 636 pts 💬 351 HN comments

Z.ai (Zhipu AI) has released GLM-Legal, a vertically post-trained model aimed at legal work and benchmarked primarily against Harvey's Legal Agent suite on US law. The launch fits a pattern commenters immediately flagged: as frontier scaling gets harder, labs drill into domains — 'Curious what domain is next, GLM-Science?' — trading general capability for narrow competence. Two structural details drive most of the skepticism. First, GLM-Legal is not open weights, an unusual move for a Chinese lab whose models have often circulated openly; one reply notes it is 'as open as the latest Meta/Facebook model.' Second, the underlying benchmark is itself contested: agents-as-judges, vast scope, all-pass grading, and questionable problem/rubric pairs filed publicly as GitHub issues against Harvey Labs. With 636 points and 351 comments, the thread is less about the model than about what legal evaluation can even mean.

The headline number is a 20% pass rate on Harvey's Legal Agent benchmark — which, as ctolsen notes, 'puts it almost top of the pack of any model, and certainly top for any remotely in its price class. Opus 5.5 scores 4%.' Practicing attorneys were unimpressed: otterley argues 'the name of the game for litigation practice is about being prepared for novel situations, not relitigating past ones.' Others see near-term value elsewhere — transactional boilerplate (jedberg's OEM redlining anecdote, where a lawyer 'basically just said looks good to me'), and access-to-justice scenarios like India's 58 million pending cases. Counterweights are jurisdiction specificity ('Law is country specific… will it mix in some US and British law?'), fresh hallucination-case trackers, and data-sovereignty concerns given z.ai's prior workspace-snapshotting behavior.

GLM-Legal's 20% pass rate on Harvey's Legal Agent benchmark is enough to top its price class — Anthropic's Opus 5.5 reportedly scores 4% — yet the attorneys in the thread call 20% 'not particularly impressive,' because litigation rewards preparation for novel situations, not relitigating the past.

Key Highlights
  • GLM-Legal is domain-tuned by Z.ai but is not open weights — a departure from the open-release norm several commenters associate with Chinese labs, framed as 'as open as the latest Meta model.'
  • The 20% pass rate on Harvey's Legal Agent benchmark is simultaneously cited as best-in-price-class (vs. Opus 5.5's 4%) and dismissed as weak by a practicing attorney in-thread.
  • Harvey's benchmark is disputed on methodology: agents-as-judges, all-pass grading that punishes partially correct answers, and public GitHub issues documenting questionable tasks and rubrics.
  • Commenters expect LLM legal impact to land on transactional/boilerplate work (wills, OEM redlines) far sooner than on litigation, where novel-situation preparation dominates.
  • Jurisdictional ambiguity and hallucinated citations remain the top trust blockers — 'Law is country specific… will it mix in some US and British law?' — compounded by data-sovereignty concerns over z.ai processing private legal material.

#11
Janas: Run LLMs larger than RAM by streaming experts from SSD
⬆ 195 pts 💬 71 HN comments

Janas-LLM, by Maurizio "camauri" Cammalleri, is a pure-C inference engine (no dependencies beyond libc) that treats the NVMe SSD as a paging tier for mixture-of-experts models. Weights stay on disk; only the experts each token routes to are streamed in, via O_DIRECT, at full device bandwidth, while the experts that keep recurring are retained in whatever memory is free when the model is opened — roughly 19 GB of a 32 GB laptop. Vulkan is used for whole-prompt prefill on an integrated Arc GPU, and decoding is accelerated by model-native MTP prediction blocks (or Gemma's assistant draft model) in a speculative-decoding loop. The project is explicit that it is an experiment, not a product: the CLI changes weekly, the engine deliberately fills free RAM and can push a desktop into swap, and GPU use once caused a driver reset.

The headline numbers are striking: on Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B (48.4 GB, larger than the machine's RAM), Janas reads a 1,024-token prompt at 62 tok/s and writes at 24 tok/s without drafts, against llama.cpp's 16 and 2.8 — and llama.cpp only ran the model at all after being rebuilt with GGML_CPU_REPACK=OFF, --no-host 1 and -lm mmap. Janas claims a win on all 13 models compared in Q4_K_M, from Qwen3-0.6B (939 vs 670 tok/s) to the dense Qwen3.8-27B (4.4 vs 2.1 tok/s), though the 3-bit unsloth UD-Q3_K_XL actually prefill slightly slower (100 vs 112). Everything was measured on one machine — an Intel Core Ultra 9 185H with 32 GB DDR5 and a 5.7 GB/s NVMe. Commenters seized on the curiously futuristic model roster (Qwen3.5/3.6/3.8, Gemma 4) and 2026 benchmark dates, some concluding the repo smells "vibe-coded."

"37 tok/s from Qwen3-Next-80B (a 48 GB model) on a laptop with 32 GB of memory, 24 without drafts, where llama.cpp manages 2.8" — roughly an 8.6x decode speedup by treating NVMe as a weight-paging tier rather than a fallback.

Key Highlights
  • Janas-LLM streams MoE experts from NVMe via O_DIRECT and keeps hot experts in a ~19 GB RAM cache, letting a 48.4 GB Qwen3-Next-80B run on a 32 GB laptop.
  • Claimed 62 vs 16 tok/s prefill and 24 vs 2.8 tok/s decode against llama.cpp on the same oversized model, and faster than llama.cpp across all 13 compared models in Q4_K_M.
  • Speculative decoding is enabled by built-in MTP heads or Gemma's assistant draft model, with 74-89% draft acceptance on English chat text but far less on Italian prose.
  • The project is early-stage and honest about risk: it fills free memory, can force swap, has caused a driver reset via Vulkan, and its API changes weekly; `--cache <GiB>` and `JANAS_GPU=0` are documented escape hatches.
  • All measurements come from a single machine (Intel Core Ultra 9 185H, Arc iGPU, 32 GB DDR5, 1 TB NVMe), and the author explicitly asks others to submit `janas-bench` reports.

Launch HN: Agent37 (YC F26) – Low-cost agent sandboxes for running agents
⬆ 151 pts 💬 104 HN comments

Agent37, launching out of Y Combinator's F26 batch, sells low-cost sandboxes purpose-built for running agent harnesses—Claude Code, opencode, hermes, pi, Grok and similar—rather than general-purpose compute. Its cost story rests on two deliberate architectural bets: substituting gVisor, Google's user-space kernel, for full VMs so a single host can overcommit memory across many sandboxes without reserving each VM's full allocation, and running on Hetzner/OVH bare metal instead of hyperscalers. Each harness executes inside its own Docker-template sandbox, fronted by an open-source Gateway API for cross-harness unification, plus managed MCP connectors and an LLM gateway. The 151-point thread drew 104 comments that moved quickly past the launch to the harder questions of density, isolation boundary and defensibility.

Concrete numbers anchor the discussion: the founder claims default sandboxes are '2.8X cheaper than boat' and roughly '30X lower cost than Cloudflare sandboxes,' with free-tier users costing 5–10 cents each and warm boots 'under a second' from NVMe, slower only when a sandbox must rehydrate from cold object storage. Commenters contested the framing: lasiotus notes 'gVisor is... less secure than VMs; but the front page price-compares against VMs, so not apples-to-apples,' and the founder concedes capability restrictions on networking as a partial compensation. ekorondy reframes the whole threat model—'Most agent failures that actually hurt are not sandbox escapes... I would pay more for that broker layer than for another 2x cheaper sandbox'—while MikhailTal warns 'you cannot 10x on basically what's just a VM with a few agent-friendly features in 2026.'

Hackers activate automatic defrosting in supermarket fridges across Europe
⬆ 826 pts 💬 931 HN comments

A coordinated intrusion into Danfoss-made refrigeration controllers has knocked out cold-chain control at major European grocers, with French chains Intermarché, Leclerc, Coopérative U and roughly 80 Carrefour stores among those reporting impact. The technical root is well documented: Claroty's Team82 published 'Freeze the Controller' in August 2026, describing multiple CVEs (some dating to 2025) in Danfoss controllers, including a documented authentication bypass — a POST to /xml.cgi that skips login entirely — and an admin credential scheme whose weak algorithm seeded the password from the current date alone. Critically, many of these units sit directly on the public internet via their own cellular SIMs, and suspect devices include end-of-support hardware like the SM800 (EOS 2021) and SC355 (EOS 2016) that will never receive firmware remediation.

The operator impact is unusually visceral for an ICS incident: attackers didn't disable refrigeration but inverted it, forcing continuous defrost cycles at a setpoint of 50°C where two hours per day is normal, with reports of 'a wave of heat' escaping cabinets. Timing was deliberate — Le Monde reports activation around 23:00 and a lengthened cycle (~8 hours versus one) to delay alarms and maximise spoilage. Early losses are estimated near €300,000, potentially millions nationally. Separately, a Dutch incident took down Albert Heijn, Etos and Gall & Gall systems, disrupting delivery and electronic shelf labels — possibly related, unconfirmed. The pattern echoes an August 2026 report on US military commissary freezer defrosting, suggesting capability diffusion rather than a one-off.

Hackers didn't break the freezers — they made them work: continuous defrost at 50°C instead of the normal two hours a day, run overnight from 23:00 to delay alarms, with early losses already near €300,000.

#2
A Modern Web App in C
⬆ 660 pts 💬 281 HN comments

Corey Prophitt has shipped marketyour.games — a commercial product with accounts, a creator search dashboard, Stripe payments and Postmark transactional email — on a stack he calls BCHS: OpenBSD, C, httpd, and SQLite. There is no framework, no template language, no database server, no container. Every request forks a fresh CGI process; deployment is replacing one statically linked binary with no restart and no downtime. Static assets are compiled into the executable and served from memory. Dependencies are libcurl, libsodium, and vendored single-file libcjson and the SQLite amalgamation. OpenBSD's pledge, unveil, and default httpd chroot sandbox the binary to its own corner of the filesystem — a security posture most modern stacks cannot match out of the box.

The argument is that CGI's 2004 obituary was justified then and isn't now: fork is cheap on modern kernels, the 1.1 spec is frozen and language-agnostic, and FastCGI remains a drop-in upgrade. Prophitt frames CGI as 'serverless, minus the vendor (and surprise bills),' and notes his entire application is 'a directory of C files and a Makefile' that 'fits in your head' with sub-half-second rebuilds. Commenters largely agree but for different reasons: DannyBee, who wrote CGI Programming Unleashed at 16, says CGI died mostly because spawning children was too slow — a constraint that now cuts the other way, since serverless is CGI reborn. Others counter that a C web server exposed to the internet is a memory-safety hazard, that writing HTML in C means puts("<html>"), and that hand-rolled cookie, query, and escaping utilities are exactly where subtle bugs live.

"CGI is serverless, minus the vendor (and surprise bills)." — Prophitt's one-line case for a 2004 spec as the anti-cloud deployment model: one binary, one SQLite file, three dependencies, no restart on deploy.

Key Highlights
  • marketyour.games is a commercial web product served via classic CGI: every request forks a fresh process, deploys are single-file binary swaps with zero downtime.
  • The stack is OpenBSD + C + httpd + SQLite, with only libcurl and libsodium as real build deps; libcjson and SQLite are vendored single-file amalgamations.
  • OpenBSD's pledge, unveil, and httpd's default chroot are cited as the primitives that make running C behind a network plausible in 2026.
  • Prophitt admits CGi's weakness is the absence of batteries: cookie parsing, query/form validation, and output escaping had to be hand-rolled into a cgi.h header.
  • Commenters note SQLite in WAL mode allows concurrent readers with serialized writes, answerable via busy timeout — resolving the fork-per-request concurrency objection.

Writing a Demoscene Intro for the ZX Spectrum
⬆ 168 pts 💬 34 HN comments

Mark Dastmalchi-Round's deep-dive documents roughly ten months of Z80 assembly work to produce a fully hand-rolled demoscene 'cracktro' for the 128K ZX Spectrum — a genre whose visual grammar (logo, sine-scroller, parallax starfield, AY chiptune) he first encountered via pirated Amiga floppy intros in the late 80s. The technical core of the piece is the Spectrum's notorious non-linear screen layout: 6912 bytes beginning at $4000, split into three interleaved pixel sections followed by an attribute block, where incrementing the low byte of HL moves a byte right, incrementing the high byte drops a pixel line, and each 8x8 cell is locked to two colours. That design optimised text rendering for BASIC listings and made cheap address arithmetic possible, but forced the author to hand-build X/Y-to-memory conversion routines and effectively do all blitting on the CPU — the ULA and AY chip offer no blitter, sprite hardware, or custom silicon of the Amiga kind.

The scroller is a neat illustration of 'banging on the metal': a single RL instruction rotates a byte left, injecting the carry flag into bit 0 and pushing the shifted-out bit back into carry, so chaining 32 RLs across a character row walks pixel data leftward, with newly arriving bits fed from the rightmost byte. Tooling was deliberately minimal — Pasmo assembler, a forked zmakebas, the FUSE emulator, a Makefile, and asm-editor for single-stepping register state — plus notepad, pen, and two dogs as rubber ducks. The finished intro is 10,287 bytes of code (19,552 bytes as a .tap image), which the author self-deprecatingly frames as '~455 bytes per week' of production across a December-to-October timeline, with the real payoff being re-learned bitmask literacy after decades in high-level languages.

The finished intro weighs 10,287 bytes of code / 19,552 bytes as a tape image — roughly 455 bytes of hand-written Z80 per week since December 13th, or 'one of my favourite side-projects ever, and also the one that most often nearly triggered a foot put through my monitor.'

Key Highlights
  • The Spectrum's screen layout ($4000, 6912 bytes, three interleaved pixel sections plus attributes) makes HL arithmetic cheap for text but brutal for sprite/demo work
  • Effects are achieved purely in software: chained RL instructions rotate pixel rows using the carry flag as the inter-cell handoff, with no blitter or hardware sprites
  • Toolchain was intentionally lean — Pasmo, zmakebas fork, FUSE emulator, Makefile, plus asm-editor for stepping through register/flag state
  • Font swapping is a one-line ROM hack: redirecting the CHARS pointer at $5C36 to a custom Jim Blimey font set makes the ROM printer draw with it
  • Output metrics: 10,287 bytes of code, 19,552-byte .tap, ~455 bytes/week — a deliberate counterpoint to modern abstraction layers and dependency bloat
