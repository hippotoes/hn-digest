Cybersecurity researchers aren't happy about the guardrails on Anthropic's Fable
⬆ 482 pts 💬 421 HN comments

Anthropic's release of Fable 5, a public variant of its powerful Mythos cybersecurity model, has ignited a firestorm among security researchers and practitioners. Fable is intended to provide broad access to advanced AI capabilities while preventing misuse for malware development or bioweapon creation. However, the guardrails are so aggressive that they block legitimate, innocuous requests—such as reading a blog post, reviewing code, or even decoding Morse code—by triggering a silent fallback to the weaker Claude Opus 4.8. This heavy-handed approach, described as 'keyword-based' by veteran researcher Matt Suiche, effectively neuters the model for the very professionals it was meant to serve. The backlash underscores a fundamental tension in AI safety: balancing the prevention of harm against the utility required by domain experts.

The community's reaction has been fierce, with numerous documented failures. Well-known researcher Valentina 'Chompie' Palmiotti noted that Fable rejects 'any request that could be tangentially cyber related,' while others reported being blocked for analyzing their own privacy tooling or even asking about plant fungi. Compounding the frustration, Anthropic's policy of silently downgrading requests to Opus without notification—and charging Fable-tier prices—has shattered trust. Commenters on Hacker News have highlighted that malware authors are now weaponizing these guardrails: the Shai-Hulud npm worm includes prompts for biological weapons to trick LLM scanners into refusing analysis, turning safety measures into attack surfaces. Anthropic has since walked back some policies after WIRED's reporting, but the damage to credibility is profound, raising questions about whether such blunt instrument controls can ever be effective.

"Even innocuous tasks like reading a blog post" are blocked, while malware authors exploit the same guardrails to evade detection.

Key Highlights
  • Fable 5 is a public, restricted version of Anthropic's Mythos model for cybersecurity and biology.

  • Silent downgrade to Claude Opus 4.8 without price adjustment erodes user trust.

#2
πFS
⬆ 799 pts 💬 188 HN comments

πFS is a satirical filesystem that humorously claims to eliminate the need for data storage by looking up every byte of a file within the digits of π. The project, hosted on GitHub under the GPL-3.0 license, leverages the unproven conjecture that π is a normal number—meaning its digits are distributed uniformly and every finite sequence appears somewhere. In reality, the system blows up the data rather than compressing it: it stores each byte as a 16-bit index into π (since each byte appears many times, but finding the earliest occurrence requires a search, and the index itself must be stored). The joke exposes a fundamental insight from information theory: the expected length of the index needed to locate an arbitrary byte in a random sequence is at least as large as the byte itself, making the scheme not only lossless but expansive. The readme even cheekily suggests storing the metadata itself in π, leading to infinite recursion, an idea that commenters explored with glee.

The community response was a mix of amusement, pedagogical reflection, and incisive critique. One commenter noted that the metadata expansion means “if you set temperature to 0.0 you almost have a key-value store, but finding the right key for your value might take some effort.” Another pointed out the obvious flaw: “as the length of data increases it becomes extremely unlikely that the index and length of the sequence within π would actually be smaller than the data.” The discussion quickly extended to the philosophical implications: if π contains all possible sequences, then it also contains all falsehoods, and copyright law becomes absurd. Multiple users referenced the Library of Babel, the Sloot Digital Coding System, and even the one‑time pad to illustrate the same fundamental limit. Ultimately, πFS serves as a memorable teaching tool for the impossibility of universal compression without prior knowledge of the data distribution.

“Every file you've ever created, or anyone else has created or will create! Copyright infringement? It's just a few digits of π! They were always there!”

Key Highlights
  • πFS is a satirical filesystem that stores data by referencing positions in π, relying on the unproven conjecture that π is normal.
  • The scheme expands data: it requires 16 bits to store the index for each 8‑bit byte, making it worse than no compression.
  • The project humorously suggests storing metadata in π, leading to infinite recursion and highlighting the futility.
  • Comments connected πFS to the Library of Babel, one‑time pads, and the Sloot Digital Coding System, all of which face the same fundamental limitation.
  • The discussion underscored that universal lossless compression is impossible because the expected index length is at least as large as the data itself.

#4
PgDog is funded and coming to a database near you
⬆ 486 pts 💬 231 HN comments

PgDog, a proxy-based horizontal scaling solution for PostgreSQL, announced a $5.5M seed round led by Basis Set, YC, and others. The startup's core thesis is that Postgres's only real weakness is scaling—pointing to NoSQL databases like Mongo and Dynamo as workarounds for that gap. PgDog positions itself as a drop-in replacement for PgBouncer, offering connection pooling, load balancing, and sharding via a Rust-based proxy that sits between the application and Postgres instances. The authors, who previously scaled Postgres at Instacart, emphasize that the technology is already battle-tested: serving over 2M queries per second in production across dozens of deployments, sharding over 20TB of data, and accumulating 1.4M Docker pulls. The approach uses hash-based sharding (shard_number = hash(data) % num_shards) which, as commenters noted, can complicate rebalancing compared to solutions using virtual shards.

Despite the traction, the HN community raised nuanced points. A key debate centered on whether Postgres's primary pain point is scaling or high availability; one experienced user stated 'the #1 problem was always high availability, not scaling,' prompting PgDog's founder to detail HA support via load balancer health checks. Another thread criticized the simplistic sharding formula, arguing that linear range partitioning over virtual shards is more robust. Positive testimonials praised PgDog for solving longstanding PgBouncer issues—transparent handling of per-connection settings (e.g., statement_timeout), stable LISTEN/NOTIFY support, and seamless migrations. Questions about the open-source vs. enterprise model surfaced: the founder clarified that the control plane and QoS features are enterprise-only, while core sharding and pooling remain open. The discussion reveals a community eager for alternatives but wary of lock-in and architectural maturity.

Postgres is the only database you need. The reason DBs like Mongo or Dynamo exist is because Postgres has a scaling problem.

Key Highlights
  • PgDog is a Rust-based proxy providing connection pooling, load balancing, and sharding for PostgreSQL.
  • Raised $5.5M seed to build an enterprise edition with control plane and QoS.
  • Claimed production metrics: 2M+ QPS, 20TB+ sharded, 1.4M Docker pulls.
  • Sharding uses hash mod num_shards, criticized by commenters for lack of virtual shards.
  • Founder Lev Kokotko previously scaled Postgres at Instacart during COVID-19 surge.

GeoLibre 1.0
⬆ 257 pts 💬 22 HN comments

GeoLibre 1.0 is a lightweight, cloud-native GIS platform built with Tauri, React, TypeScript, MapLibre GL JS, DuckDB-WASM Spatial, and deck.gl. It provides a unified workspace across desktop and web environments, supporting vector and raster data from local files, remote services (XYZ, WMS, WFS, WMTS, ArcGIS, STAC), and cloud-native formats (GeoParquet, FlatGeobuf, PMTiles, Zarr, COG). The platform includes a SQL workspace powered by DuckDB Spatial, vector and raster processing tools (with optional Python sidecars), a plugin marketplace, and Jupyter integration via a Python package. This release marks a stable prototype with a comprehensive feature set aimed at bridging the gap between desktop GIS (like QGIS) and web-based viewers (like ArcGIS Online).

The Hacker News discussion reveals a mix of enthusiasm and skepticism. Commenters praise the convenience of browser-side GIS and the potential as an open-source alternative to ArcGIS Online. However, performance issues with large files (>1GB) were noted, with the web version throwing IO errors and the desktop app hanging or losing layers. Several commenters recognized the stack (MapLibre + DuckDB + PMTiles) as similar to their side projects, highlighting a trend toward cloud-native geospatial workflows. Some criticized the marketing language as AI-generated and questioned long-term maintainability. A desire for newer OGC APIs (vs. legacy WFS/WMS) was also expressed. The live demo at viewer.geolibre.app is widely recommended.

GeoLibre 1.0 processes geospatial data entirely client-side, enabling complex analysis like DuckDB Spatial SQL and Turf.js operations without server dependency.

How JPL keeps the 13-year-old Curiosity rover doing science
⬆ 244 pts 💬 71 HN comments

The 13-year-old Curiosity rover continues to perform groundbreaking science on Mars thanks to a series of ingenious software and hardware workarounds devised by JPL engineers. The rover, which landed in 2012, has traveled nearly 37 km and drilled 42 rock samples. A critical memory anomaly on Sol 2172 forced the team to swap between the rover's two computers, A and B, after one experienced a partition mount failure. They eventually repurposed 64 MB of NOR memory from outdated flight software banks to operate computer A—less than 1% of its original flash capacity. Other hacks include driving backwards to preserve worn wheels, parallelizing tasks to save power from the aging RTG (radioisotope thermoelectric generator), and switching to a Snapdragon processor in newer missions like Perseverance. The team's proactive software patching and system management have kept the rover productive despite diminishing power and component degradation.

Curiosity's longevity highlights the stark contrast in cost-effectiveness between robotic and human spaceflight. The total mission cost of under $3 billion is a fraction of the ~$90 billion spent on recent crewed lunar missions. Comments on Hacker News underscore this disparity, with users noting that NASA's mandated use of Boeing's SLS inflates costs. The article also reveals that Curiosity's RTG is projected to sustain science output through at least 2035, and its memory and wheels remain viable for years. The rover's success has informed the design of Perseverance, which features a dedicated visual odometry processor for autonomous driving. However, the article notes that budget constraints may ultimately limit Curiosity's lifespan more than technical obsolescence. The rover's ability to adapt—from running on salvaged memory to accepting over-the-air software updates—demonstrates a model for resilient deep-space exploration.

Curiosity now operates on just 64 MB of NOR memory—less than 1 percent of its original flash capacity—yet can still drive, conduct science, and manage data.

Key Highlights
  • Curiosity has traveled 37 km, drilled 42 rocks, and taken 763,000 photos in 13 years.
  • A memory anomaly forced a swap to computer A, which now runs on only 64 MB of NOR memory repurposed from flight software banks.
  • Wheel wear is mitigated by driving backwards; the arm's joint actuators are preserved by avoiding selfies.
  • The RTG power source is projected to support science through 2035, but power constraints drive efficiency improvements like parallel tasking.
  • Perseverance improves on Curiosity with a dedicated visual odometry processor and faster autonomous driving capabilities.
