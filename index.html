Beam: Reflection's 501B open-weight model
⬆ 495 pts 💬 160 HN comments

Reflection AI — the US lab founded by Misha Laskin (DeepMind/Gemini reward modeling) and Ioannis Antonoglou (AlphaGo co-creator) — has announced Beam, its first open-weight model: a sparse Mixture-of-Experts with 501B total and 23B active parameters, aimed squarely at coding, reasoning and agentic workloads. The pretraining stack is 23.8T curated tokens, with an architecture built for numerically healthy long-horizon optimization: interleaved local/global attention, fine-grained routed experts, auxiliary-loss-free load balancing augmented with cosine decay of expert-bias updates, sequence-level balancing, depth-based residual scaling, SandwichNorm, elementwise attention gating and FP32 residual accumulation. Reflection reports near-uniform expert utilization (busiest expert at 1.04x) and bounded residual RMS across all 52 layers. Notably, this is an announcement, not a release: weights, technical report and model card are promised 'later this month,' with early access behind a waitlist.

The differentiation claim is RL scale and inference efficiency rather than raw capability. Beam was trained with fully asynchronous policy gradients on 10.5K NVIDIA GB300 GPUs for four weeks, generating over 100M rollouts at up to 256K context across ~1M curated environments and ~1.3B sandbox executions; the team reports stable numerics even at one-day staleness — 107 weight versions behind the current policy — with inference-to-training GPU ratios drifting from 3.9:1 to 5.4:1, median weight propagation of ~12 seconds, 110K concurrent rollouts and 170K concurrent sandboxes. Reflection explicitly concedes Kimi K3 leads on raw capability while claiming 3–4x less inference compute than GLM-5.2 at comparable reasoning, positioning Beam as an enterprise 'workhorse.' HN's reaction mixed genuine interest in that infrastructure with demands to see the weights, complaints that the benchmark chart buries stronger competitors, and comparisons to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash.

#7
Dust: Pretraining Transformers Without Backpropagation
⬆ 239 pts 💬 62 HN comments

Dust, from Q Labs Research, is a zeroth-order optimization method that pretrains GPT-style transformers without any backward pass. Rather than perturbing weights as in classical evolution strategies, Dust adds Gaussian noise to activation outputs independently at every token, turning each token into a 'virtual population' member so a single forward pass evaluates thousands of candidates in parallel. Credit assignment is generic: each token's noise is rewarded by the local loss reduction — optionally propagated to future tokens through attention with decay gamma — and the reward-weighted noise, averaged over K draws, becomes the estimated output error, whose outer product with the layer input yields the weight gradient. Backprop forms the same outer product; only the source of the error signal differs. The method corrupts token loss directly only at the LM head, crediting attention internals through alignment with estimated attention-output gradients, and jitters each layer type in separate cached forward passes to suppress interference.

Empirically, Dust is the first zeroth-order method competitive with backprop on language-model pretraining — and at large populations it exceeds it in several settings. It is claimed to be 10^3-10^4x more efficient than a transformer implementation of EGGROLL from 1M tokens up. Counterintuitively, larger models are more population-efficient, not less: a 243M-parameter model beats a 120x smaller one at most population sizes, and gradient alignment with backprop improves as population grows, holding up to 1B tokens. Experiments use 8-layer, width-512 transformers on FineWeb with a 16k-token batch and SGD plus momentum. The authors concede Dust is not yet compute-competitive with backprop and frame it through Sutton's Bitter Lesson — a bet that search-based credit assignment wins in a compute-rich regime — while HN commenters debate whether that analogy holds.

A 243M-parameter Dust model outperforms a 120x smaller model at most population sizes, suggesting overparameterization may enlarge the search space's usable geometry rather than dilute it — the paper's most surprising scaling claim.

Key Highlights
  • Dust replaces backprop with token-independent activation perturbations, giving a 'virtual population' thousands strong per forward pass.
  • Reward-weighted noise produces weight gradients via the same outer product backprop uses, differing only in how the output error is estimated.
  • Reported 10^3-10^4x efficiency edge over weight-space ES (EGGROLL) beyond 1M tokens.
  • Larger models proved more population-efficient, contradicting conventional zeroth-order scaling wisdom.
  • Authors position it as Bitter Lesson research rather than a near-term backprop replacement, leaving compute-efficiency work to the future.

Opus 5.5 agents discover two room-temperature magnetic semiconductor candidates
⬆ 414 pts 💬 285 HN comments

Vals AI — an evals platform, not a materials lab — published a claim that a team of Claude Opus 5.5 agents used density functional theory (PBE+U and HSE06, plausibly via Quantum Espresso) to identify 'Luttinger-compensated' antiferromagnetic semiconductors: zero net spin moment, yet inequivalent spin-up/spin-down sublattices that still sort carriers by spin at the band edges. Two candidates emerged. YBaMnFeO5 is a novel five-element oxide predicted to have a 2.35 eV gap with 1.0 eV hole and 1.4 eV electron spin windows, ordering magnetically to ~420–490 K. KV[Cr(CN)6], a Prussian-blue analogue first synthesized in 1999, is predicted to have a 2.1 eV gap and spin windows of 2.6 eV and 1.6 eV. The physics is coherent, and the chosen figure of merit — spin-window width versus 26 meV of room-temperature thermal agitation — is exactly the right spintronics framing.

The caveats, however, dismantle the verb 'discover.' YBaMnFeO5 needs a perfect Mn/Fe checkerboard that Monte Carlo disorder simulation scrambles near 950 K, while oxide synthesis runs at 900–1300 °C — a scrambled crystal loses the spin sorting entirely. KV[Cr(CN)6] exists only as a 1999 powder with water in its pores and a 0.125 μB residual moment; PBE+U and HSE06 disagree on whether the hole window survives hydration (HSE06 says yes, PBE+U says it shrinks by more than half). Neither band gap nor spin sorting has ever been measured. Inputs, raw outputs, analysis code and a one-command checker are public on GitHub — more transparency than most 'AI discovered X' posts offer — but the HN headline's framing did much of the persuading.

"It had been hiding in plain sight": the stronger candidate, KV[Cr(CN)6], was synthesized in 1999 and its spin-split band edges were already plotted in a 2008 hybrid-functional study — nobody had named it a room-temperature Luttinger-compensated semiconductor or quantified its spin windows. The agents' contribution was scaled literature re-reading plus DFT, not a new measurement.

Key Highlights
  • Vals AI claims Claude Opus 5.5 agents designed one Luttinger-compensated AF semiconductor (YBaMnFeO5) and re-identified another (KV[Cr(CN)6], synthesized 1999) using DFT at PBE+U and HSE06 levels.
  • Luttinger compensation means zero net spin moment with inequivalent up/down sublattices, preserving spin-split band edges — the property ferro- and ordinary antiferromagnets respectively over- and under-deliver for spintronics.
  • Predicted spin windows (1.0–2.6 eV) dwarf the ~26 meV thermal scale, the core quantitative argument for room-temperature operation; magnetic order persists to 376 K in the 1999 KV[Cr(CN)6] sample.
  • Synthesis and validation are unaddressed: YBaMnFeO5's checkerboard disorders near 950 K, KV[Cr(CN)6] exists only as a hydrated powder with 0.125 μB residual moment, and PBE+U/HSE06 disagree on robustness.
  • Commenters note the work is essentially LLM-driven search over a DFT objective function, a workflow comparable to a senior undergraduate project that would normally get torn apart at a lab meeting rather than published as a discovery.

Example.com just launched the biggest redesign in decades
⬆ 279 pts 💬 187 HN comments

example.com is the internet's canonical placeholder: a reserved domain set aside by IANA under RFC 2606 in 1999, guaranteed never to be registered, and used in documentation, config samples, and textbooks precisely because it is inert. On 28 September 2026 it received its largest functional change in decades. The served HTML was minified down to a static English message, with a separate JavaScript bundle responsible for injecting five additional translations (Arabic, Chinese, French, Russian, Spanish) plus an SVG book icon. The initial implementation also animated the language rotation with a per-character opacity fade: every character wrapped in its own span, each given an incrementally larger CSS transition-delay so the glyphs shimmered in sequence. That lasted five days. Following feedback that the rotation was hostile to slow readers and screen readers, the animation was stripped and all six translations are now rendered simultaneously, with the client selecting a preferred language. IANA's stated motivation is bandwidth: most traffic to the domain is automated, will not execute JavaScript, and therefore never downloads the extra payload.

The redesign is one entry in a long, sparse changelog. The Wayback Machine's earliest capture is January 2002, an Apache 1.3.22 table-layout page listing reserved TLDs; RFC 2606 was cited in 2003; the site redirected to IANA from 2011 to 2013; EdgeCast CDN took over in 2013, then vanished into the Edgio bankruptcy and Akamai, and by December 2025 the response carried a CF-RAY header. June 2026 added an inline empty favicon, href=data:, to suppress the automatic /favicon.ico probe. IANA's statement is blunt about scope: 'There is no requirement there is a HTTP service present on the host in order to fulfill its purpose, we just operate it as a courtesy.' Hacker News responded with Hyrum's Law in its purest form: engineers who had quietly wired monitoring, proxy debugging, and language-model evaluations to the response body found their assertions red, and the thread became a referendum on whether a placeholder owes anyone stability.

IANA's own framing kills the entire use case: 'This is not a service, avoid relying on it for testing and monitoring purposes' — yet the redesign shipped a page that is 4.5x the size of the original (about 2,540 bytes) and injects its content via JavaScript, a strategy one commenter called out as implausible for a bandwidth-reduction goal given that each character was briefly wrapped in a span with its own transition-delay.

Key Highlights
  • example.com is a reserved domain governed by RFC 2606, never registrable, and explicitly not a service — IANA operates the page only as a courtesy to wayward humans.
  • The 28 September 2026 redesign replaced a static English page with minified HTML plus a JS bundle injecting six translations and an SVG icon, initially using per-character staggered opacity transitions.
  • Within five days the animation was removed in favor of showing all languages at once, after feedback about readability and accessibility.
  • IANA's stated rationale is reducing egress bandwidth, since automated clients typically do not fetch or execute the JavaScript payload.
  • The thread became a live demonstration of Hyrum's Law: undocumented, unintended dependencies on page contents broke, prompting one commenter to publish a self-hostable replica of the classic layout.

#5
Find the flattest route between any two points in SF
⬆ 251 pts 💬 87 HN comments

Flatten SF (flattensf.com) is a browser-side routing tool by Drew Edwards that computes Pareto-optimal walking and cycling routes across San Francisco, trading total distance against cumulative elevation gain. The technical foundation is notable for what it avoids: no server round-trips. Roughly 160,000 street segments from the Overture/OpenStreetMap graph, fused with USGS 3DEP 1 m lidar elevation, are loaded into the page, and the solver enumerates the set of routes nothing else beats on both distance and climbing — from the shortest path to the flattest route worth walking, where 'a foot of climb costs 200 ft of walking.' A single slider walks that frontier; sliding right never shortens the route and never adds climbing. Stairways count on foot but are excluded for bikes, and place search is fully offline, with SF intersections, places and addresses baked into the bundle.

The HN thread (251 points, 87 comments) quickly split along four axes. Prior-art pointers dominated: andalinmicphew plugged bikehopper.org, built on 1 m DTM elevation because 'DTM is a must for elevation based routing in SF as there are so many sizable buildings, large trees [that] the other models fail hard'; others cited BRouter, Valhalla, OpenTripPlanner's cost triangle, and cheeaun's 2014 Steepless. Objective-function critics argued cumulative gain is the wrong metric — jez noted the flattest route to Nob Hill goes 'straight up Taylor from Market' when Polk→California is far nicer, while laurencerowe hit a 22.7% grade on 29th St that saved 'maybe 1 ft of climbing.' Data-quality skeptics found a Cabrillo St failure traced by the developer to an OSM slow-street tag bug, and jeffbee recommended USGS 3DEP DTM at 25 cm. askjdfksdbfhk bluntly observed 'the Claude UI has made many inexplicable decisions here.'

The tool's entire frontier reduces to one scalar: 'a foot of climb costs 200 ft of walking' — beyond that, in Edwards' words, 'the routes stop being routes.'

Key Highlights
  • 160,000 street segments plus USGS 1 m lidar are solved entirely client-side, with offline geocoding for SF addresses and intersections.
  • The slider enumerates the Pareto frontier of distance vs. cumulative climbing, not grade — a distinction several commenters argue is the wrong objective.
  • Prior-art cluster points to bikehopper.org, BRouter, Valhalla and OpenTripPlanner's multi-cost triangle as more mature elevation-aware routers.
  • DTM vs. DSM elevation matters in dense cities: buildings and trees corrupt surface models, which is why bikehopper uses 1 m DTM and jeffbee suggests 25 cm USGS 3DEP.
  • A parser bug (OSM slow-street 'destination-only' tags read as pedestrian-closed) and 22.7%-grade routes show the routing graph and cost model still need tuning.

#6
Friendship ended with Deno, now Node is my best friend
⬆ 242 pts 💬 158 HN comments

David Bushell's post-mortem on migrating a SvelteKit client project and his static site generator off Deno and back onto Node is really an argument about runtime maturation. Running Node v26.10.0, he found surprisingly little work: Deno's filesystem API swapped for the now-modern node:fs, Deno.serve replaced by Hono's node adapter wrapping node:http, and @std/path replaced by node:path as a straight import swap. The surprising result was a 15% faster build. Around the runtime he rebuilt the toolchain: fnm instead of the curl-pipe-bash NVM installer, pnpm aliased as npm/npx to dodge post-install script execution, and pnpm-workspace.yaml knobs — minimumReleaseAge: 1440 and trustPolicy: no-downgrade — as a crude supply-chain delay valve (he wanted a month, settled for a day to keep version resolution viable). Publishing TypeScript still fails under node_modules with ERR_UNSUPPORTED_NODE_MODULES_TYPE_STRIPPING, forcing tsdown back into the build.

The Deno half is a corporate post-mortem, not a technical one: layoffs, no roadmap, JSR returning aggressive 429s, weeks of broken ZSH integration, and bugs that made the runtime choke on concurrent HTTP requests — 'borderline unusable' on top of his other criticisms. He dismisses Deno Land Inc. as a 'boring start-up' shipping 'vibe-coding Temu Cloudflare,' a jab commenters contested in defense of cell-d. Node's win is accretion, not revolution: ECMAScript sugar landed, require() is optional, node:test exists, and type stripping works everywhere except inside node_modules — a restriction Node documents as deliberate, which the author reads as philosophical anti-Microsoft gatekeeping but which commenters correctly attribute to unresolvable tsconfig mismatches in third-party code. The practical fallout: dropped provenance after moving to self-hosted Forgejo, and comments on what runtime consolidation means when LLMs are the new default-setting cohort.

After swapping Deno's APIs for node:fs, Hono's node adapter, and node:path, Bushell measured 15% faster static-site builds — while still writing idiomatic Deno, meaning he's likely leaving more performance on the table.

Key Highlights
  • Node v26.10.0 absorbs enough ECMAScript and API modernization that a Deno-native codebase migrated with only fs/serve/path substitutions and got 15% faster builds.
  • Node refuses type-stripping inside node_modules (ERR_UNSUPPORTED_NODE_MODULES_TYPE_STRIPPING) — the author calls it philosophical anti-Microsoft policy, but commenters note it's about tsconfig mismatch, not vendor politics.
  • Supply-chain hygiene drives tooling choices: pnpm aliases, minimumReleaseAge: 1440, trustPolicy: no-downgrade, and blocked post-install scripts as defenses against npm malware.
  • Deno's decline is organizational, not technical — layoffs, JSR 429 throttling, broken ZSH integration, and concurrency bugs eroded a formerly loyal user base.
  • Runtime choice is increasingly shaped by LLM defaults: agents reach for Node, not Deno, which commenters see as a structural blow to Deno's adoption.

Benchmark in Milliseconds
⬆ 17 pts 💬 0 HN comments

Matklad's blog post 'Benchmark in Milliseconds' proposes a deceptively simple heuristic for microbenchmark design: adjust input size until the benchmark runs for approximately 300 milliseconds. The rationale is multifaceted. First, milliseconds as integers from 1 to 999 offer sufficient precision to detect small improvements while remaining easy to scan visually, avoiding floating-point units or unit conversions. Second, durations below 10ms risk being skewed by fixed costs like interpreter startup; hundreds of milliseconds amortize such overheads without requiring complex correction techniques. Third, 300ms is an eternity for a computer but perceptible to humans, allowing the developer to leverage their intuitive sense of time and speed rather than relying solely on numeracy. Finally, it's fast enough to run repeatedly for variance checks, facilitating rapid iteration. The underlying assumption is that benchmarking's purpose is not precise measurement but providing the author with enough intuition to make correct optimization decisions.

The post challenges the conventional wisdom that benchmarks must be statistically rigorous, as advocated by frameworks like JMH or Criterion. Matklad argues that for the purpose of guiding optimization, a 300ms target hits a sweet spot: it avoids the noise of ultra-short runs while remaining far quicker than the multi-second or minute-long runs often required for statistical significance. He notes that 'anything longer than a second makes iterating on the benchmark slower than it needs to be,' and that running a benchmark ten times should be fast. The emphasis on human perception—'Pushing numbers into human-perceptible range allows me to use my intuitive sense of time and speed'—reflects a pragmatic, developer-centric view of performance work. This approach trades off formal rigor for ergonomics, but matklad contends that the goal is decision-making, not publication-grade measurement, making the trade-off worthwhile.

The purpose of benchmarking isn’t so much a precise measurement of performance, but rather providing the author with enough intuition to make a correct decision.

  • Matklad's rule of thumb: adjust input size so microbenchmark runs ~300ms.
  • Rationale: milliseconds as integers 1-999 give precision without floats, avoid fixed costs, human-perceptible, fast iteration.
