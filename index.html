Launch HN: Magnitude (YC S25) – Self-optimizing inference engine for agents
⬆ 179 pts 💬 88 HN comments

Magnitude (YC S25) is an Apache-2.0 desktop inference engine for local agents that takes an unusual stance: rather than shipping kernels precompiled for broad hardware classes like llama.cpp, Ollama, or LM Studio, it compiles and tunes its kernels on the user's actual device before a model runs. The pitch is up to 2x faster than llama.cpp — 92% faster decode on Metal, 19% on CUDA — plus 27% less memory per agent and a prefix-tree cache shared across concurrent sessions to avoid the slowdown that hits agents holding multiple 128k contexts. It runs on Apple Silicon, NVIDIA, AMD (via Vulkan), or CPU-only, ships as a Mac/Windows/Linux app with a bundled CLI, and one-click connects Pi, OpenCode, Hermes, Codex, Claude Code, and Cline through an OpenAI-compatible endpoint. The strategic bet is narrow: hand-optimized kernels for a curated set of popular open-weight families, where same-family architectures reuse the same kernels.

The thread's real energy, though, is adversarial benchmarking. kmike84 calls beating llama.cpp “a low bar,” noting Mac fast-paths like ds4, omlx, and mtplx; sebastienburel insists MLX, not llama.cpp, is the correct Apple Silicon baseline. bythreads ran it on an M5 Max across six Qwen models and concluded it “adds next to nothing,” while mrtsepelev measured decode gains (82.8 vs 76.5 tok/s) but prefill ~2.6x slower than oMLX. Founder anerli engages nearly every thread, conceding real gaps: no multi-GPU support, a known M5 Metal 4 matmul shortfall, and that UI speed estimates “don't account for gains from speculative decoding.” The recurring counter-argument is that agents bottleneck on KV cache and prefix reuse, not single-stream tok/s.

“Beating llama.cpp on speed is a low bar” — the sharpest community retort, given llama.cpp is treated as a portable baseline rather than the fast path on any specific accelerator.

Key Highlights
  • Self-optimizing engine compiles and tunes kernels on-device, claiming up to 2x faster than llama.cpp (92% Metal decode, 19% CUDA).
  • Critics argue MLX on Apple Silicon and ds4/omlx/mtplx are stronger baselines than llama.cpp, undercutting the headline speedup.
  • Independent M5 Max testing found near-parity; one user saw faster decode but ~2.6x slower prefill versus oMLX.
  • Agent realism critique: KV-cache footprint and prefix-cache reuse across turns and concurrent sessions matter more than raw tok/s.
  • Founder engages transparently — acknowledging no multi-GPU support, an M5 Metal 4 kernel gap, and speculative-decoding-blind estimates.

The last time my family was replaced by technology
⬆ 294 pts 💬 577 HN comments

Manuel Darcemont's short personal essay (294 points, 577 comments) deliberately refuses the usual AI-displacement thinkpiece framing, instead grounding the argument in family genealogy: his great-great-grandfather was a farrier in Mandres-en-Barrois, a village in rural France, shoeing horses and repairing farmers' carts until the automobile arrived; he retrained as a mechanic, and every male descendant since kept the trade. The author's claim is deliberately narrow — not that history guarantees smooth transitions, but that the *purpose* (helping people get around) survived the abrupt change in *technology*. His aphorism — "Be willing to let go of the how to hold on to the why" — rests on the observation that most developers chose the profession for making things and watching people use them, and only then learned to code, so the desire preceded the medium.

The comment thread dismantles that analogy with unusual rigor. The single most-upvoted rebuttal inverts the metaphor: "You are not the farrier in this analogy. You are the horse." Several commenters note the farrier's tools remained corporeal and adjacent — hands still worked on metal — while software engineering is displaced by a general-purpose reasoning machine, not a coding machine (zeven7). Others attack the survivorship bias: the author admits he would never have heard of farriers who failed, and phtrivier points out that many of them "were sent to the front and died by the 1000s." The thread also leans on labor economics — CGP Grey's line that "there isn't a rule of economics that says better technology makes more, better jobs for horses," and the 70%-agriculture baseline — plus concrete 2025 hiring anecdotes.

"You are not the farrier in this analogy. You are the horse." — the thread's top rebuttal, which reframes the post's central metaphor from retraining optimism into displacement.

Key Highlights
  • The author's great-great-grandfather moved from farrier to mechanic when cars arrived, keeping the same purpose — mobility — while swapping the underlying technology.
  • The essay explicitly disclaims prescriptiveness; Darcemont says it is a tribute, not advice to "just shut up and adapt."
  • Top rebuttal inverts the metaphor: developers are not the farrier but the horse, since whoever can retrain (the farrier/owner) is distinct from the capacity being automated.
  • Commenters dismantle the farrier analogy on two axes: farriers physically persist (Ensorceled, railroad blacksmithing), and the failed majority is invisible in family history.

OpenDLSS: A Vulkan Reimplementation of Nvidia's DLSS 5 Neural Rendering Network
⬆ 184 pts 💬 94 HN comments

OpenDLSS-NR is a clean-room Vulkan reimplementation of NVIDIA’s DLSS 5 Neural Rendering network, claiming bit-exact parity with build 310.8.0. It reconstructs the 71-block Swin/ViT U-net—six pooling levels, FP8 E4M3 activations, FP16 accumulation, 141 MiB weights—and uses VK_KHR_cooperative_matrix, VK_EXT_shader_float8, and generated PTX mma.sync kernels with cp.async rings and counter-based chaining. The repo includes a GLSL reference path, a WebGPU browser port, and a Filament demo patched for motion vectors. It is not an upscaler; it takes an LDR proxy, noise lanes, reprojected history, and conditioning scalars to produce an RGB residual plus temporal-blend logit. Weights are not shipped; users supply the model directory. This is reverse-engineering of a generative neural renderer, not a mere API wrapper.

Performance on an RTX 4070 SUPER: 2.8 ms at 768², 7.8 ms at 1080p, 12.6 ms at 1440p, 29.3 ms at 4K, with 241 dispatches per frame. The WebGPU port hits 72 ms at 512² with no tensor cores and no FP8, demonstrating that exactness is specified, not hardware-bound. HN commenters split sharply: TheJCDenton calls bit-exactness “sorcery”; MYEUHD cites original DLSS costs—9.9 ms at 1080p on RTX 5060, 13.7 ms at 4K on RTX 5080. Skeptics note missing weights, an LLM-generated README (“factually wrong”), and a repository duplicated by aloshdenny/open-dlss. Debate also covers NVIDIA’s open-source posture, Zluda, and whether depth/Z-buffer should be an input. The project is MIT, unaffiliated, and avoids shipping NVIDIA IP. Impact: it pressure-tests the black box of DLSS 5 and may accelerate AMD/Linux alternatives.

All 75 block boundaries match byte for byte—not just the final image—and the browser WebGPU port reproduces the same bytes with no tensor cores and no FP8, at 72 ms vs 2.7 ms native.

Key Highlights
  • Bit-exact Vulkan reimplementation of NVIDIA DLSS 5 Neural Rendering network, matching build 310.8.0 across 71 Swin/ViT blocks and 75 boundaries.
  • Runs FP8 E4M3 tensor-core GEMMs with FP16 accumulation, plus PTX kernels for mma.sync, cp.async rings, split-K, and counter-based chaining.
  • No weights included: users must supply a model directory; repo has no NVIDIA affiliation and ships under MIT license.
  • Performance ranges from 2.8 ms at 768² to 29.3 ms at 4K on RTX 4070 SUPER; WebGPU port is 72 ms at 512² without tensor cores.
  • HN reaction mixes technical awe with skepticism over LLM-authored docs, repo provenance duplication, missing weights, and practical cost.

A brief history of the Bloomberg terminal
⬆ 339 pts 💬 143 HN comments

Bloomberg Terminal's origin story is a case study in vertical integration around market data. IEEE Spectrum traces it to 1981, when Michael Bloomberg, fired from Salomon Brothers, founded Innovative Market Systems with Thomas Secunda, Duncan MacMillan, and Charles Zegar. Their Market Master terminal combined a monochrome CRT, custom keyboard, communications controller, and private network into one interface for real-time prices, historical data, and instant bond analytics. Merrill Lynch invested $30 million for a 30 percent stake and five-year exclusivity, taking the first 22 terminals in 1982. That timing exploited electronic trading and floating U.S. interest rates, which made bond valuation both volatile and computationally urgent. Unlike Reuters or Dow Jones news subscriptions, Bloomberg sold an integrated calculation environment. IMS rebranded as Bloomberg LP, and Market Master became the Bloomberg Terminal.

The hardware and business details explain its staying power. The hand-assembled 'Chiclet' keyboard used color-coded, finance-specific function keys and hotkeys; later versions added a trackball (1990), speaker and telephony jacks (1992), Instant Bloomberg chat, support for 23 languages by 1996, and fingerprint biometric login in the early 2000s. A 1999 subscription cost $1,600 per month with a two-year minimum; today it is upwards of $32,000 per year. Bloomberg launched Open Bloomberg software in 1995 and stopped leasing dedicated terminals in 2000, yet preserved a closed-network feel. After Dow Jones threatened news access in 1990, Bloomberg built its own newsroom under Matthew Winkler. The Smithsonian now holds Bloomberg keyboards, including Bill Gross's with his password taped on front. HN commenters debate dense expert UIs, Chromium/C++/JavaScript internals, an OCaml derivatives DSL, and Bloomberg's bond-data monopoly.

A single Bloomberg Terminal subscription cost $1,600 per month in 1999 and now runs upwards of $32,000 per year—yet the first 22 Market Master terminals shipped to Merrill Lynch in 1982.

Key Highlights

  • The terminal integrated real-time data, historical analytics, communications, and trading via a proprietary network, custom keyboard, and centralized compute.
  • Keyboard evolution included the hand-assembled Chiclet hotkeys, a 1990 trackball, 1992 speaker/telephony jacks, Instant Bloomberg chat, 23 languages by 1996, and early-2000s fingerprint biometrics.
  • Pricing shifted from $1,600/month in 1999 to over $32,000/year today; Open Bloomberg launched in 1995 and dedicated terminal leasing ended in 2000.

The top secret URSALA, RAQUEL, and FARRAH satellites (2025)
⬆ 267 pts 💬 132 HN comments

The declassification of documents revealing the URSALA, RAQUEL, and FARRAH satellites sheds light on a secret NRO program that launched small ELINT 'hitchhikers' alongside larger HEXAGON photo-reconnaissance satellites from the 1960s to the 1980s. These suitcase-sized spacecraft spun rapidly in low Earth orbit, using parabolic dishes to intercept faint radar side lobes, and evolved from bespoke, short-lived designs into standardized systems under Program 989. The shift marked a pivotal move from national-level intelligence to tactical support, culminating in TENCAP, which enabled direct downlink of electronic intelligence to mobile vans near the Forward Edge of the Battle Area, empowering Army, Navy, and Air Force commanders with real-time threat data.

Technical specifics underscore Cold War ingenuity: URSALA conducted general search in the 2–12 GHz band to counter double-agile radars, while RAQUEL performed directed search in the 4–18 GHz range. URSALA I, designed for nine months, operated for 70 months; RAQUEL 1 lasted 63 months. RAQUEL 1A, costing $13.6 million in 1976, was reoriented during the 1982 Falklands War to monitor Argentine forces, potentially aiding in the sinking of the ARA General Belgrano. The satellites packed electronics to the density of 'solid mahogany' and introduced encrypted downlinks. Their legacy includes the NRO's gift of Hubble-class spy telescopes to NASA, highlighting a persistent technological gap between military and civilian space programs.

RAQUEL 1A, a $13.6 million satellite, was reoriented during the 1982 Falklands War to intercept Argentine radar and communications, potentially aiding in the sinking of the ARA General Belgrano.

Key Highlights
  • URSALA, RAQUEL, and FARRAH were small ELINT satellites launched as hitchhikers on HEXAGON photo-recon satellites, collecting radar signals for tactical intelligence.
  • URSALA provided general search in 2-12 GHz; RAQUEL did directed search in 4-18 GHz, with RAQUEL 1A costing $13.6M (1976) and used during the Falklands War.
  • The program evolved from bespoke, short-lived satellites to standardized designs, leading to TENCAP and direct downlink to mobile ground stations near the FEBA.
  • URSALA I exceeded its 9-month design life by lasting 70 months; RAQUEL 1 lasted 63 months, showcasing remarkable reliability.
  • Declassification in 2025 revealed details of previously secret space shuttle payloads and the satellites' role in supporting tactical forces, including encrypted downlinks.

⬆ 262 pts 💬 222 HN comments

The article 'LinkedIn Larpmaxxing' is a polemic against performative productivity, but its technical hook is a from-scratch YOLOv8 detector trained to flag LinkedIn posts announcing yet another YOLO project. The author collects 200 feed screenshots with a shell loop using scrot and xdotool Page_Down, annotates them in Roboflow, then fine-tunes YOLOv8n for 50 epochs at imgsz=320 on a 'potato PC.' Inference runs at confidence 0.10 and saves boxes. The build takes roughly 90 minutes, mostly manual annotation and training wait. Technically, the project is minimal: no custom architecture, no data augmentation pipeline, no evaluation beyond loss curves; it is a pretrained nano model applied to a culturally specific screenshot domain. That low effort is the point: the same hand-tracking and pothole-detection demos flooding LinkedIn are often tutorial derivatives with no deployment, no optimization, and no real utility, wrapped in LLM-polished corpo speak. The detector is recursive: it automates criticism of the exact 'excited to announce' genre it belongs to.

The systemic argument is that LinkedIn rewards appearing interesting, not getting better. The author writes, 'It's just LARPing productivity,' and notes that critical comments are suppressed because they show up on your profile. HN commenters extend this into platform economics: off-platform links are demoted, login walls block Google results, JS obfuscation stops agents, and ads/Premium depend on engagement. Yet LinkedIn is also mandatory job infrastructure for many, so quitting is not simple. Some commenters note the author's own HN post is the same kind of self-promotion, proving nowhere is immune.

The author built a YOLOv8 slop detector in about 90 minutes, with most time spent annotating 200 screenshots and waiting for 50 epochs—undercutting LinkedIn's entire 'AI project' performance economy.

Before pixels: Modular industrial dashboards
⬆ 241 pts 💬 43 HN comments

Marcin Wichary's Unsung essay collects photographs of modular industrial control panels from German, Polish, and railway museums — Deutsches Museum München, Fernmeldemuseum Stuttgart, DASA Dortmund, Nuremberg, Warsaw — arguing they constitute a coherent pre-pixel design system. The panels share a grammar: standardized swappable tiles (lamps, counters, toggle switches, annunciators, ammeters, plate-covered blanks) mounted in an identical grid, with state encoded as illuminated bulbs rather than rendered glyphs. Wichary frames these as both a design system and an interaction system, noting that the same physical vocabulary reappears across air traffic control, light rail, trams, and power-plant control rooms — a literal component library where the grid is the layout engine and the backplane is the API.

The piece is deliberately light on engineering claims, and the commentariat fills that gap. The consensus is that these panels were mechanically modular, not electrically pluggable: as one commenter puts it, "They're mechanically modular. They don't automatically get data somehow... you still have to wire each individual component," with the actual logic — "mechanical relays, timers, potentiometers... built by electricians" — living in a separate cabinet behind the fascia. Commenters decode specifics: the "Rotte" label signals a track-work crew, SpDr signal boxes operate on a "from where to were" press-two-buttons protocol, and the celebrated day/night switch is contested between panel backlighting and a 30 A/700 W–6.6 kW load.

What the commenters repeatedly reach for is affect. The thread's dominant emotional register is elegiac: a nostalgia for interfaces whose failure modes were visible, whose affordances were literal, and whose components could be replaced in a museum by unscrewing a plate.

#8
What TLA+ can and can't check
⬆ 217 pts 💬 47 HN comments

Hillel Wayne's essay is a deliberate cooling agent applied to the recent fever around formal methods, triggered by Boris Cherny's claim that Opus used TLA+ to surface race conditions in code. Rather than rehearse the familiar complaint that correct specifications don't imply correct implementations, Wayne attacks a deeper limitation: to verify a property you must first be able to *express* it. He walks through TLA+'s expressive core — invariants like `[](at_most_one_green)`, action properties such as `[](x' >= x)` and `[](P => P')`, and the liveness family built from `<>`, including `[]<>P` for recovery, `<>[]P` for termination-with-correct-result, and the sugared `P ~> Q` for causality. All of these are safety or liveness properties, and all are implicitly universally quantified over every behavior of the system.

The trouble starts with that universal quantifier. TLA+ cannot natively express existential reachability ('this game is winnable'), multi-step properties ('delete then undo restores state'), real time or floating point, hyperproperties over *sets* of behaviors (security noninterference, 'energy-saving mode uses less power', any 95th-percentile latency claim), or state-space metaproperties like path uniqueness. Wayne concedes workarounds exist: auxiliary variables, self-composition, and TLC's newer `REACHABLE` keyword and `TLCGet` — but calls them hacks that wreck refinement, exponentiate the state space, and make specs stop resembling the systems they model. Better answers may come from differently-shaped tools: CTL, PRISM, or GenMC. The piece landed at 217 points and 47 comments.

To verify a property, you first need a property to verify — and TLA+ cannot even express that a game is winnable, that an energy-saving mode uses less power, or that a 95th-percentile response time is 5ms.

Key Highlights
  • TLA+ checks invariants, action properties, and liveness formulas, all universally quantified over behaviors
  • Existential reachability, multi-step properties, real-time/floating-point semantics, and hyperproperties are inexpressible natively
  • Workarounds (auxiliary variables, self-composition, REACHABLE, TLCGet) are hacks that break refinement and explode state spaces
  • Boris Cherny's claim about Opus finding race conditions with TLA+ drove an outsized 'formal methods will save agentic coding' narrative
  • Commenters push Quint as a friendlier front-end, Ada/SPARK and Dafny/Lean as spec-to-code bridges, and GenMC/RustMC for weak-memory modeling

5x faster Edge Functions: V8 isolates to Firecracker MicroVMs
⬆ 204 pts 💬 87 HN comments

Netlify has moved its Edge Functions off a hosted, external V8-isolate execution service and onto Firecracker MicroVMs running inside its own edge network, cutting warm invocation latency from 25-40ms to 5-6ms at p50 — roughly 5x at the median, with p99 invocations 47.4% faster. At roughly a billion invocations per day, the path is: TLS termination and route matching at the nearest edge node, which writes a machine spec naming three images (runtime, platform, edge function) plus CPU, memory and connection limits. That spec's hash and site data become a service ID, so deploys with different code or env vars never share a MicroVM. Rendezvous hashing pins each service to a compute node for cache warmth, relaxing stickiness above a traffic threshold to prevent hotspots. MicroVMs boot from a memory-mapped snapshot, with bundles mounted as uncompressed EROFS images read lazily. Unikraft supplies the VM lifecycle: boot, snapshot, restore and scale-to-zero.

The published numbers are specific: VMs are created in under a millisecond and start in about 2ms at p99; cold invocations — where a region has never seen the images — occur on ~1.2% of requests and average ~9ms; availability sits at 99.998%, with log delivery 5x faster and local DNS resolvers inside compute nodes. Netlify frames this as unlocking npm packages out of beta, revisiting isolate-era limits of 50ms CPU, 512MB memory and 20MB compressed code, and building anything that depends on controlling the network path. The HN thread, however, pushed hard on attribution: several commenters note the old isolates ran off-network, so much of the win is eliminating a network hop rather than a microVM outrunning V8. Others welcomed the stronger isolation and pointed at Firecracker's AWS lineage and Unikraft's rising profile.

p50 warm invocations dropped from 25-40ms to 5-6ms, but the crowd's sharpest read is that the speedup came largely from moving execution in-network — not from MicroVMs beating V8 isolates on raw start time.

Commenters broadly welcome the security model and Firecracker's momentum while heavily debating whether the '5x faster' headline is an architectural apples-to-oranges comparison.

Warm p50 latency fell from 25-40ms to 5-6ms; p99 invocations improved 47.4% with 99.998% availability.

Cold invocations hit only ~1.2% of requests at ~9ms average, with MicroVMs created in under 1ms and booting in ~2ms p99 from a memory-mapped snapshot.

A machine spec naming runtime, platform and function images is hashed into a service ID, guaranteeing that deploys with differing code or env vars never share a MicroVM — isolation V8 isolates cannot provide.

Rendezvous hashing keeps services pinned to compute nodes for cache warmth, relaxed above a traffic threshold to stop one hot function from saturating a node.

#12
Halfspace experimental IDE for solid modeling with distance fields
⬆ 166 pts 💬 9 HN comments

Halfspace is Matt Keeter's experimental IDE for solid modeling with signed distance fields — a GUI wrapped around the Fidget implicit kernel, which has been in development since 2022 and which Keeter has used to drive kernel improvements in a non-trivial application. The project's framing is unusually architectural: Keeter argues that authoring implicit surfaces directly is "a bit like writing assembly: it's low-level, powerful, and annoying," and so Halfspace takes both escape routes at once — a small but growing standard library of primitives and transforms, plus first-class support for incremental composition, where a complex model is decomposed into parameterized, individually visualizable pieces. The choice of implicit kernels over boundary-representation geometry is deliberate: B-rep stitching is formidably hard (two NURBS surfaces may intersect with no closed-form solution), whereas an implicit kernel can plausibly be written and fully understood by one person, which suits personal-scale fabrication and 3D printing.

The distinguishing design decision is putting distance fields in the foreground rather than treating them as CSG plumbing. Keeter's sawtooth example shows two fields with identical signs everywhere but different magnitudes: the poorly behaved one has a C0 jump discontinuity where the boundary transitions from inside to outside without crossing zero, so Fidget's automatic-differentiation normals point the wrong way, yielding visibly incorrect shading on a cabin's shingles. Normalizing the gradient to magnitude 1 improves downstream mesh quality. The stack is aggressively cross-platform and honestly enumerated: Rust throughout, manual wasm-bindgen, egui/egui_dock, wgpu for both UI and GPU compute, Rhai for scripting, and Rayon plus wasm-bindgen-rayon doubling as a background task pool. Notably, fidget-wgpu moved rasterization and shading entirely onto the GPU, since CPU rasterization through WebAssembly ran at "non-interactive speeds." Keeter notes plainly that, since it is 2026, this is "not vibe-coded." Halfspace is MPLv2, experimental, and explicitly not for load-bearing use.

"Working with low-level implicit surfaces is a bit like writing assembly: it's low-level, powerful, and annoying" — and the payoff for foregrounding the distance field is that a C0 jump discontinuity, invisible in a sign-only CSG view, becomes immediately diagnosable as broken autodiff normals.

Key Highlights
  • Halfspace is a GUI/IDE built on the Fidget implicit-surface kernel, foregrounding distance fields rather than hiding them behind CSG operations.
  • Two fields with identical signs can differ catastrophically: a C0 jump discontinuity breaks autodiff-derived normals and produces visibly wrong shading.
  • The stack is Rust + wasm-bindgen + egui + wgpu + Rhai + Rayon, with wgpu used for both UI rendering and GPU compute.
  • fidget-wgpu moved rasterization and post-processing fully to the GPU, restoring native rendering speed on the web build.
  • Keeter explicitly disclaims vibe-coding and warns Halfspace should not be used in any load-bearing capacity; it is open-source under MPLv2.

CHOMPI portable sampler instrument is now open-source (hardware and software)
⬆ 99 pts 💬 26 HN comments

The CHOMPI Mk1, a boutique portable sampler built on Electrosmith's Daisy embedded audio platform (an STM32H750 Cortex-M7-class SoC running the libDaisy/DaisySP C++ toolchain), has been discontinued by Chase Bliss and released as a full open-source package. The drop is unusually complete for consumer hardware: firmware sources for TAPE 2.0 (the shipping sampler), TEMPO 1.0 (a pattern generator), WAVE 1.0 (an 8-voice wavetable synth written as a starting point for custom firmware), and a v6.4-beta bootloader that never shipped on production units; the factory microSD card profiles; and a hardware repository containing schematics, BOM, EAGLE PCB files and a full fabrication package, plus six-panel enclosure files and laser-cut paths for DIY panels. The carve-outs are deliberate: all panel artwork, logos and the graphic set were stripped for copyright reasons, and anyone building their own unit is asked to rename it to avoid trademark infringement.

The framing is explicitly a terminal release, not a maintained project: "a permanent source for the files and documentation," with no further updates, no customer support, and a hard warning that this is for "developers and code nerds" comfortable with Daisy and C++, not for beginners. The move makes CHOMPI a useful template for open-sourcing a discontinued product without surrendering IP — publish the fabrication data, withhold the brand. Community life migrates to a dedicated open-source channel on the Chase Bliss Discord, while existing owners retain normal repair and support. The HN thread drew 99 points and 26 comments that split less over the code than over the price, and one commenter, johnwheeler, has already ported the instrument's behavior outward to a VST/Audio Unit/AAX plugin called Nibbi — evidence that open firmware extends a dead embedded target's lifespan by moving its ideas to host DSP.

"All of the original source files for hardware and software are available to use, including blank enclosure panels. All CHOMPI artwork has been excluded." — a clean precedent for open-sourcing a discontinued device while retaining trademark and copyright.

Los Alamos bets on ENIAC: Nuclear Monte Carlo simulations, 1947–1948 (2014) [pdf]
⬆ 36 pts 💬 0 HN comments

In 1947, Los Alamos faced a computational crisis: thermonuclear calculations required solving neutron diffusion equations that were analytically intractable. Stanislaw Ulam and John von Neumann proposed the Monte Carlo method—using random sampling to approximate solutions—but it demanded millions of random numbers. The only machine capable of such throughput was ENIAC, the Army's electronic computer at the Moore School. This paper by Haigh, Priestley, and Rope details the high-stakes bet: Los Alamos contracted ENIAC for a series of simulations, marking the first large-scale use of Monte Carlo in physics. The technical hurdles were immense: programming ENIAC via plugboards, generating pseudo-random sequences, and validating results against known cases. The collaboration epitomized postwar big science, merging military funding, academic research, and cutting-edge engineering.

The ENIAC runs in 1948, code-named 'the H-bomb calculations,' consumed over a million random numbers and produced critical insights into thermonuclear burn. The machine's 5,000 additions per second enabled computations that would have taken humans years. The project's success validated Monte Carlo as a universal tool, later fueling fields from statistical physics to AI's Monte Carlo tree search. It also cemented the role of computers in national security, leading to the hydrogen bomb. The paper reveals the human story: the ENIAC programmers—six women—translated mathematical formulations into physical wiring, a feat often overlooked. Ultimately, Los Alamos' gamble on ENIAC not only advanced nuclear weaponry but birthed computational science, establishing a paradigm where simulation rivals theory and experiment.

The ENIAC's 5,000 additions per second enabled the first Monte Carlo simulations in 1948, consuming over 1 million random numbers to model thermonuclear reactions—a computational bet that birthed modern simulation science.
