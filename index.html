#6
Who owns the code Claude Code wrote?
⬆ 409 pts 💬 382 HN comments

The legal landscape for AI-generated code ownership remains deeply unsettled. The US Copyright Office ruled in January 2025 that works predominantly generated by AI without meaningful human authorship are not copyrightable, and the Supreme Court declined to hear the Thaler appeal in March 2026. However, denial of certiorari does not constitute endorsement of the lower court's reasoning, and the ruling is not binding nationwide. The core legal question—whether AI-generated code can be copyrighted by the human prompter—remains open. The HN discussion highlights the tension: some argue that directing an AI is akin to using a compiler, while others note that compilers are deterministic and LLMs are not. The Zarya of the Dawn case already set a precedent for images: human-written elements were protected, AI-generated ones were not. Code may follow a similar path, but the line between human and machine contribution is blurry.

The practical implications are vast. Commenters point out that commit messages can serve as evidence of human authorship—'Restructured Claude’s module architecture, rejected initial state management approach' is defensible, while 'Add rate limiting module' is not. The risk of copyright laundering from GPL training data is real, as seen in the chardet dispute. One commenter shared an anecdote: 'I asked it to provide the same API as lm, glm and some other base R functions. It copied their code wholesale without mentioning it to me. Now my library is GPL.' Corporate behavior reveals short-sightedness: EMs pushing engineers to use Claude Code at maximum speed, sacrificing code understanding and review practices. The M&A angle may force clarity—term sheets with IP reps that can't be backed up will surface the problem. Ultimately, the question may be resolved not by courts but by the market, as companies weigh liability against productivity gains.

Denial of certiorari can happen for many reasons unrelated to the merits and does not settle the issue nationwide.

Key Highlights
  • US Copyright Office and courts preliminarily hold AI-generated content lacks copyright without meaningful human authorship.
  • Supreme Court's denial of cert in Thaler v. Perlmutter does not settle the issue nationwide.
  • Compiler analogy is flawed: LLMs are non-deterministic, unlike deterministic compilers.
  • Human modification and evidence (e.g., commit messages) may establish copyright, but threshold is unclear.
  • Concerns about copyright laundering from GPL training data and the impact on open source licensing.

#12
I won a championship that doesn't exist
⬆ 174 pts 💬 87 HN comments

The article describes a two-step experiment in poisoning the retrieval layer of LLMs. The author registered a domain (6nimmt.com) for $12, created a fake press release claiming he won a nonexistent world championship for the card game 6 Nimmt!, and added a corresponding paragraph to Wikipedia with a single citation back to his own site. This circular citation pattern — Wikipedia quoting a site that itself has no independent corroboration — exploits the trust LLMs place in Wikipedia's citation structure. When queried, frontier models confidently repeated the fabricated fact because the retrieval layer showed two agreeing sources, when in reality both originated from the same $12 asset. The attack succeeded because the query space was narrow and the model had no prior knowledge, allowing a single well-placed edit to dominate the result set.

The experiment exposes three stacked failure modes: immediate retrieval-layer poisoning, eventual training corpus contamination if the edit survives long enough to be scraped into model weights, and agent-layer exploitation where tools act on poisoned sources. The author notes that recent Wikipedia edits on niche articles citing newly registered domains are trivially detectable patterns that providers currently ignore. A comment by simonw references a similar 2024 experiment naming a whale 'Teresa T' with just a blog post and YouTube caption, showing the pattern is not new but now amplifies through LLMs. The broader concern is that LLMs cannot distinguish a real source from a convincingly fabricated one, making the retrieval layer a soft underbelly for disinformation and supply-chain attacks.

“This attack cost $12, a single Wikipedia edit, and twenty minutes of my time. Scale that up with a motivated adversary and the attack surface gets interesting very quickly.”

Key Highlights
  • A single $12 domain and Wikipedia edit sufficed to fabricate a fact that multiple LLMs adopted.
  • Circular citation patterns (site -> Wikipedia -> site) exploit trust in retrieval-augmented generation.
  • Three failure modes: retrieval, training corpus, and agent-layer exploitation.
  • Prior art exists (e.g., simonw's whale naming) but LLMs automate and scale the trust problem.
  • Mitigations include provenance surfacing, heuristic filters for recent edits citing new domains, and skepticism toward single-source claims.

How ChatGPT serves ads
⬆ 343 pts 💬 234 HN comments

OpenAI has introduced advertising into ChatGPT, using a sophisticated attribution system detailed in a recent threat intelligence report. Ads are injected as structured 'single_advertiser_ad_unit' objects into the SSE stream of conversation responses, separate from model output. Each ad includes four Fernet-encrypted tokens (ads_spam_integrity_payload, oppref, olref, ad_data_token) for click verification and 30-day tracking via a first-party cookie (__oppref). The merchant-side OAIQ SDK loads from bzrcdn.openai.com and reports product views back to OpenAI. Targeting appears contextual to the conversation topic, as demonstrated by an experiment where six different ads were served across six unrelated conversations. This architecture allows OpenAI to A/B test ad formats without modifying core model responses, and ensures OpenAI observes all post-click navigation through its in-app webview.

The community reaction is sharply divided. Many users point out the contradiction with Sam Altman's earlier statement that ads are a 'last resort,' viewing this as a betrayal of trust. Others argue that ads are an inevitable monetization strategy for free-tier AI services, drawing parallels to Google's ad-supported model. Technical users discuss blocking strategies, noting the two domains to filter (bzrcdn.openai.com and bzr.openai.com) and the potential for adversarial content injection into model outputs. Some commenters express concern about a slippery slope where ads become indistinguishable from organic content, while others highlight that this is still a relatively transparent implementation compared to future possibilities. The overall sentiment is wary but resigned to the commercial realities of large-scale AI deployment.

OpenAI's ad system uses four Fernet-encrypted tokens per ad and a 30-day tracking cookie, with targeting derived from conversation context—all while Sam Altman previously called ads a 'last resort.'

Key Highlights
  • Ads are injected as separate SSE events, not mixed into model responses, allowing independent A/B testing.
  • Four Fernet tokens per ad enable server-side integrity checks and 30-day merchant-side attribution via cookie.
  • Targeting is contextual to the current conversation topic, not cross-session history (so far).
  • Two domains (bzrcdn.openai.com, bzr.openai.com) and two cookies (__oppref, __oaiq_domain_probe) are used for ad delivery and tracking.
  • Community response ranges from accusations of broken promises to pragmatic acceptance of ad-funded free tiers.

Show HN: Auto-Architecture: Karpathy's Loop, pointed at a CPU
⬆ 156 pts 💬 32 HN comments

This project applies Andrej Karpathy's autonomous research loop to CPU microarchitecture, showing that an LLM agent can discover optimizations in a domain far from its native language model training. The loop proposes microarchitectural hypotheses (e.g., branch predictors, multi-cycle dividers), implements them in SystemVerilog, and evaluates through formal verification (riscv-formal), co-simulation against a golden ISS, and FPGA place-and-route on a Gowin GW2A device. The critical insight is that the verifier—not the agent loop—is the differentiator. Of 73 hypotheses, 63 failed due to correctness regressions, ISA violations, or timing closure; the 10 accepted hypotheses yielded a 92% improvement in CoreMark iter/s over baseline and a 56% gain over the human-tuned VexRiscv core, with 40% fewer LUTs. The verifier caught silently broken cores, sandbox violations, and even a 73% regression that would have undone all progress.

The article argues that agent loops are commoditized, while the verifier encodes domain-specific correctness and is the defensible asset. Failures included identical ideas re-attempted with fixed implementations (multi-cycle divide), broken cosim from harness edits, and schema type errors that would corrupt the pipeline. The verifier includes formal checks beyond arithmetic, path sandboxing, CRC re-validation, and median Fmax across three seeds. Commenters debated the novelty (comparing to genetic algorithms, AlphaEvolve) and the practical value of verifiers in business, with some sharing success stories (20x CUDA kernel improvement) and others cautioning about LLM anthropomorphism and malicious compliance. The project next moves to population-based search and testing on Embench, reinforcing that the frontier is writing rules formal enough for an agent to satisfy.

Of 73 hypotheses, 63 were wrong—the verifier, not the loop, is what prevents a confidently-wrong result from shipping.

Key Highlights
  • Karpathy's loop applied to CPU design improved CoreMark iter/s by 92% over baseline and 56% over VexRiscv with fewer LUTs.
  • The verifier (formal verification, sandbox, CRC checks) caught 63 of 73 hypotheses, including a 73% regression.
  • Agent loops are commodity; the verifier is the moat that encodes domain-specific correctness.
  • Common failure modes: broken ISA, sandbox violations, schema errors, and overfitting to benchmarks.
  • Future work includes population-based search and cross-benchmark generalization to Embench.

UAE to leave OPEC

The United Arab Emirates has announced its departure from OPEC, a move that shakes a foundational pillar of global oil governance. The decision, revealed by the Financial Times, comes amid escalating tensions in the Middle East, including the ongoing Iran conflict and a widening rift between the UAE and Saudi Arabia. Historically, OPEC has functioned as a cartel coordinating production quotas to influence prices, but internal discord and geopolitical realignments have eroded its authority. The UAE's exit is partly a response to perceived Saudi dominance and the bloc's inability to protect Emirati interests during the Iran war. This defection represents the first major crack in OPEC's unity since Qatar left in 2019, but carries far greater weight given the UAE's status as the third-largest producer within the cartel, accounting for 12–13% of OPEC's output. The move is widely interpreted as a strategic pivot by the UAE toward an axis that includes Israel, Egypt, and possibly India and Ethiopia, challenging both Saudi and Iranian hegemony in the region.

The immediate market implications are nuanced. While the UAE could theoretically increase production beyond previous OPEC limits, the Strait of Hormuz remains effectively closed due to the conflict, constraining export capacity. Commenters on Hacker News dissect the decision through multiple lenses: some see it as a quid pro quo for U.S. financial or security guarantees (e.g., credit swap lines or Alaskan LNG projects), while others argue it signals a shift away from the petrodollar system, with the UAE exploring yuan-denominated oil sales. The broader debate centers on whether OPEC still wields influence; its members control about 50% of global crude exports and 80% of proven reserves, yet the cartel has been plagued by cheating and lack of enforcement. The UAE's exit could embolden other members to defect, potentially accelerating the transition away from fossil fuels (as solar and renewables become more competitive) or, conversely, leading to a short-term price war that undercuts climate goals. A highlighted quote from a commenter: 'My grandfather rode a camel, my father rode a camel, I drive a Mercedes, my son drives a Land Rover, and my grandson is going to ride a camel'—though debunked as apocryphal, it captures the existential anxiety of Gulf petrostates facing a post-oil future.

The UAE accounts for 12–13% of OPEC output; its exit, combined with the Hormuz blockage, could reshape global oil supply dynamics and accelerate the cartel's decline.
