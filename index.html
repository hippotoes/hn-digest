⬆ 410 pts 💬 241 HN comments

Crucially, the discount is an OpenRouter promotion, not an OpenAI price change — official docs still show $5/$30. Providers include OpenAI, Azure in multiple regions, and Amazon Bedrock, with uptime above 99% on most routes. AI;DR (AI; Didn't Read)
⬆ 851 pts 💬 523 HN comments

Rick Manelius's essay coins "AI;DR" as a response to the growing flood of unedited AI-generated text in personal and professional communication. In Q3 2026, AI assistance is normal, but the author draws a sharp line between using AI as a tool and acting as a copy-paste conduit: "If you’re not bothered enough to review and edit it... then I’m not going to bother reading it." The Hacker News discussion extends this from newsletters and Slack to engineering culture, where PRs are littered with long AI-generated descriptions and code comments. One commenter describes a "post readability code base" full of performative AI commentary, while another jokes about the "Claudism" of an AI adding comments explaining code that was never actually merged. The core pain point is not AI use itself, but the absence of human curation and accountability.

Beyond the surface complaint, commenters surface deeper epistemic issues. One prominent proposal: instead of sending AI output, send the prompt, because that is the only part containing the human's intent. Others reframe the act as a bizarre middleman scenario—if you wouldn't ask Alice to ghostwrite for Bob and then paste her words verbatim, why do it with Claude? Several note AI's tendency toward verbosity, over-confidence, and lack of nuance, making readers flinch. A quieter minority pushes back: if the result is genuinely insightful, provenance shouldn't matter, and a well-directed AI can be more concise than a human gatekeeper. The debate reflects a shift in content economics: a wall of text once signaled effort, but now often signals a default AI dump. The AI;DR acronym is thus a norm-setting move—an attempt to revalue attention in an era of cheap words.

"If you’re not bothered enough to review and edit it... then I’m not going to bother reading it."

AI-Generated GitHub Copilot “Autofix” Allowed Compromise of Snowflake's Jira
⬆ 367 pts 💬 141 HN comments

The article details Wiz Research's autonomous "Red Agent" discovering a GitHub Actions script-injection vulnerability in Snowflake's snowflake-connector-net repository. The flaw, live from June 18, 2026, after PR #1218 was merged, arose when a workflow was refactored from a safe `env` + `jq --arg` pattern to direct shell interpolation of `${{ github.event.issue.title }}`. A carefully crafted issue title containing a single quote breaks out of `echo '...'`, enabling arbitrary command execution on a GitHub Actions runner. Notably, GitHub Advanced Security analyzed the final PR but did not flag the injection; the protective-looking `if` condition referencing `github.event.pull_request.user.login` always evaluated true on `issues` events because that field is null. The AI agent autonomously exploited the flaw, adjusted payload after syntax errors, exfiltrated credentials via OAST callback, and accessed Snowflake's internal Jira — all within five days, without human intervention.

Snowflake patched within hours, rotated the Jira token, and confirmed no third-party access. The incident underscores a new reality: AI coding assistants can "autofix" or modify workflows with probabilistic guesses, and when reviewed routinely, insecure patterns slip through, while autonomous AI security agents shrink discovery to days. The HN discussion quickly questions the headline's accuracy: comments by vultour/croemer note the vulnerable commit may not have been authored by Copilot, while others like kozikow argue that adversarial outlier must be measured against the denominator of AI-assisted changes. Commenters also emphasize static analysis (zizmor, shellcheck, env variables), the perils of YAML interpolation, fail-open null checks in GitHub expressions, and that verification—not generation—is now the bottleneck. The episode is a case study in CI/CD security regression, where a seemingly cleanup PR removed defensive patterns and no automated gate caught it.

The vulnerable workflow was live for just five days before an autonomous agent discovered and exploited it — even though GitHub Advanced Security had already scanned that exact final PR and missed the injection.

The model family shows meaningful stratification: Terra and Luna are close on detection (44.7/43.3) and improve counting (67.6/66.2), but latency ranges from ~10s/image for Sol to ~5s for Luna, making Luna the value pick at <$0.005/image. These results matter less for one-off prompts than for agentic workloads and auto-annotation pipelines. Author Piotr Skalski admits in HN comments that the post, written four weeks earlier, is already outdated: Gemini 3.7 Flash is now the better low-cost choice. HN reaction is split: skeptics argue pill counting and box detection are solved more cheaply with OpenCV and 0.8B local models, while practitioners report strong results in UI redesign, video captioning, sheet music transcription, and grocery navigation. The New Yorker's photo essay, adapted into a photo book and upcoming film, documents 'los puesteros' of southern Chilean Patagonia—solitary, aging men employed to watch over remote ranches and livestock. Working for meager pay in one of the most volatile climates on Earth, they often go weeks or months without any human contact. Dutch photographer Pie Aerts first spotted them from afar in 2018; during the 2020 lockdown, he began exploring their isolation and eventually partnered with filmmaker Matías Bolla to create 'Coirón,' named for a resilient native grass. The article emphasizes the men's profound attachment to land they will never own, quoting one: 'What a shame to come to love the land that will never be mine.' Aerts describes the language barrier as a 'fertile constraint,' leading to silent, nonverbal connection—a stark counterpoint to hyper-connected modern life. Technically and thematically, the piece and its resulting book occupy a curious intersection of analog documentary, environmental precarity, and tech-era longing.
