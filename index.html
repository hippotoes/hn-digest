Livenerf: Has Opus 5.5 been nerfed yet?
⬆ 758 pts 💬 317 HN comments

livenerf is a deliberately narrow, append-only measurement instrument built to answer one question that the LLM community has debated almost entirely on vibes: does a frontier model silently get worse after launch? Author ninjahawk starts the clock on Claude Opus 5.5's 2026-09-22 release, running a frozen panel once a day for 30 days — days 1–10 as baseline, then two 10-day comparison windows, with the first verdict possible around 2026-10-24. Because sampling parameters are gone and thinking cannot be disabled, livenerf makes everything else deterministic instead: one frozen system prompt, a hash-pinned Claude Code CLI (2.1.280), hermetic `claude -p` calls with no tools, MCP servers, hooks, memory or CLAUDE.md, a fixed empty working directory, and exact-match graders that never invoke an LLM judge. It is built on Inspect, the UK AI Security Institute's open-source eval framework, with statistics following Anthropic's own 'Adding Error Bars to Evals' (Miller 2024).

The engineering discipline is in the selection and the decision rule. From 2,336 GPQA Diamond, MMLU-Pro, competition-math and AIME questions screened with 4 samples each, only the 78 the model sometimes gets right form the panel — pass rates rose from 54.7% to 62.0% on fresh samples, and the power calculation uses those corrected rates. One daily run detects roughly a 7.5-point accuracy change per 10-day window for about 3.6% of a weekly Max plan, budgeted in the same percentage meters `/usage` shows. Validation moved tokens far more than accuracy (effort low: −62% output tokens, −8.3 ± 4.5 points), which is why output-token count is the secondary signal. Crucially, the readme admits its own limit: swapping Opus 5 for Opus 5.5 was not distinguishable at 99% (−3.8 ± 6.3 points, −23% tokens). A change is only called a change if a 99% interval excludes zero in two consecutive windows, exceeds 3 points, and the Opus 5 control arm does not move. Eight answer keys look wrong and 30 questions are ambiguous — reported, not dropped.

livenerf can flag a ~7.5-point accuracy drop per 10-day window for ~3.6% of a weekly Max plan — yet its own validation admits it cannot statistically distinguish Opus 5 from Opus 5.5 (−3.8 ± 6.3 points, −23% tokens), the very same-family swap a real 'nerf' might resemble.

Key Highlights
  • livenerf pre-registers its panel, metric and decision rule in a public git timestamp before collecting any series data, using paired per-item score deltas with clustered standard errors so item difficulty cancels out.
  • 78 questions are selected from 2,336 candidates specifically because Opus 5.5 gets them right only sometimes; always-right questions carry no information and are filtered out.
  • Output token count is treated as a leading indicator, since validation showed effort reductions cut output tokens (−62% at low effort) far more sharply than measured accuracy (−8.3 ± 4.5 points).
  • The harness pins the Claude Code CLI version and refuses to run if `claude --version` drifts, because a CLI update is indistinguishable from a model change.
  • The readme concedes the instrument's detection floor: an Opus 5-for-5.5 substitution produced a −3.8 ± 6.3 point difference, below the pre-registered 99% threshold, while a report-only audit found 8 likely-wrong answer keys and 30 ambiguous questions.

Language models for text classification: From bag-of-words to Jev
⬆ 166 pts 💬 7 HN comments

Sebastian Raschka's visual guide traces the evolution of text classification from bag-of-words to the newly hyped Jev model, positioning Jev as a general-purpose classifier that sits between task-specific models and full LLMs. The article methodically covers bag-of-words with naive Bayes/logistic regression, RNNs with LSTM/ULMFiT, CNNs, and transformer-based approaches like BERT and GPT. It highlights that while bag-of-words loses word order, it remains a cheap baseline (89.9% on IMDb), whereas LSTM from scratch underperforms (85.66%) due to overfitting. Pre-training and fine-tuning, as in ULMFiT (95.4%) and ModernBERT (~95%), prove crucial. Jev's selling point is its generality: it can classify diverse texts without task-specific fine-tuning, akin to a 'ChatGPT moment for classification,' but it trades off some accuracy for speed and cost efficiency.

The article and HN discussion emphasize calibration as a critical, often overlooked aspect for production deployment. A commenter notes that a 96% model with overconfident outputs is operationally worse than a 94% model with honest probabilities, citing Guo et al. on networks overfitting NLL without overfitting 0/1 loss. This matters for routing decisions like email triage. The community's reaction is largely positive, with practitioners sharing real-world email classification experiments and praising the historical framing. However, Topfi warns about hype-induced misunderstandings, especially from game demos that implied visual input support when actually using game state. Overall, the piece demystifies Jev while acknowledging its potential as a cheaper, faster alternative to LLMs for classification tasks.

Like ChatGPT in 2022 was exciting because it was a general-purpose chat model that could generate all kinds of texts, one of the reasons the tech community is excited about Jev is that it is the ChatGPT moment for classification.

How Delhi cut electricity loss from 50 to 5 percent
⬆ 548 pts 💬 298 HN comments

Delhi's grid transformation is a case study in how commercial and technical losses compound. In the early 2000s, the city's nearly 100-year-old distribution network was overloaded, unmetered in places, and losing more than half its power. The article lays out the physics: distribution lines carry active and reactive power; inductive loads raise current, and I²R heating wastes energy, while reactive power causes voltage drops that make devices draw more current—a vicious cycle. But the root causes were institutional as much as electrical: rampant theft, faulty meters, manual billing, corrupt and undertrained staff, and no accountability. India's Electricity Act 2003 unbundled generation, transmission, and distribution, enabled privatization, created regulators, and allowed theft prosecution. In 2002, Delhi Vidyut Board was split; Tata Power and BSES took over distribution with losses of 53.5% and 51.5–63.1%.

By 2026, Delhi cut losses to 5–6%, matching France and Belgium, and raised reliability from about 70% to over 99.9%. Peak demand reached 8,748 MW; Tata's transformer failure rate fell from 11% to under 1%. Utilities deployed SCADA for real-time visibility, hundreds of capacitor banks to supply reactive power locally, voltage regulators, and insulated three-phase cables that deter hooking. Digital meters and billing reduced tampering. In theft-heavy low-income pockets, Tata and BSES paired infrastructure with water improvements, financial literacy, and paid women to collect payments. HN commenters debated how much was technical versus theft reduction and privatization, and questioned the Estonia comparison. The social impact is clear: reliable power enables EV buses, businesses, and rooftop solar adoption, while shifting the grid toward distributed generation.

Delhi cut combined technical and commercial electricity losses from over 50% in 2002 to 5–6% in 2026—on par with France and Belgium—while grid reliability rose from ~70% to over 99.9%.

Key Highlights
  • Delhi's combined technical/commercial losses fell from >50% in 2002 to 5–6% in 2026, with reliability rising from ~70% to >99.9%.
  • Technical fixes included SCADA, capacitor banks for reactive power, voltage regulators, insulated three-phase cables, transformer replacements, and digital meters.
  • Theft and billing reform were central: privatized distribution by Tata Power and BSES, accountability, digital billing, and community payment collection.
  • India's Electricity Act 2003 unbundled generation/transmission/distribution, enabled privatization, and created mechanisms for theft prosecution.
  • HN debate focuses on Estonia data provenance, World Bank loss metrics, and whether privatized incentives explain most gains; rooftop solar is seen as the next phase.
