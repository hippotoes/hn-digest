#5
Gemini Decisions API
⬆ 526 pts 💬 151 HN comments

Google's Gemini Decisions API is a deliberate departure from the chat-completion paradigm: instead of decoding an answer token by token, the new gemini-decision-1 model scores a closed set of enumerated candidate answers in a single forward pass and returns normalized probabilities plus a 0-1 confidence score. The endpoint exposes three purpose-built primitives — "noul" (yes/no with optional true/false criteria), "choice" (2-255 candidates, returning the winner and a distribution over all of them), and "score" (an ordered rubric rendered as a continuous weighted grade with per-level probabilities). One shared multimodal input — text, images, audio, video, or PDFs up to 256K tokens, using the same Content blocks as the Interactions API — can be interrogated by up to 1,000 independent questions in one request, and that shared context is billed exactly once. The framing matters: this turns intent classification, policy checking, and routing into declarative configuration rather than custom classifier training or latency-heavy generative calls.

The economics and latency claims are the sharp end. Google reports a median of 50-100 ms of model compute (150-250 ms end-to-end), nearly flat from 1 to 100 questions per request, at $0.10 per 1M input tokens with output tokens billed at zero — roughly 5x cheaper across its shared benchmark suite than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite on the Interactions API, and with no separate OCR or transcription line items. Google also claims the lowest calibration error in its comparison set, which is the metric that actually matters for closed-set probability outputs. Internal dogfooding is instructive: Gemini Notebook uses it as a millisecond router to cut everyday query latency by up to 40%, Search uses it as an adversarial-content safety layer for MCP tools, and CC picks one emoji from 200+ options without visible latency. It ships into a crowded lane already occupied by Typesafe AI's Jev and OpenAI's Decisions API, and Vertex availability is still pending.

Up to 1,000 yes/no, multiple-choice, and rubric questions answered in a single forward pass over one 256K-token multimodal input — at $0.10/1M input tokens and zero billed output tokens, with 150-250 ms end-to-end latency.

Key Highlights
  • Single forward-pass scoring replaces autoregressive decoding, yielding probabilities and confidence scores rather than generated text
  • Three primitives — noul, choice (2-255 options), and ordered rubric scoring — cover routing, policy, and grading use cases
  • Native multimodal ingestion (text, image, audio, video, PDF) up to 256K tokens is the capability most commenters consider the real differentiator
  • $0.10/1M input, $0 output, shared input billed once per request — roughly 5x cheaper than Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite on the Interactions API
  • Ecosystem integrations land with OpenRouter, LangChain, Vercel, LiteLLM, Pydantic AI, Pipecat, LiveKit, and OpenCode; Vertex support not yet available

Tim Peters has died
⬆ 1358 pts 💬 165 HN comments

Guido van Rossum's memorial post on discuss.python.org confirmed that Tim Peters died on July 15, 2026, at 74 — news that reached much of the Python community only in November. The obituary frames Peters not as a peripheral contributor but as a language co-designer: the person who persuaded Guido that int/float mixed arithmetic should Just Work, who acted as gatekeeper and polisher for early feature proposals, and who set the tone of core development by never dismissing people outright and always explaining why something could or could not work. His resume predates Python entirely — Cray Research compilers, Dragon Systems speech recognition, Harley-Davidson — and that compiler and numerical-analysis background shows up everywhere in CPython: Timsort, the dict implementation and its 'citizen/virgin/turd' slot-state notes, the Karatsuba long-object multiplication, doctest, difflib, pymalloc's Py_ADDRESS_IN_RANGE, and the hardening of Neil Schemenauer's cyclic GC and simple generators.

The technical artifacts are the concrete legacy. Objects/listsort.txt — a design note as much essay as specification — was cited by one commenter as the reason he chose a programming language at all; Timsort went on to Java and Android, and became entangled in the Oracle v. Google litigation. `import this` remains the most widely executed three-word program in the ecosystem, and its payload is stored rot13-encoded in this.s, a detail that delighted the thread. Commenters also surfaced Peters' non-CPython work: SpamBayes with Gary Robinson and Robert Woodhead, turning Paul Graham's 'A Plan for Spam' into working Bayesian filters two decades before probabilistic inference became fashionable. He served on the PSF board from 2001–2014. The thread's harder edge is that the announcement came four months late, and that Peters spent his final years suspended from the community he built.

"The real world is more complicated than our clean abstractions hope for" — the closing line of Tim Peters' last post on the Python forums, quoted as his epitaph.

Key Highlights
  • Timsort, shipped in 2002 for Python lists, was later adopted by Java and Android and featured in Oracle v. Google.
  • Peters convinced Guido that int/float mixed arithmetic should work implicitly — arguably the first design change he drove.
  • Beyond sorting, he hardened pymalloc, optimized dict and long-object (Karatsuba) arithmetic, wrote doctest, difflib, tabnanny and pickletools.
  • He co-implemented SpamBayes from Paul Graham's 'A Plan for Spam' — Bayesian filtering roughly 20 years before LLM-era probabilistic inference.
  • He served as a PSF director 2001–2014, and was suspended by the Steering Committee in a controversy that dominates the thread's negative sentiment.

#2
AWS emulator
⬆ 931 pts 💬 255 HN comments

AWS has published aws/aws-emulator, an official, CC0-1.0-licensed local emulator of the AWS APIs written in Rust, and the Hacker News reaction (931 points, 255 comments) is less about the tool than about how it came to exist. Architecturally the project is notable: the workspace holds one crate per AWS service, each driven by a checked-in Smithy model that generates the service declaration and therefore the wire protocol, with a central router binary mounting every service behind a single HTTPS endpoint on 127.0.0.1:9297 with a self-signed certificate. State is in-memory per service, operations are one file each, and an `embedded` facade lets Rust test suites wire the emulator's HTTP client directly into an SDK client in-process — no endpoint, cert, or environment variable. Fidelity gaps are tracked as files under `issues/things-that-do-not-work/` versus `issues/things-that-now-work/`, with per-release fidelity scores.

The cost of that completeness is engineering reality: the repo demands a Rust toolchain, a C compiler, and 'at least 100 GB of free disk for target/' because a debug build compiles every service crate — commenters note you cannot even compile it on 32 GB of RAM without throttling `jobs`. Telemetry is on by default (opt-out via `--telemetry off`, `EMULATOR_TELEMETRY`, or `config.toml`), the repo takes no PRs, and AWS explicitly warns against using it for security testing: it 'never denies a request signed with its own key' and does not evaluate KMS key policies or S3 bucket policies. Practical reports are mixed — one commenter found Serverless Framework `Assign` fields and Lambda Python unsupported where MiniStack worked — while others celebrated the end of the LocalStack tax.

A cold build of the emulator needs 'at least 100 GB of free disk for target/' and won't compile on 32 GB of RAM without lowering `jobs` — the price of one Rust crate per AWS service, across 30,585 commits.

Key Highlights
  • One Rust crate per AWS service, each with a checked-in Smithy model that generates the wire protocol — unlike Floci/MiniStack which hand-implement common endpoints
  • Embedded mode compiles the emulator into your test binary for isolated in-process tests, but inherits the same multi-hundred-GB build cost
  • AWS's own security docs disqualify it for IAM policy, bucket policy, KMS key policy, and cross-account/cross-region testing; the emulator never denies its own signed requests
  • No quick state reset short of restarting the process, making it a poor drop-in for Moto-style per-test isolation
  • Commenters frame the real novelty as agents building and verifying against the live service — a bounded, property-testable task where Rust's static guarantees give fast feedback loops

#3
A false smoke alarm almost drowned Jane Street's only NYC datacenter
⬆ 547 pts 💬 259 HN comments

Michael Bacarella's account of a false smoke alarm at Jane Street's only New York datacenter is really an essay about coupling. The firm deliberately kept its machine room across the hall from the trading floor — partly to keep proprietary trading strategies physically out of reach, partly because a partner with nuclear-submarine experience believed automation must always have a mechanically connected lever you can pull. That ten-by-ten-cabinet room inside 1 New York Plaza eventually housed an online UPS, CRACs, refrigerator-sized switches and a subterranean diesel generator, all subject to ordinary office-building fire code. So when contractors cutting into an adjacent prep room kicked up dust and tripped the smoke detector, three systems interlocked in exactly the wrong order: sprinkler mains pressurized, the fire panel de-energized the CRACs so as not to feed a hypothetical fire, and the room began baking toward the ~150 °F fusible-link threshold that would have sprayed water over every rack.

Bacarella, who lived a nine-minute walk away and had told everyone so, ran down Water Street and yanked power cords cabinet by cabinet; the temperature climbed to 143.6 °F and stopped. Because the NYC markets were closed, the blast radius was one night of downtime and a post-mortem that — in Jane Street's matter-of-fact style — kept the sprinklers and the building location anyway. The durable fix was software: an OCaml daemon that asks every Linux box for its onboard temperature each minute and alerts on a rising fleet-wide mean, because CRAC telemetry is less trustworthy than the servers themselves. HN commenters pushed back on the absent EPO button and the CRAC interlock — the IMC explicitly permits keeping air handlers running where a single-space unit under 2,000 cfm will not spread smoke — while others traded Sandy-era diesel-hauling lore.

The room peaked at 143.6 °F — roughly six degrees below the sprinkler heads' thermal trigger — after an alarm that never involved any fire at all.

Key Highlights
  • Smoke detection pressure-fills sprinkler mains, but discharge requires a separate heat-fusible link near each head — hence a race against 150 °F, not against smoke.
  • The same alarm that must silence air handling to starve a fire also removes all cooling from a high-density room, creating a failure mode where the suppression trigger is the load itself.
  • The fix was operational telemetry rather than new hardware: an OCaml poller reading per-server temperature sensors and alerting on fleet-wide mean drift, chosen precisely because it is independent of CRAC/BMS reporting.
  • Commenters note the missing emergency power off (EPO) button, which US code increasingly expects in datacenters, and debate whether tripping it proactively would have been the correct move.
  • Jane Street kept both the sprinklers and the trading-floor-adjacent machine room after the incident; Hurricane Sandy later forced the relocation question anyway.

#4
93% of paint splatters are valid Perl programs (2019)
⬆ 536 pts 💬 69 HN comments

Colin McMillen’s SIGBOVIK 2019 paper treats a joke—can you smear paint on a wall without producing valid Perl?—as an empirical programming-languages question. He ran OCR over 100 paint-splatter images and asked whether each recognized string parses as Perl, reporting that 93% do; only seven splatters failed. The result is less about paint than about Perl 5’s grammar: a dynamic lexer, bareword and unquoted-string features, and context-sensitive parsing let many arbitrary alphanumeric runs become string literals or expressions. The paper’s deadpan style (“We answer this question in the affirmative”) and its gallery of “pretty Perl programs” are SIGBOVIK hallmarks; it even won an “Unwitting Participation Ribbon” for containing a genuine artifact. The technical claim is deliberately fragile—it depends on OCR settings—but it is a real probe of how permissive a language grammar can be.

The data points are small but memorable: 100 images, seven failures, multiple OCR configurations, and an errata list of accidental programs. OCR reads one splatter as lerzfijglpFiji-j (evaluates to 0), another as -*? (also 0), another as ;i;c;;#\?z{;?;;fn':.; (evaluates to "c"), and ;E,'__' (evaluates to E__). Commenters immediately foreground the confound: traditional Tesseract is tuned for high-contrast text, and selecting OCR settings until output parses is not a baseline. A blank file is valid Perl; “stitch & shutter” is a valid bareword expression; Java properties and CSV are similarly permissive. Others cite Perl’s shell heritage and unquoted strings, note a PEG-based Perl 11 might reject the noise, and propose LLM-constrained Rust or reversing the exercise to execute paintings. The artifact endures as a compact lesson in grammar laxness, OCR hallucination, and HN’s split between laughing with the joke and auditing its method.

“Merely 93% of paint splatters parse as valid Perl,” the paper deadpans—but the interesting number is 7/100 failures, since OCR settings and Perl’s bareword grammar dominate the result.

Key Highlights
  • SIGBOVIK 2019 paper uses OCR on 100 paint-splatter images; 93 parse as valid Perl, 7 do not.
  • Validity stems largely from Perl 5’s dynamic lexer, unquoted strings/barewords, and context-sensitive grammar.
  • Critics argue the experiment measures tuned OCR output, not paint or Perl, and lacks an OCR baseline.
  • Errata examples show accidental programs evaluating to 0, "c", and E__, plus non-Perl-specific permissiveness.
  • Commenters extend the joke to LLM-constrained Rust, reverse execution, and obfuscated-code contests.
