Amateur armed with ChatGPT solves an Erdős problem
⬆ 354 pts 💬 213 HN comments

Liam Price, a 23-year-old with no advanced mathematics training, used a ChatGPT Pro subscription (GPT-5.4 Pro) to solve a 60-year-old Erdős problem about primitive sets. The conjecture, which states that the sum of 1/(n log n) over any primitive set of integers greater than x approaches 1 as x grows, had resisted proof by prominent mathematicians including Jared Lichtman. The AI's solution employed a novel method—using a formula well-known in related areas but never previously applied to this type of problem. Terence Tao noted that experts had collectively taken a 'slight wrong turn at move one,' creating a mental block that the LLM bypassed. However, the raw output was 'quite poor' and required expert sifting by Lichtman and Tao to distill the key insight. The proof has been shortened and is now available at erdosproblems.com.

This achievement marks a departure from earlier AI math 'solutions' that often turned out to be rediscoveries or less original. Here, the AI's approach was genuinely novel, validating an intuition held by Lichtman that these problems share a unifying structure. Tao stated, 'We have discovered a new way to think about large numbers and their anatomy.' The cost-effectiveness is striking: Price's monthly subscription fee versus years of concerted effort by top mathematicians. Yet the solution still required human experts to interpret and formalize it, highlighting the current symbiotic role of AI in mathematical research. The broader implications are significant: if LLMs can routinely uncover such cross-domain connections, they could accelerate progress on a range of open problems, though the long-term significance remains under evaluation.

The AI 'used a formula that was well known in related parts of math, but which no one had thought to apply to this type of question.' — Terence Tao

USB Cheat Sheet (2022)
⬆ 303 pts 💬 58 HN comments

Fabien Sanglard's 'USB Cheat Sheet' serves as a definitive technical reference for the labyrinthine world of USB standards, addressing the notorious naming confusion that plagues even seasoned engineers. The article meticulously catalogs every major USB generation—from USB 1.1's 12 Mbps to USB4's 40 Gbps—including marketing names, signaling speeds, encoding overhead, real-world throughput, and cable wiring requirements. It highlights the absurdity of rebranding: USB 3.0 became USB 3.1 Gen 1, then USB 3.2 Gen 1, all while technically identical. The cheat sheet also explains lane configurations (Gen x A x B notation), the evolution from 4-wire (half-duplex) to 12-wire (four lanes) cables, and the Type-C connector's unique capabilities for power delivery (up to 240W with PD 3.1 EPR) and alternate modes. This resource is invaluable for anyone decoding product specs or debugging hardware issues.

The comment thread amplifies the article's core tension—USB naming is 'indefensibly awful' (esskay) and 'misleading by design' (maxloh) to allow vendors to sell old stock as new. A notable correction comes from SyncOnGreen clarifying that SBU stands for 'Sideband Use,' not 'Secondary Bus,' and that HPD in DP Alt Mode uses CC lines, not SBU. Commenters also contrast USB's chaos with Thunderbolt's clarity: 'Thunderbolt 5 cable will always support 80 Gbps, DisplayPort 2.1, PCIe, USB4 and power of up to 240 watt' (retired). A deeper technical insight emerges from 15155, requesting additions like PAM3 encoding for USB4v2 and proprietary QuickCharge schemes. The discussion underscores a systemic industry failure—where standards bodies prioritize backward compatibility and vendor flexibility over user clarity, forcing engineers to rely on cheat sheets like this to navigate a fragmented ecosystem.

USB 3.2 Gen 2x2 offers 20 Gbps using two lanes with 128b/132b encoding, yet real-world sequential reads reach only 1,600 MiB/s due to overhead—far from theoretical 2,500 MiB/s.

Key Highlights
  • USB naming is rebranded across versions: USB 3.0 ≡ USB 3.1 Gen 1 ≡ USB 3.2 Gen 1.
  • Multi-lane USB (e.g., Gen 2x2) uses lane striping on TX and lane bonding on RX.
  • USB Type-C supports up to 12 wires, enabling two lanes and alternate modes like DisplayPort.
  • Thunderbolt 5 is a strict profile of USB4 v2, ensuring 80 Gbps, 240W power, and mandatory features.
  • Real-world throughput suffers from encoding overhead (8b/10b, 128b/132b) and system limitations.

Martin Galway's music source files from 1980's Commodore 64 games
⬆ 174 pts 💬 25 HN comments

Martin Galway, the legendary composer of Commodore 64 game music, has released the original source files for his iconic tracks on GitHub. The repository includes assembly code for titles like Wizball and Game Over, using the '1st Generation' player (1984–1987) and the later '2nd Generation' player. Galway acquired the rights from Infogrames and licensed the work under GPL-3.0, allowing re-assembly, modification, and new music generation. This is a rare treasure for chiptune enthusiasts, reverse engineers, and retro-computing historians, as it exposes the inner workings of the SID chip music players used by Ocean Software.

The release garnered 174 points and 25 comments on Hacker News, reflecting strong interest from the tech community. The source files, written in 6502 assembly, are accompanied by raw assembler directives (e.g., 'DSP' for displacement) that reveal the development process. Commenters praised Galway's work, with some recalling childhood memories and others diving into technical analysis. Notably, attempts to translate the music into modern platforms like Strudel JS via AI (Claude) were met with mixed results, highlighting the gap between algorithmic generation and human composition. The repository serves as both a historical artifact and a learning resource for understanding constrained yet expressive audio programming.

Martin Galway: 'I am the current copyright owner in all this music & programming code, but was not the owner at the time it was created in the 1980's. I acquired the rights from Infogrames later.'

America's Geothermal Breakthrough
⬆ 109 pts 💬 124 HN comments

Enhanced geothermal systems (EGS) are emerging as a game-changer for baseload renewable energy, with the potential to unlock up to 150 GW of clean, constant power in the United States—far exceeding the current 2.7 GW of conventional geothermal capacity. By adapting hydraulic fracturing techniques from oil and gas, companies like Fervo Energy are drilling horizontally into hot rock formations to create artificial hydrothermal reservoirs where none exist naturally. This allows geothermal development beyond traditional hotspots like California and Nevada. The U.S. Geological Survey estimates 135 GW of EGS potential in the Great Basin alone, while Fervo’s leased acreage across the West could support over 42 GW of capacity. The first commercial EGS plant—Fervo’s 100 MW Cape Station in Utah—is slated to launch in 2026, with a total resource potential of 4.3 GW. Federal support, including a $171.5 million DOE funding round under the Trump administration, underscores bipartisan interest in geothermal as a grid-stabilizing complement to solar and wind.

Fervo Energy’s recent IPO filing (ticker FRVO on Nasdaq) signals growing investor confidence, though skepticism remains. The company has signed a three-year deal with Turboden America for 1.75 GW of turbine capacity, targeting both grid electricity and data center loads. Critics note that the 150 GW figure, while technically possible, depends on continuous innovation and cost reduction. The actual technological breakthrough is incremental—better drilling and reservoir engineering—not a single leap. However, the integration of EGS with existing power infrastructure could provide firm, dispatchable clean energy at a scale comparable to hydropower, without geographic constraints. If successful, geothermal could cover 10% of U.S. electricity demand (roughly 150 GW of the ~1,500 GW total) and serve as a critical baseload partner for intermittent renewables, reducing reliance on natural gas peaker plants.

Enhanced geothermal systems could unlock up to 150 GW of firm, carbon-free power in the U.S. alone—equivalent to roughly 10% of current total generation—using fracking-inspired drilling techniques.

  • Federal support includes $171.5M DOE funding under Trump's energy policy, and Geothermal enjoys bipartisan backing unlike other renewables.
  • The 150 GW estimate is derived from U.S. Geological Survey data for the Great Basin, but realizable capacity depends on cost, permitting, and grid integration.

Quirks of Human Anatomy
This piece by Lewis Held presents a detailed catalog of human anatomical 'quirks'—structures that are suboptimal or outright dangerous due to evolutionary constraints. Drawing on comparative anatomy and developmental biology, Held explains how features like the inverted retina, the recurrent laryngeal nerve, and the tight birth canal stem from historical contingencies rather than intelligent design. For example, the retina is 'inside out' because our chordate ancestors internalized their neural tube by invagination, dragging the eyespot along and reversing its orientation. This inversion then forced ganglion cell axons to exit through the retina, creating a blind spot. The article is rich with cross-species comparisons (e.g., squid have right-side-out retinas) and medical implications, such as May-Thurner Syndrome, where a normal vascular crossover can cause life-threatening clots. Held's analysis resonates deeply with the engineering mindset: evolution is a tinkerer, not an engineer, and the body is a legacy codebase full of non-negotiable technical debt.
