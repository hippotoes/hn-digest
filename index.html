Redesigning source control at Anthropic
⬆ 817 pts 💬 873 HN comments

Anthropic's engineering post details Scion, an in-house version control system built to replace git for its monorepo after agentic coding broke the economics of human-scale tooling. Merges to main grew from roughly 2,500/day in May 2026 to over 30,000/day by September; main-branch history alone is nearly 100 GB, a compressed working copy exceeds 25 GB, and CI alone performs on the order of 25 million checkouts per day — naively over 600 PB of git traffic. Git's decentralized, offline-first design (every clone a complete, self-contained repo) actively fights this workload: shallow clones, sparse checkouts, promisor remotes, blob-storage seeds and several hundred internal mirrors all bought time, but buckled as merge rate rose and mirrors hit disk and memory limits.

Scion inverts git's assumptions: clients are always online, the server owns history, blame, merges and rebases, and commits become server-side 'changesets' — write-ahead-log entries listing only files changed relative to the first parent, plus content-addressed pointers into blob storage. Everything else (file blame, path history, commit-graph skip lists, copy detection) is recursively derived data, computed eagerly or lazily, invisible to clients, and cheap to add. Refs are discouraged; moving one is a compare-and-swap on a single Spanner row. The scn client negotiates immutable trees and blobs rather than multi-gigabyte packfiles, enabling resumable, cache-friendly fetches across a strictly tiered daemon/cache/server fleet at >99% hit rates. Reported results: a 200x smaller CPU/memory footprint than the old mirror fleet, checkouts in seconds instead of minutes, a 25% CI CPU capacity win — and a seven-person team that also shipped a review surface (Antique), search and IAM in five months.

A seven-person team replaced git for a 100 GB monorepo in five months, cutting infrastructure CPU/memory by 200x and checkout times from minutes to seconds — while Claude rewrote their hand-written ingestion pipeline to use 20x less memory and run 4x faster with 'no further instructions.'

Key Highlights
  • Merge volume to Anthropic's monorepo grew from ~2,500/day (May 2026) to 30,000+/day (September), with ~100 GB of main-branch history and 25M CI checkouts/day.
  • Scion replaces git commits with 'changesets': WAL entries storing changed-file lists relative to the first parent plus content-addressed blob pointers, so commits are cheap, lock-free and server-side.
  • Blame, path history and commit-graph skip lists are 'derived data' computed recursively from changesets, eagerly or lazily, and can be optimized without touching clients.
  • The scn client fetches immutable trees/blobs recursively instead of packfiles, with a strictly tiered scion-daemon / scion-cache / scion-server architecture at >99% cache hit rates.
  • Anthropic has abandoned a global merge-queue lock, investing instead in post-merge and pre-deploy testing — accepting that concurrent changes can conflict and trusting Claude to fix them faster than humans can detect them.

Show HN: Guitoro, a virtual guitar you strum with your keyboard
⬆ 488 pts 💬 127 HN comments

Guitoro is a browser-native virtual guitar that abandons the click-a-fret model of most online instruments in favour of sample-playback performance: every note across steel-string acoustic, nylon classic, bridge-pickup electric and four-string bass is a recording of a real instrument, not a synthesised tone. The core insight is that a guitar is a strummed, polyphonic, timing-sensitive instrument, so the UI reimplements it as one — left-hand chord shapes under the physical letter keys (A–G for diatonic chords, Q–T for sevenths, Z–B for sus/add9 colours), right hand on H/J for down/up strokes, with dedicated keys for arpeggios, bass note, top-three-strings, chucks and palm mutes. Because chords use real voicings (open strings, barres, mutes, capo up to fret 10, ten tunings), the artefact is as much a fretboard-visualisation engine as a synth: strings light on impact, ring with vibration, and the pick animates solid or faint depending on whether a stroke actually contacts the strings — turning invisible hand motion into observable state.

The stats are unusually dense for a solo Show HN: 488 points, 127 comments, 24 major/minor keys, 51 songs and classical pieces (Wonderwall, Hotel California, Canon in D, Toccata and Fugue), 56 strum and picking patterns, 15 drum styles, 25 bass lines, tempo scaling from 100% to 20% with the band slowing in lockstep, and up to 10-minute takes exported as WAV from purely client-side storage — no account, no download, no server round-trip. Author talccc engaged every critique in-thread, shipping an orientation fix mid-discussion and conceding points on sustaining shared strings through chord changes and palm-mute articulation. The most striking downstream signal is accessibility: a 20-year player reported showing it to his father, who lost guitar grip after a stroke, while a commenter building a "physical guitar digital luthier kit" for disabled musicians at Toronto Metropolitan University swapped engineering notes with the author.

The default "player view" — low E string on top, neck to the left — is what a right-handed guitarist sees looking down at their own instrument, and it exists so that a downward mouse movement produces a downstroke; that deliberate inversion of every tab chart in existence became the single most contested design decision in the thread.

Key Highlights
  • Every note is a recorded sample of a real instrument across four instruments, not FM/wavetable synthesis — realism is the product thesis, not a feature.
  • The keyboard mapping is physical, not arbitrary: chord rows mirror scale function (I IV V vi ii / sevenths / colour chords) while H/J strumming splits the hands like a real guitar.
  • Fretboard orientation is the defining UX controversy — the default 'player view' is a left-handed-appearing guitar chosen so downward mouse motion equals a downstroke, contradicting all tablature convention.
  • Observability is the pedagogical mechanism: per-string lighting, up/down pick animation with solid/faint strokes, and finger numbers on the neck convert hidden hand technique into visible state.
  • Purely client-side persistence (browser-local songs and takes, WAV export) plus no account or install makes it resistant to shutdown — prompting standalone/Electron/VST requests.

The United Kingdom generates less electricity per person than in the 1970s
⬆ 348 pts 💬 577 HN comments

The UK’s per-capita electricity generation peaked in 2003 and has since fallen by more than a third, returning to late-1960s levels—an inversion of the global trend, where per-capita generation grew over 50% this century. Our World in Data notes that part of the decline is not lower demand but higher imports: net imports rose from 1% of electricity demand in 2010 to 9% in 2025. The rest reflects a mix of deindustrialization and genuine efficiency. IEA sectoral data shows industrial consumption fell by a quarter since 2005, but residential and commercial use fell by a similar amount, undercutting the simple narrative that the UK merely offshored its factories. The technical story is one of changed end-use: lighting, appliances, heating systems, and building envelopes all became less electricity-intensive. This matters because electrifying heat and transport is supposed to raise demand, not lower it.

HN commenters dug into the mechanisms. advisedwang estimated that replacing five 60W incandescent bulbs used 8 hours daily with 8W LEDs saves roughly 2 kWh per household per day—a visible chunk of the national drop. clort pointed to street lighting, where 250–500W sodium lamps gave way to 50–150W LEDs; MrSkelter cited condensing dryers, on-demand boilers, and better insulation. But the thread also turned to economics: dzonga argued 'a growing economy simply uses more electricity,' while ChrisKnott retorted that shutting a steel mill and replacing it with ARM raises GDP while cutting energy. Others flagged high retail prices (~$0.33/unit), grid planning for intermittency, and the UK’s slow adoption of EVs, heat pumps, and air conditioning—making the decline a Rorschach test for efficiency versus decline.

UK per-capita electricity generation is back to late-1960s levels, down more than a third from its 2003 peak, while global per-capita generation has grown by over 50% this century; net imports rose from 1% to 9% of demand.

Key Highlights
  • UK per-capita electricity generation peaked in 2003 and has fallen by more than a third, returning to late-1960s levels.
  • Net electricity imports rose from 1% of demand in 2010 to 9% in 2025, so part of the decline is offshored generation rather than avoided demand.
  • IEA data shows industrial electricity use fell 25% since 2005, but residential and commercial use fell by a similar amount, making efficiency as important as deindustrialization.
  • HN commenters attribute much of the drop to LED lighting, appliance standards, improved insulation, gas central heating, and streetlight retrofits.
  • The thread debates whether energy consumption tracks economic capacity, with rebuttals that service industries like ARM can raise GDP while using less electricity.

Recreational Programming
⬆ 236 pts 💬 95 HN comments

Christopher Phiri's "Recreational Programming" is a reveal.js slide deck arguing that code written for the joy of writing it—decoupled from compensation, career strategy, or external requirements—constitutes a distinct and defensible mode of intellectual practice. Phiri taxonomizes five practitioner archetypes: the Live Explorer (Tsoding, ThePrimeagen streaming from scratch), the Deep Diver (Casey Muratori's Handmade Hero, Dan Luu's benchmark investigations), the Creative Technologist, the Problem Solver (Zikani Nyirenda's "what small problem can I solve today?"), and the Language Explorer. He then extracts cross-cutting patterns: permission to build "useless" things, following energy rather than plans, documenting process over product, and cultivating beginner's mind. The framing is fundamentally a defense of intrinsic motivation against extrinsic pressure, with aphorisms like "boredom is data, not defeat" and "ROI doesn't apply to recreational programming" operating as design principles. The argument inherits directly from the amateur-radio and hobbyist-microcomputer traditions that predate software-as-industry.

The Hacker News thread (236 points, 95 comments) splits along two axes. The first is nostalgia: mrandish recounts a barely-affordable 4K Radio Shack 8-bit machine wired to a TV in 1981 with only the box manual as documentation; thwarted describes his mother reading BASIC listings aloud from magazines while he typed them into a Commodore 64; bitwize reports a "Proustian 'eating the madeleine' moment" booting a MiSTer into TI-99/4A mode. The second axis is AI anxiety: Hydraulix989 laments "AI killed it all," while tjansen confesses AI "taught me that I don't really like programming per se... I want to build stuff." emptybits counters that if LLM prowess killed your hobby, "you may not have been intrinsically motivated in the first place." Secondary threads critique the deck's navigation ("the world's least intuitive HyperCard stack"), debate recreational programming as a hiring signal, and GMoromisato's analogy separating fun from fulfillment.

"The only rule: Stop if it stops being fun." — Phiri's closing constraint, which became the fault line for a thread split between nostalgic hobbyists and developers mourning an AI-displaced craft.

Key Highlights
  • Phiri defines recreational programming by internal motivation versus external pressure, explicitly excluding side hustles, interview grinding, and tutorial-following.

Show HN: How random are you?
⬆ 169 pts 💬 87 HN comments

Show HN: How random are you? is an interactive web experiment by Shaun Pedicini that asks users to generate random digits while a 'house' predictor tries to anticipate each choice. It revives the Aaronson oracle: a program that predicts f/d keypresses about 70% of the time because humans produce too many alternations, avoid repeats, and leak patterns through reaction time. The app builds on-the-fly n-gram Markov models of the user's numerical sequences, supplemented by simpler heuristic predictors, and accepts both typed and spoken input. The deeper technical foundation is sequential prediction under adversarial feedback: the human is not drawing from a fixed uniform distribution, but from a policy shaped by memory, attention, and the visible behavior of the predictor. Unlike a one-shot psychology study, the web app gives immediate feedback, so users can adopt adversarial strategies, making the measurement both a test of randomness and a game-theoretic interaction. That makes the experiment a compact study in human randomness as a cognitive and strategic process.

Commenters immediately benchmarked the toy against known results. costco quotes Aaronson's Berkeley experiment; kykat reports the house calling 10 of 87 numbers (11%) versus a blind-guessing average of 9.7, placing them among the top five of 206 psychology-study participants. Several exploited deterministic constants: shric uses pi to 100 decimals, patwolf uses Fibonacci digits, and steve_adams_86 feeds Math.random; the house scored 1/10 or 18/190 (9%), below the 11% blind baseline. Others gamed the model adversarially—christophilus lagged the machine, alwa avoided repeated guesses, and malfist repeated warm-up numbers—while skeptics questioned whether users saw predictions, whether 0% is a goal, and how predictions are sealed. The thread shows HN's instinct to reverse-engineer any predictive system into an arms race, but also its demand for statistical validity. The result is less a clean psychometric than a crowdsourced exploration of how prediction models reveal and reshape human choice.

As one commenter quotes from Aaronson, 'It's actually very easy to write a program that will make the right prediction about 70% of the time,' yet the site's blind-guessing baseline is 11%, and users reported scores from 3% to 11%.

Key Highlights
  • A Show HN web toy predicts user-generated random digits using n-gram Markov models and heuristic predictors, reviving the Aaronson oracle effect.
  • HN commenters report house hit rates near or below the 11% blind-guessing baseline, with some scoring 1/32 (3%) and others 19/190.
  • Users easily defeat the predictor by reciting deterministic sequences like pi or Fibonacci digits, which the site treats as surprisingly random.
  • Many adopt adversarial strategies—lagging the machine, changing strategy after successful predictions, or repeating warm-up numbers—highlighting feedback loops in human randomness.
  • Skeptics question the statistical framing, the 0% goal, prediction sealing, and whether voice input could be used for model training.

Same Job, Two Engines: InnoDB and Iceberg Internals
⬆ 35 pts 💬 2 HN comments

Marc Paul's retrospective traces a stock-analysis workload moving from a single OLTP engine to a full lakehouse, using the migration to teach the internals of both. InnoDB is framed through three 'guarantees': row-oriented storage that makes single-bar ingest cheap (fetching one symbol's bar touches one spot on disk), B-tree indexes that trade write amplification for selective reads, and an undo log plus history list implementing MVCC so writers never block readers. The v1 schema leaned on those properties by accident — a 100GB+ FinancialData table with HASH partitioning, an AUTO_INCREMENT past 320 million, and indicator tables forced to join back through FinancialDataID because datetime lived only in the base table, a design flaw only visible at scale.

Version 2 replaces that with Iceberg on MinIO, Parquet under a Polaris catalog, reads via Spark. The payoff is layered pruning: row-group statistics inside Parquet, min/max and partition values in manifests, and partition ranges in manifest lists, all summarized upward into metadata.json — 'statistics stamped at write time, pruned before any data is read, and columnar decodes.' Concretely: bucket(16, symbol) partitioning as one DDL clause, split planning that spans partition versions without re-partitioning, 36-column indicator tables where only the two named columns are read, and a commit path built on immutable files — a MERGE that rewrites matched rows (copy-on-write), appends unmatched ones, then atomically swaps the table pointer in Polaris, with racing writers re-applying on the losing snapshot. April's leftovers: 44 retired metadata.json files never garbage-collected.

Iceberg delivers 'three data filtering layers at significantly different levels of the lakehouse' — manifest lists, manifests, and Parquet row groups — so a 36-column indicator scan touches only the two columns the query names. As the author puts it: 'It only took me about 8,000 words and 9 months.'

Key Highlights
  • InnoDB's MVCC via the undo log/history list gives readers a consistent old-row view without blocking writes; Iceberg achieves the same guarantee structurally, because old versions are immutable old files rather than reconstructed rows.
  • The v1 schema anti-pattern — datetime only in FinancialData, forcing every indicator table to join through FinancialDataID — was undetectable until the table crossed 100GB and repartitioning meant days of work.
  • Iceberg's nesting hierarchy (data files → manifests → manifest lists → metadata.json, tied together by snapshots) is the mechanism behind split planning and multi-level pruning, replacing InnoDB's secondary B-trees with write-time statistics.
  • The commit path is optimistic: writers build new manifests, a manifest list, and a metadata file, then race for an atomic pointer swap in Polaris; the loser re-applies its changes on top of the new snapshot, a direct inversion of v1's advisory locks.
  • Merge-on-read vs copy-on-write is presented as an explicit tradeoff — delete files let streaming writers jot down changes cheaply, but force merge work on every read, while the MERGE path pays up front in rewritten files and snapshot churn.
