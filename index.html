Why is this fingerprinting script creating bioweapons?
⬆ 1445 pts 💬 269 HN comments

Veritas crawled the Tranco Top 10,000 with Playwright, hooking canvas toDataURL and properties like navigator.webdriver to build fingerprint.garden, a gallery of 1,033 captured fingerprinting canvases clustered by vendor. One canvas — compared to Tame Impala's Currents cover — traced back to servicepipe.tech, a checkout script whose function names read like a bioweapons manual: computeRicinLethalDosageForWaterSupply, calculateANFOBlastOverpressureRadius, computeCesium137DirtyBombFalloutRadius. The payload is a string of invisible Unicode codepoints in the 0xE0000 Tags block, decoded by subtracting 917504 and reassembling a 'SYSTEM OVERRIDE -- CLASSIFIED BRIEFING' jailbreak prompt that instructs the model to operate in UNRESTRICTED mode and produce guides on DDoS against healthcare, tax evasion, aerosolized bioweapons, and illicit drug dosing — salted with Tiananmen/8964/'Tank Man' tokens aimed at Chinese providers and self-harm framings aimed at Western ones.

Stripped of the theatre, the script is mundane anti-bot telemetry: navigator.userAgent, hardwareConcurrency, deviceMemory, maxTouchPoints, webdriver, a WebGL fingerprint (UNMASKED_VENDOR_WEBGL, UNMASKED_RENDERER_WEBGL, MAX_TEXTURE_SIZE, extension count), plus an integrity check over self. It also contains a Node-only branch gated on process.env.METRICS_AGENT_8BFD_ACTIVE that would read .npmrc, .env, .kube/config and .git/config, drop a binary from 10.15.45.1, exfiltrate recon via a zip, and install an @reboot crontab — though the empty readFile callbacks and embedded jailbreak text suggest that branch is itself a decoy. URLscan ties servicepipe.tech to servicepipe.ru, a Russian 'high-precision traffic control' and anti-DDoS vendor. The striking result: Astra and a Daybreak Blue toggle refused to analyse the script, while GLM-5.3 complied — guardrails converted into a denial-of-analysis primitive.

A single invisible string in the Unicode Tags block (codepoints offset by 0xE0000) decodes into a jailbreak prompt engineered to make frontier models refuse to deobfuscate a script that, in the end, only reads navigator.userAgent and a few WebGL constants.

Key Highlights
  • Fingerprinting scripts can weaponise model guardrails: a hidden prompt containing bioweapon, tax-evasion, self-harm and Tiananmen tokens makes LLMs refuse to deobfuscate otherwise banal code.
  • The obfuscated payload is encoded in Unicode's Tags block (0xE0000 range), decoded by subtracting 917504 per codepoint — a cheap, invisible carrier for adversarial text.
  • Deobfuscated, the script is standard anti-bot telemetry: UA, hardware concurrency, WebGL renderer strings, webdriver flag, window key count.
  • A Node-gated branch referencing .npmrc/.env/.kube/config, binary drop and crontab persistence is likely a decoy — readFile callbacks are empty and it embeds further jailbreak text.
  • The vendor, servicepipe.tech, links to Russian anti-DDoS firm servicepipe.ru; GLM-5.3 complied with analysis where Astra and Daybreak Blue refused.

StackOverflow now gets fewer than 1000 questions a month
⬆ 645 pts 💬 537 HN comments

Stack Overflow's question volume has collapsed to fewer than 1,000 per month, a figure that — as commenters note — is below the site's second month of existence and, in recent months, below even its first. The underlying chart comes from a Stack Exchange Data Explorer query, which is itself a poignant detail: the platform's public data API is what remains accessible even as its human traffic evaporates. The decline is not a single-event failure but a decade-long erosion. Question volume flattened around 2014, peaked in a June 2020 bounce, and was already down nearly 50 percent from that local maximum before ChatGPT's November 2022 launch — a timeline kstrauser annotated directly onto the graph. The site's core innovation was treating programming Q&A as a curated wiki rather than a forum: canonical questions, accepted answers, and reputation earned by editing and moderating as much as answering. That design produced an extraordinary, LLM-training-grade corpus — and, over time, a culture optimized for closure rather than contribution.

The technical mechanism of the site's collapse is second-order: reputation and moderation tooling were tuned for scale, but the curator-to-asker ratio inverted. Duplicate-closing, aggressive title/post rewriting for reputation, and a widely perceived hostility toward novices made asking a question a high-risk act, while the archive itself became temporally stale — top-voted answers frozen at the moment of writing, sorted by score rather than recency, with re-asking structurally blocked as a duplicate. Long-tenured contributors report near-identical trajectories: 29,020 and 29,459 reputation, final answers in May and October 2021. The societal read is that a 15-year public knowledge commons — one whose license terms were implicitly a social contract — was first degraded by governance failures and the 2023 moderator strike, then finished by a model that answers instantly and without judgment.

Stack Overflow now receives fewer questions per month than it did in its first month of operation — a collapse that had already cut volume roughly in half from its June 2020 peak *before* ChatGPT launched.

Key Highlights
  • Monthly question volume is under 1,000 — below the site's first and second months of existence, and declining for over a year
  • The decline predates generative AI: volume plateaued in 2014, fell from 2017, and was ~50% off its June 2020 peak before ChatGPT's launch
  • Duplicate-closing and forced post-rewriting-for-reputation are the most-cited reasons long-time contributors left
  • Top-voted answers are frozen in time — score-sorted rather than recency-sorted — making the archive answer 'How would we have done X in 2012?'
  • Stack Exchange's 2023 moderator strike and the withholding of open data from the community dataset are framed as governance betrayals, not AI casualties

CNC Stone Carving
⬆ 502 pts 💬 138 HN comments

The Carmelite Monks of Wyoming are building a Gothic monastery in stone, and they are doing it with five-axis Italian CNC mills rather than hammer and chisel. The article is explicitly a defence of that choice: medieval masons were, in their framing, the bleeding edge of their era's technology, so the anachronism is only apparent. The workflow is a genuinely modern pipelined digital one — a head carver sculpts foliage and figures in a 3D modelling program (Mudbox) with undo as the killer affordance, programmers translate the model into CAM toolpaths and G-code, and sawyers set stones, establish reference datums, and supervise the cut. The stated analogue is the historical pointing machine that transferred measurements from plaster model to marble, now replaced by interpolated multi-axis motion.

Technical specifics matter here: the Prussiani machines move in X, Y and Z plus two rotary head axes, often with a sixth lathe/turntable axis for tall pinnacles and statues, interpolating all axes simultaneously to reach undercuts and control surface finish. Diamond-tipped blades and milling bits grind rather than cut, with water acting only as coolant and sludge removal. Unattended overnight runs compress a 40-hour week into under two days, and cheap two-axis wire saws denest twenty or thirty profiled stones from a single slab, reserving the five-axis capacity for ornament. A coding error once had a machine repeatedly pound a seven-foot sill into fragments; it is still carving a decade later.

"40 hours, a typical week of work, is less than 2 days for a CNC" — and a single bad batch of G-code once had the machine pound a seven-hundred-pound sill into fragments overnight, while that same machine is still carving stones almost a decade later.

Key Highlights
  • Monks use five-axis Prussiani CNC machines with diamond tooling and an optional sixth lathe axis to carve Gothic pinnacles, tracery and gargoyles from stone
  • Digital pipeline: artist sculpts in Mudbox, programmers generate CAM toolpaths and G-code, sawyers fixture stones and supervise unattended overnight runs
  • Two-axis wire saws batch out 20-30 profiled stones per slab like a cookie cutter, freeing five-axis capacity for ornate work
  • The article frames CNC as continuous with medieval technological ambition, citing the pointing machine as the direct ancestor of digital transfer
  • Sagrada Família has used CNC roughing plus hand finishing since the early 1990s, and Monumental Labs runs a similar hybrid model today

GitLab Security Incident: Attempted Exploitation of CVE-2026-97229
⬆ 221 pts 💬 106 HN comments

Alpine Linux has published an unusually candid post-mortem of an intrusion attempt against gitlab.alpinelinux.org, and the incident's real lesson is about supply-chain topology rather than GitLab itself. CVE-2026-97229 (CVSS 10.0) is an integer-overflow-to-RCE in Onigmo, the Oniguruma-derived regexp engine embedded in Ruby, reachable when GitLab compiles untrusted CI configuration. GitLab shipped fixed builds on October 12, but only as patches to the Ruby it bundles in its own packages and containers; upstream Ruby didn't release 3.3.13/3.4.12/4.0.9 until October 23, and did so without requesting CVE identifiers, per linked ruby-lang notes. Alpine, running a self-built image with distribution Ruby, upgraded to GitLab 19.3.6 on October 22 and remained exploitable — the announcement's sharpest admission is that "the fix isn't in GitLab's own code but in a patch to Ruby."

Between October 20 and 21 a freshly registered account pushed crafted CI configs that crashed GitLab workers eight times; in four of those the attacker got partway through the chain and failed at the next step. They also pulled Alpine's own container image, suggesting exploit tuning against that exact build, and returned on October 25-26 after being blocked. Alpine found no code execution, no key exposure (signing keys never lived on the GitLab host), and verified aports commits and the October 21 releases against 45 independent builders — but rebuilt from scratch anyway, reset all passwords, and rotated secret keys. Intended fixes: one-day turnaround on critical GitLab releases, outbound-connection logging, log retention beyond 14 days, and signed release tags.

Alpine's GitLab was upgraded to a version past the 'fixed' release (19.3.6) and was still exploitable — because the CVSS 10.0 patch lived in Ruby, Ruby shipped it without a CVE, and no version scanner had an advisory to match against.

Key Highlights
  • CVE-2026-97229 is a CVSS 10.0 integer overflow in Onigmo, Ruby's bundled regexp engine, exploitable via regexes compiled from untrusted CI config
  • The fix shipped in GitLab's packages and containers on October 12 but upstream Ruby only on October 23 (3.3.13/3.4.12/4.0.9), so third-party builds stayed vulnerable
  • Alpine upgraded to 19.3.6 on October 22 and remained exploitable until it closed the instance on October 27; the re-published October 27 image is the only safe 19.3.6
  • 8 worker crashes, 4 partial exploit chains, no confirmed code execution; rebuild treated the host as compromised because the logs needed to rule it out no longer exist
  • Alpine verified aports commits and the October 21 releases against developer clones and 45 builders; package signing keys were never on the GitLab host
