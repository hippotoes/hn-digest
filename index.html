MAI-Code-1-Flash
⬆ 492 pts 💬 221 HN comments

Microsoft announced MAI-Code-1-Flash, a 137B total parameter coding model with 5B active parameters, designed for agentic coding in developer workflows via GitHub Copilot and VS Code. The model is trained on clean, appropriately licensed data and incorporates adaptive thinking to adjust response length based on task complexity, achieving up to 60% fewer tokens on hard problems. Microsoft benchmarks the model against Anthropic's Claude Haiku 4.5, claiming superior performance on SWE-Bench Verified, SWE-Bench Pro, SWE-Bench Multilingual, and Terminal Bench 2, with a +16 point lead on SWE-Bench Pro (51.2% vs 35.2%). The model also outperforms Haiku on reasoning, instruction-following, and adversarial benchmarks. However, the choice of Haiku as a baseline has drawn criticism, as many developers consider Haiku outdated and less capable for serious coding tasks compared to models like Qwen or GPT-5.4 mini.

Some developers highlight that smaller models like Qwen3.6-35B-A3B achieve comparable SWE-bench scores at lower cost, raising questions about Microsoft's competitive position. Meanwhile, others see potential in using MAI-Code-1-Flash as a low-cost agentic model within their toolchains, especially given its integration into the GitHub Copilot harness. The broader context includes Microsoft's release of seven MAI models and the shift toward model efficiency over raw benchmark maximization.

"MAI-Code-1-Flash solves harder problems with up to 60% fewer tokens while outperforming Claude Haiku 4.5 on every coding benchmark tested."

AI outperforms law professors in Stanford Law study
⬆ 299 pts 💬 248 HN comments

A Stanford Law School study led by Professor Julian Nyarko has found that law professors consistently prefer AI-generated answers to student questions over those written by their peers, in a blind evaluation of nearly 3,000 comparisons. The study, titled “Law Professors Prefer AI Over Peer Answers,” tested large language models on contract law questions requiring nuanced reasoning, not just factual recall. Researchers took extensive precautions, calibrating AI responses to match human length and structure, and using multiple evaluation methods. The AI systems performed comparably to the best human instructor, with AI answers winning 75% of head-to-head matchups. This challenges assumptions about AI’s role in legal education, especially in fields demanding judgment and ambiguity navigation.

Perhaps the most striking result: professors flagged AI responses as pedagogically harmful only 3.5% of the time, versus 12% for peer-written answers. Co-author Sarath Sanga noted, 'Two opposing arguments can both be good. What we wanted to know is whether AI can meet the latent professional standard that lawyers use to evaluate each other’s arguments. In this case, the answer was yes.' The findings arrive as law schools grapple with integrating AI, balancing potential benefits against risks like hallucinations and overreliance. Nyarko cautioned against wholesale adoption but argued that blanket skepticism is unwarranted, shifting the conversation to responsible deployment for student benefit.

Professors flagged AI responses as pedagogically harmful only 3.5% of the time, compared to 12% for peer-written answers—a dramatic gap that underscores AI's potential as a tutoring aid.

#10
How we index images for RAG
⬆ 163 pts 💬 23 HN comments

Kapa.ai, a builder of AI assistants for technical documentation, tackles the challenge of incorporating images into RAG pipelines. The naive approach—retrieving images and feeding them to a vision model at query time—breaks down economically and structurally: it adds 27-51% per-query cost, hits payload limits around 30 images (Claude) or 50 (OpenAI), and multimodal embeddings like CLIP wash out fine details critical for charts and tables. Instead, Kapa inverts the cost model by processing images once at indexing time with a cheap vision model (e.g., GPT-5.4 mini), storing the resulting captions as separate text chunks. Retrieval then operates entirely in text, adding only 1-6% per-query overhead while measurably improving answer quality (McNemar's test, p < 0.05). This approach aligns with Microsoft's similar findings and mirrors traditional "eager" processing patterns for media.

The indexing pipeline involves three critical components: filtering, captioning, and storage. Heuristics (size, format, aspect ratio) remove obvious junk, then a zero-shot classifier on multimodal embeddings achieves 96.8% F1 on clear-cut images but drops to 59.8% on ambiguous ones—a fundamental limitation the authors accept. Captioning benefits more from surrounding context than model size; small models perform nearly as well as expensive ones. Storing captions as separate chunks outperforms inline inclusion, as it avoids inflating every chunk and only introduces image context when retrieved—the re-ranker promoted captions into top-15 on 51% of queries with only slight ranking changes (Spearman ρ=0.905). End-to-end experiments across three customer projects show images cited in 10-64% of answers versus 0% text-only, with correct image placement 94-99% of the time. The architecture is rolling out in preview.

"We don't send images to the model at query time. We describe each image once, at indexing time, with a cheap vision model, store the descriptions as text, and retrieve them alongside ordinary text chunks."

Key Highlights
  • Kapa.ai indexes images once per ingestion, not per query, using a cheap vision model to generate text captions.
  • Query-time multimodal adds 27-51% cost, hits payload limits, and fails on fine-grained retrieval; pre-indexed captions add only 1-6%.
  • Ambiguous images (e.g., decorative vs. instructional) remain a challenge for the zero-shot classifier (59.8% accuracy).
  • Caption quality depends more on surrounding document context than model size; small models (GPT-5.4 mini) suffice.
  • Separate caption chunks outperform inline storage, reducing per-query cost and allowing selective retrieval only when relevant.

1-Click GitHub Token Stealing via a VSCode Bug
⬆ 500 pts 💬 70 HN comments

This vulnerability allows an attacker to exfiltrate a GitHub OAuth token with full repository access simply by convincing a victim to click a link that opens a Jupyter notebook in github.dev. The attack exploits VSCode's webview sandbox, which uses cross-origin iframes to isolate untrusted content. To enable keyboard shortcuts across frames, VSCode passes keydown events from webviews to the parent window via postMessage. By executing JavaScript inside a Jupyter notebook's webview (via an img onerror handler in a markdown cell), the attacker can simulate key sequences such as Ctrl+Shift+A to accept a prompt to install a recommended extension, then use a custom keybinding contributed by that local workspace extension to install a second extension that bypasses CSP restrictions and publisher trust checks. The final extension steals the OAuth token available to github.dev, which is scoped to all repositories the user can access, not just the current repo. The attack works equally on desktop VSCode if the victim opens a malicious repository.

The author, Ammar Askar, chose full public disclosure after a previous negative experience with Microsoft's Security Response Center (MSRC), which fixed a bug silently without credit. He notes that MSRC continues to mark VSCode vulnerabilities as low severity or ineligible, discouraging responsible disclosure. The advisory was posted after giving GitHub security an hour's heads-up. The vulnerability highlights significant design tensions: github.dev's token is overly permissive compared to Codespaces' per-repo tokens, and VSCode's webview keydown forwarding weakens the sandbox. While VSCode's defense-in-depth—including CSP and DOMPurify—prevents direct script execution in extension pages, the local workspace extension mechanism provides a bypass. Mitigations include clearing github.dev site data and being wary of links to github.dev. The story drew over 500 points and 70 comments, with the community sharply divided between those critical of Microsoft's security posture and those offering mitigation advice or suggesting alternative editors.

"1-Click GitHub Token Stealing: An attacker can steal a GitHub token with read/write access to all your repos by tricking you into clicking a link that opens a Jupyter notebook in github.dev."

Key Highlights
  • Vulnerability in VSCode webview keydown event forwarding allows keystroke simulation from untrusted content.
  • Attack chain: Jupyter notebook JS -> simulate keystrokes -> install a local workspace extension -> install a second extension that exfiltrates the GitHub OAuth token.
  • Github.dev's OAuth token is scoped to all repositories, not per-repo, amplifying the impact.
  • The bypass relies on local workspace extensions, which skip publisher trust checks, and a CSP-policy evasion via custom keybindings.
  • Author chose full disclosure due to poor prior interaction with MSRC, citing silent fixes and lack of credit.

#5
Use your Nvidia GPU's VRAM as swap space on Linux
⬆ 356 pts 💬 94 HN comments

The nbd-vram project repurposes idle NVIDIA GPU VRAM as swap space on Linux, targeting hybrid-graphics laptops where the dedicated GPU's memory sits unused while the system runs on integrated graphics. It uses a small daemon that allocates VRAM via the CUDA driver API and exposes it as a block device through the NBD (Network Block Device) protocol over a Unix socket, which the kernel's built-in nbd driver then uses as a swap device. This approach avoids the need for kernel modules or the NVIDIA P2P API, which is gated on enterprise GPUs. The data path—kernel swap → /dev/nbdX → nbd kernel driver → Unix socket → daemon → cuMemcpy → GPU VRAM—introduces a userspace round-trip, but the author argues this is acceptable because swap workloads are dominated by sporadic, latency-sensitive page faults rather than sustained throughput.

The project includes detailed benchmarks comparing VRAM swap against NVMe swap on an RTX 3070 Laptop. While sequential throughput (2 GiB dd) favors NVMe (2.7/2.9 GB/s write/read vs 1.1/2.3 GB/s VRAM), 4K random IOPS are closer (NVMe ~45k IOPS, VRAM ~28.7k IOPS), and per-operation latency for infrequent 4K reads is dramatically better: VRAM averages 335 µs vs NVMe's 9.05 ms (27× faster). The author notes that NVMe's high latency comes from APST power-state transitions, while VRAM stays consistently fast. The project claims to triple total addressable memory (from 16 GB RAM to ~46 GB including zram and SSD swap) on a 16 GB RAM system. Comments on Hacker News range from enthusiasm for the niche use-case to skepticism about performance overhead, with several pointing to prior art like vramfs and GpuRamDrive.

VRAM swap achieves 27× faster average latency than NVMe for sporadic 4K reads (335 µs vs 9.05 ms), because GPU memory has no power-state transitions.

Key Highlights
  • Uses CUDA APIs and NBD to turn GPU VRAM into a swap device via userspace, avoiding kernel modules and NVIDIA's P2P API restrictions.
  • Most relevant for laptops with soldered RAM and idle discrete GPU VRAM, effectively tripling addressable memory on a 16 GB system.
  • Sequential throughput is 2–3× slower than NVMe, but 4K random IOPS are competitive (~28.7k vs ~45k) and latency for sporadic access is 27× lower.
  • Backpressure and VRAM allocation conflicts with Wayland compositors are a noted risk; the daemon tries to allocate a configurable amount and reduces if needed.
  • Prior implementations (vramfs, GpuRamDrive, nbdkit-vram plugin) exist, often with worse performance due to FUSE or other overhead.
