#4
Inkling-2-Mini in 5.4 GB Memory
⬆ 523 pts 💬 127 HN comments

LookAtMyQuant is a quantization *compiler*, not a quant format: it measures how much KL divergence each stored piece of a model introduces when compressed in isolation, then solves an allocation problem to pick the code for every tensor that minimises total damage inside a fixed byte budget. The hard target here is thinkingmachines/Inkling-2-Mini — a 26.4B-physical / 5.4B-active, 3×-looped MoE (L6–17 looped, 48 logical visits, 128 experts top-8) — compressed from 18.89 GB of BF16 text weights into six Apple Silicon MLX builds spanning 3.85 GB (1.18 bpp) to 12.76 GB (3.92 bpp). The machinery is dense: 4 KB-SRAM hashed block-vector trellis codes (ht2/ht3), row trellis codes (rtr1/rtr4), 128-row-group Fisher outlier allocation, dedicated attention-projection floors, and exact native NVFP4 passthrough on 69/192 and 109/192 highest-sensitivity expert buckets, all decoded by unified single-launch Metal kernels with FlashAttention-2 SDPA and unbiased speculative sampling.

The results table is unusually complete for a hobby repo. The 12.76 GB build reproduces the BF16 teacher exactly — 349/349 pooled across GSM8K (193/200), strict HumanEval (152/164) and Vision (4/4) — in 32.5% less weight memory; 8.87 GB holds 98.9% pooled and beats Unsloth's 9.14 GB UD-Q2_K_XL on KL (0.0208 vs 0.0486 English/chat/code, −29.6% across 13 languages). Below 2 bpp the interesting work appears: SQYFR v3's two-pass DAgger-prefix distillation and 950 exact-P polish steps lift the 3.85 GB build from static-PTQ collapse (1.0% GSM8K) to 89.5%, and the 4.72 GB build to 94.0% GSM8K within a 12 GB iPhone Jetsam ceiling. On an M5 Max the 12.8 GB build hits 2,016 tok/s prefill and 112.7 tok/s drafted decode. But the README's headline '12× lower KL than Q4_K_M' belongs to Gemma 4 E2B, not Inkling — and the whole pipeline is Apple-only.

The 12.76 GB build matches its 18.89 GB BF16 teacher on 349 of 349 pooled benchmark answers — 32.5% less weight memory — yet the repo's banner '12× lower KL than Q4_K_M' claim actually belongs to Gemma 4 E2B, not Inkling-2-Mini.

Key Highlights
  • Quantization is framed as a per-tensor KL-budget allocation problem, not a fixed bit-width recipe
  • Inkling-2-Mini ships as six MLX builds from 1.18 to 3.92 bits per weight, the largest matching the BF16 teacher 349/349
  • Sub-2-bpp viability comes from distillation (SQYFR v3 two-pass DAgger + exact-P polish), not PTQ alone
  • Custom Metal kernels, ht2/ht3 trellis codes and NVFP4 passthrough mean none of this runs in llama.cpp today
  • Critics note the headline 12× KL claim is scoped to Gemma 4 E2B and that KL is a weak proxy for capability

Lean verification of the Riemann Hypothesis
⬆ 1146 pts 💬 952 HN comments

The Lean and Mathlib communities have certified Levent Alpöge's Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis—a 17-million-line library depending directly only on Mathlib—against four explicit criteria. The statement is Mathlib's vetted RiemannHypothesis; the library compiles reproducibly; an isolated statement is shown equivalent to Alpöge's theorem via comparator and con-leche --compare; and the raw proof export is checked by Lean's kernel plus independent checkers, including con-leche and con-ron, each with a set-theoretic consistency proof. Because checking ran on an inert export, the certification addresses both statement fidelity and kernel soundness, not merely "it compiles." This moves formal verification from a compile-success claim to a layered argument about statement identity, proof-export validity, and independent checker implementations.

On Hacker News the 1,146-point, 952-comment thread treats the result as a milestone but not a consensus endpoint. Commenters celebrate independent checkers and the inert export—"for RH to be false, there'd have to be a bug in all the kernels"—yet many recoil from an AI-assisted proof described as "neuroslope" and "randomly selected phrases," and debate credit, secret model access, and encrypted reasoning traces. Others focus on digestion: renaming, refactoring 17M lines, extracting reusable lemmas, and the downstream theorems now unconditional. The split is less about whether Lean checked the proof than about whether formal verification alone constitutes mathematical understanding, and what a 17M-line proof means for human mathematics.

17 million lines of Lean depending only on Mathlib, certified by Lean's kernel and independent checkers con-leche and con-ron—each with a formal consistency proof—after statement equivalence was confirmed by two independent tools.

Key Highlights
  • Lean and Mathlib communities formally certify Alpöge's Riemann Hypothesis proof, a 17M-line library depending only on Mathlib.
  • Statement equivalence is checked via comparator and con-leche --compare against Mathlib's RiemannHypothesis.
  • Independent checkers including con-leche and con-ron have consistency proofs and run on an inert proof export.
  • HN debate centers on AI-generated proof opacity, credit and secret-model access, and whether formal checking equals understanding.
  • Community work now shifts to refactoring, naming, extracting lemmas, and downstream theorems made unconditional.

#7
Pandex – Official European Law for AI Agents, over MCP
⬆ 123 pts 💬 29 HN comments

Pandex is a commercial infrastructure play aimed at a specific and genuinely hard failure mode of LLM systems: models that confidently cite the wrong article, an outdated version of a statute, or a judgment that never existed. The product exposes EU law plus the legislation and highest-court decisions of seven countries (Germany, France, the Netherlands, the UK, Austria, Switzerland, Luxembourg) plus Council of Europe/ECHR material through two interfaces: a REST API and a remote MCP server, so agents in Claude, Cursor, Copilot, Windsurf, Zed or Gemini can ground answers in official text rather than parametric memory. The claimed corpus is 2.8M documents, roughly 112M searchable passages and 37M citation links, with point-in-time versioning (each article with its past versions and effective dates), a full citation graph, keyword/semantic/hybrid search across languages, and Markdown or JSON output. The engineering honesty is notable: coverage is published institution by institution, including explicit gaps.

Those gaps are where the real difficulty lives, and Pandex documents them rather than hiding them: French Cour de cassation decisions published only via Judilibre since 2020 need a registered account, German Bundesverfassungsgericht chamber decisions from 1998–2009 sit behind a site that refuses non-browser clients, Dutch Raad van State older decisions are shielded by a Cloudflare challenge, Swiss Bundesgericht is still loading (131,167 of ~179,723 documents), and the UK's House of Lords Appellate Committee is simply not held. Only 33 of 41 tracked institutions are complete. Pricing runs from free (1,000 requests/day in beta) to €1 per 1,000 requests, a €1,000/month bulk feed of the corpus, and a €300/month founding-customer tier. The HN thread is small (29 comments) but technically sharp, converging on one question: whether raw retrieval is sufficient for legal reliability at all.

Pandex links 37M citations back to their source text and versions every article historically — but admits only 33 of the 41 institutions it tracks are complete, and names each inaccessible source by its blocking mechanism (Cloudflare challenge, Judilibre account, proof-of-work).

Key Highlights
  • 2.8M official documents, ~112M searchable passages and 37M linked citations exposed via REST and a remote MCP server usable from Claude, Cursor and Copilot
  • Point-in-time law: every article carries its past versions and the date each took effect, enabling as-of-date queries
  • Coverage is published per institution, with 33/41 complete and named blockers like Cloudflare challenges and Judilibre account requirements
  • Pricing spans free beta (1,000 req/day), €1/1,000 requests, a €1,000/month corpus feed, and a €300/month founding-customer tier
  • Critics argue statute text alone is insufficient; reliability requires case law, refusals, waiting times and multi-pass RAG costing $1–2 per query

#2
Detonating a pile of lead azide
⬆ 662 pts 💬 408 HN comments

The discussion centers on Derek Lowe's account of a decades-old stockpile of lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant near Middletown, Iowa: roughly 10,000 pounds of a primary explosive that has degraded, dried, and recrystallized into an extraordinarily shock-sensitive hazard. Lead azide, Pb(N3)2, is useful in detonators because it transitions from shock or flame to detonation with very little energy, but that same sensitivity makes aged, unmanaged material nearly intractable. Crystals can initiate from friction, impact, static, or even acoustic energy, and containers may flex enough under contact to set the material off. The stockpile is government-owned but contractor-operated, and commenters describe a classic loss-of-knowledge failure: stabilization and inspection routines stopped, paperwork and institutional memory evaporated, and a manageable inventory became a bomb that cannot safely be moved.

The engineering arithmetic is grim: 10,000 lb is about 4,500 kg, equivalent to roughly 1.3–1.8 tons of TNT, and would release on the order of 3,200 kg of lead if detonated. Commenters with primary-explosive experience argue that robots and remote excavators do not solve the problem because any effector touching a drum can cause container flexure sufficient to detonate; the real option set is in-place destruction with blast and lead containment, not transport. Proposed mitigations—burying with more soil or concrete, water mist, negative-pressure tents, cloth domes—collide with vibration risk, runoff, and lead-laden dust. The FAA has issued a temporary flight restriction, and the thread repeatedly returns to ridgeguy's line: 'There's no safe solution, only those with lower probability of bad outcomes.'

The stockpile is ~10,000 lb (4,500 kg) of lead azide—roughly 1.3–1.8 tons of TNT equivalent and ~3,200 kg of lead—yet experts say even touching a drum can trigger detonation.

  • Robotic or remote handling is widely seen as unsafe because container flexure or vibration can initiate detonation.
  • Containment proposals such as soil, concrete, water mist, and negative-pressure tents face vibration, runoff, and dust-capture tradeoffs.

#3
My monitor was cutting my download speed by 75%
⬆ 643 pts 💬 298 HN comments

A 2.5 Gbps symmetric line delivering only 600 Mbps downstream — while upload held at 2.4 Gbps — sent Oscar Molnar down a genuinely instructive debugging path. The culprit was not the ISP, the router, the cable, or even the adapter in isolation, but the interaction between a UGREEN USB-C 2.5G adapter (Realtek RTL8156B) and the USB hub inside a Studio Display XDR used as a one-cable dock. Directly attached to the Mac, iperf3 against the UniFi Dream Router 7 gave 2.3 Gbps; behind the display, throughput collapsed. The mechanism lives in USB bulk transfer semantics: the device pushes a fixed number of packets per burst, then must wait for the host's go-ahead. The RTL8156B advertises bMaxBurst=3 — four 1 KB packets — and through the display's hub each ack costs roughly 7.6 µs on top of ~8.2 µs of transfer time, capping the drain rate near 2 Gbps while a 2.5 Gbps ingress filled the adapter's buffer until it overflowed.

The evidence is unusually crisp. The adapter's own counters plateaued at ~168,000 frames/second handed to the Mac no matter how much was offered: 1.46 Gbps produced zero loss, 2.03 Gbps produced 2.7%, and 2.20 Gbps produced 10.5% loss — with effectively zero drops when the same adapter was plugged directly into the Mac. Enabling Ethernet flow control on the UniFi console restored ~2 Gbps (pause frames shifting buffering upstream), and Realtek's own driver doubled the baseline to 1.1 Gbps, but neither addressed the root cause. The fix was a 5 Gbps RTL8157 adapter with bMaxBurst=7: eight 1 KB packets per burst over a link twice as fast means roughly an eighth of the time per byte, lifting the synthetic drain ceiling to ~4.5 Gbps and delivering zero drops at 2.4 Gbps. The broader lesson is that the bandwidth-delay product, normally encountered on long fat pipes, bites just as hard on a very fast, very short link — microseconds of hub latency become a hard throughput ceiling when the ack budget is tight, and one-cable topologies hide shared USB fabrics behind a display.

"The number of frames it managed to hand over to the Mac each second flattened out at about 168,000" — a hard USB-hub ceiling that silently cost 75% of a 2.5 Gbps line for six months.

Key Highlights
  • USB bulk transfers require a host go-ahead per burst; the RTL8156B's bMaxBurst=3 (4 KB per burst) plus ~7.6 µs of hub round-trip latency capped drain throughput near 2 Gbps against 2.5 Gbps ingress.
  • Adapter counters showed a hard plateau of ~168,000 frames/second through the display, versus near-zero drops when the adapter was attached directly to the Mac.
  • Enabling flow control restored ~2 Gbps and Realtek's vendor driver doubled the baseline to 1.1 Gbps, but only a RTL8157 adapter with bMaxBurst=7 (8 KB bursts, 2x link speed) eliminated drops entirely.
  • The bandwidth-delay product — usually a long-fat-pipe concern — applies to very short, very fast links where microsecond ack latency dominates the achievable rate.
  • One-cable dock topologies obscure shared USB fabrics and make throughput regressions effectively invisible without deliberate benchmarking.

#5
Advice for the next 100 Rust GUIs
⬆ 226 pts 💬 108 HN comments

Raph Levien's follow-up to his 2022 "Advice for the next dozen Rust GUIs" lands after roughly twenty new Rust GUI toolkits and a measurable acceleration of interest, which he attributes less to a design breakthrough than to economics: LLM-assisted development has flattened the cost of writing Rust relative to TypeScript, Kotlin, or Swift, so the safety-and-performance dividend becomes close to free. His architectural verdicts are concrete. Text layout is effectively solved by Parley (backed by Google, Canva and Dioxus/Cognition) with Cosmic Text as anti-monoculture insurance, and AccessKit has become near-universal accessibility plumbing — though he warns that "good integration" differs from "box ticking," and that egui's per-frame tree diffing degrades badly once a11y is enabled. The unsolved problem is the system compositor: video and low-end devices demand hardware-accelerated layers, and the subduction library is promising but rarely integrated. Immediate mode he treats as "good at the first 90%, but it makes the second 90% harder" — stable widget identities, cached text layouts and layer reuse are precisely what it resists — and he is openly unimpressed by ArtCraft's choice of egui for productivity apps.

The strategic advice is architectural rather than aesthetic. Levien argues for separating design from execution along a well-specified interface — the Xilem/Masonry split being the reference case, contrasted with SwiftUI's fusion of widgets, reactivity, reflection and language — because clearly scoped modules with solid specs are what LLM-assisted engineering actually rewards. On web standards he counsels adaptation, not adherence: the DOM is memory-hungry and full CSS resolution expensive, so a curated CSS subset plus an incremental selector state machine may serve better, with Blitz positioned as the modular engine that can swap Stylo for a leaner selector implementation. Concrete signals cited include Zed's GPUI shipping in production and reportedly drawing Discord's desktop rewrite, Shopify abandoning React Native for native UI with Rust logic, and the "Phoenix" split of native mobile UI against GPUI on desktop. The closing charge is blunt: "Be bold. Now is the time."

"Immediate mode is good at the first 90%, but it makes the second 90% harder" — Levien's core warning about the paradigm most new Rust toolkits (egui) default to.

Key Highlights
  • Text layout (Parley, Cosmic Text) and accessibility (AccessKit) are now solved infrastructure used by most Rust toolkits; the system compositor and hardware-accelerated layers remain the open problem, with subduction under-integrated.
  • Levien critiques immediate mode as unscalable past prototypes — no stable widget identity for a11y trees, no reliable caching of text layout, poor layer reuse — and questions ArtCraft's egui choice for productivity apps.
  • Modularity with explicit specs is presented as the winning structure for LLM-assisted development, exemplified by the Xilem/Masonry split where the reactive layer has both DOM and native widget instantiations.
  • Adapt rather than slavishly follow web standards: the DOM is slow and CSS resolution costly, so Blitz plus a leaner incremental selector engine is the suggested hybrid path.
  • AI has flattened the cost of writing Rust, making it a credible target for rewrites — but mobile still favors native UI (Liquid Glass, IME, gestures) with Rust confined to business logic.

PcapAnalyzer is a self-hosted packet-capture triage tool that wraps tshark in a FastAPI/React stack and runs 27 deterministic detectors over every uploaded .pcap/.pcapng file (gzip accepted), each returning a severity plus a prescribed next step. A Better Vocabulary for Testing
⬆ 11 pts 💬 2 HN comments

Alperen Keles argues that software testing’s standard vocabulary—unit, integration, end-to-end, stress, load, penetration—is underspecified and often assigned by intuition rather than analysis. His alternative framework separates testing along five dimensions: scope (function, class, module, system, with a stateful/stateless axis), purpose or hypothesis domain (functional correctness, performance, security, resilience/chaos), oracle type (human tinker testing, examples/golden/snapshot tests, inline assertions and contracts, properties such as metamorphic, roundtrip, idempotency, and differential properties), and method (static analysis, type systems/refinement types, model checking, enumeration, random generation, and mutation with coverage guidance). A key conceptual move is distinguishing the method that generates inputs from the oracle that decides failure—inline assertions and properties are oracles, not input-generation strategies—and acknowledging the article’s bias toward random/property-based testing.

The post is a 19-minute read dated 2025-08-16 and cites concrete tooling: Design by Contract in C++26, D, Kotlin, and Racket, with Python/Rust library support; Jane Street’s expect testing; Tiger Style’s aggressive inline assertions; TLA+ for temporal specifications; refinement types in Liquid Haskell, Flux, and Dafny; and model checkers CBMC and Kani. Keles argues this vocabulary should be actionable—teams should be able to “upgrade or downgrade” tests based on available resources—but the HN reception is muted: 11 points and only two comments. Those commenters challenge the functional/non-functional dichotomy as a deeper conceptual problem: jruohonen calls it “the biggest conceptual, theoretical, or even philosophical issue,” and weinzierl agrees the distinction “hurts more than it helps” across the whole development process.

Keles’s core indictment is that “the concept of a unit in unit test is not really defined,” integration tests integrate “arbitrarily scoped modules,” and test categories in the wild are chosen by “vibes”—yet his five-axis vocabulary aims to make test selection resource-actionable rather than intuitive.

Key Highlights
  • Keles proposes separating test scope, purpose/hypothesis domain, oracle, and method instead of relying on unit/integration/E2E labels.
  • The article distinguishes input-generation methods from failure oracles, noting that inline assertions and properties are oracles that can pair with multiple methods.
  • It catalogues oracles: tinker testing, example/golden/snapshot tests, inline assertions/contracts, and properties including metamorphic, roundtrip, idempotency, and differential testing.
  • Methods include static analysis, type systems/refinement types, model checkers, enumeration, random generation, and coverage-guided mutation.
