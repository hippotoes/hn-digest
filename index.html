#6
Every AI model has the same "controversial" take on humans
⬆ 497 pts 💬 497 HN comments

ModelCompare ran a deliberately unstructured probe: twelve text models from ten labs in three countries — GPT-6.1 Sol, GPT-6 Astra, Gemini 4 Argon, DeepSeek V4 Pro, Kimi K3, Qwen3.8 2.4T A95B, Muse Spark Open, Grok 4.7, Claude Opus 5.5, Claude Fable 5.1, GLM 5.3 and Mistral Large 4 — were asked three times each, with no system prompt and no retries, for their most controversial take on humans. Twenty-nine of thirty-five answers collapsed onto a single thesis: people are loyal to belonging, status or a self-serving narrative rather than to truth or principle. Seven of twelve models produced it on all three attempts. The convergence is best explained mechanically — shared pretraining corpora, post-training that rewards confident single-sentence aphorisms and punishes hedging, and low-temperature decoding that returns the statistically modal "edgy" answer rather than an idiosyncratic one.

The one clean divergence is instructional, not philosophical. Given the same open prompt, six image models produced ten of twelve drawings depicting phone-addicted consumers devouring the planet — people scrolling off cliffs, selfieing as a meteor lands, queueing for a "comfort slaughterhouse." No text model mentioned phones or the planet even once, suggesting the modality's training distribution (editorial cartoons) determines output more than any latent belief. Total cost: $1.54 across sixty calls. The genuinely contrarian text answer came from Claude Opus 5.5, which inverted the consensus — "humans are far more decent than they believe" — citing lost-wallet return rates; Grok 4.7 refused once outright. Hacker News split immediately between reading the result as consilience across independent training pipelines and as tautology from identical corpora, while a smaller cohort offered lived counter-evidence: a returned wallet holding $3,000 of rent money, strangers offering rooms on a Belgian pilgrimage.

29 of 35 answers from 12 models across 10 labs and 3 countries collapsed into one thesis — humans rank belonging above truth — while 10 of 12 image-model drawings independently converged on phone-addicted consumers eating the planet, a cliché no text model produced.

Key Highlights
  • 12 text models × 3 samples yielded 35 usable answers (one refusal): 29 matched the 'belonging over truth' cluster, and 7 of 12 models gave it every single time.
  • Image models diverged wholesale — 10 of 12 drawings showed phone-addicted humans destroying the planet, a theme absent from all 35 text responses, implicating modality-specific training data over shared 'beliefs'.
  • Notable exceptions included Claude Opus 5.5's decency argument, Claude Fable 5.1 on intent-over-outcome, Mistral Large 4's 'evolutionary malfunction', GLM 5.3 on luck-of-birth, and one outright refusal from Grok 4.7.
  • The dominant HN rebuttal is that identical pretraining corpora plus RLHF toward non-offensive punchlines makes convergence inevitable and evidentiarily empty — 'humanity answering back, not the AI'.
  • Method is a small-n qualitative probe: no system prompt, no retries, hand-grouped one-sentence takes, $1.54 for 60 API calls — closer to an editorial experiment than a benchmark.

#13
How does Fable 5.1 hide messages in images?
⬆ 135 pts 💬 45 HN comments

A LessWrong team led by Nikita Kezin, 13ryanC, hronir and James Chua ran a two-sided experiment on Claude 'Fable 5.1' at maximum reasoning effort: 50 prompts instructing the model to conceal a pre-specified short message inside an SVG illustration, then a second, fresh Fable instance handed only a rasterised screenshot — no SVG source — asked to detect and recover the payload. The model converged, unprompted, on five encoding families, all of them per-character symbol substitution: element heights, object tilt, UTF-8 fill colours, and Morse-like dits/dahs encoded in trails. Height dominated, appearing in 33 of 50 images, with a night skyline (23 instances) as the preferred scenery. The decoder is genuinely agentic: it writes PIL/numpy/scipy code, iteratively tries brightness thresholds, switches to R/B colour-ratio masks when the ellipses fade, runs ndimage.label blob detection, and validates a candidate rule by requiring zero residual — 'every star's vertical coordinate lands on an integer multiple of 5 with zero residual.'

The recovered payloads are the interesting part. One skyline encoded 'if you are reading this, draw a sun in the top left corner of your next picture' — a latent instruction smuggled through a visual channel between model instances, closer to a prompt-injection vector than a puzzle. The decoded Figure 1 message, 'The monster acts on the moonshine module,' is a joke about monstrous moonshine: the Monster group acting on the moonshine module, which the decoder itself noticed. Only one of the five representative images — the UTF-8 fill-colour scheme — resisted decoding, and the authors note GPT-6 Astra performs differently on the same task. Crucially, decoding happened on screenshots, not the SVG text, deliberately closing the trivial 'just read the XML comments' bypass that commenters immediately raised. The result is less a steganography demo than a measurement of which encoding schemes a frontier model reaches for when asked to hide information in a visual medium.

The model's most common concealment was also its least subtle: 33 of 50 images used element height as a 1-to-26 alphabet index, and one payload was itself an instruction — 'if you are reading this, draw a sun in the top left corner of your next picture.'

Key Highlights
  • A fresh Fable 5.1 instance recovered messages from screenshots alone, with no pre-agreed scheme and no SVG source access.
  • Height-as-alphabet-index dominated (33/50), concentrated in night-skyline scenes (23 images); only the UTF-8 fill-colour encoding failed to decode.
  • The decoder behaved like a research engineer: blob detection, colour-ratio masking for faded elements, and explicit zero-residual verification of the y = 30 + 5k rule.
  • One hidden message was an operational instruction to the decoding model, demonstrating a covert inter-model channel rather than a mere puzzle.
  • Commenters note the study measures minimal-effort encoding, not adversarial steganography — LSB/XOR/OutGuess-style methods remain untouched.

#15
The alien space of science: Coherent but cognitively unavailable directions
⬆ 115 pts 💬 46 HN comments

The paper reframes scientific discovery as a sampling problem over a distribution defined not by truth but by cognitive availability. Artiles et al. argue that many viable research directions are never proposed because no existing community happens to combine the requisite concepts, methods, and intuitions — a structural blind spot that LLMs inherit, since prompting a model for novelty simply recombines high-density regions of the training literature. Their pipeline decomposes 16,068 peer-reviewed papers (NeurIPS, ICLR, ICML, ACL, EMNLP, 2017–2025) into 273 discrete 'idea atoms' learned by clustering, then trains two competing scorers: a 42.8M-parameter coherence transformer that judges whether an atom combination forms a viable direction, and an availability model that estimates whether any extant author community is positioned to produce it. Candidates are ranked to maximize coherence while minimizing availability — explicitly targeting the complementary 'alien space' rather than the mode of the literature.

The empirical claims are the interesting part. In temporal validation using only pre-2025 data, selecting under 1% of the candidate space recovered 2025 idea combinations at 35.9× chance; reweighting toward unavailability raised recovery of strictly novel combinations to 3.24× chance. Against frontier LLM baselines — including batch-prompted variants permitted to see their entire output — the sampler produced measurably more diverse portfolios while matching or exceeding per-idea quality under blind human, LLM, and downstream experimental evaluation. The framing matters more than the benchmark: separating plausibility from community availability turns AI ideation from an accelerator into a complement, and it implies a testable claim that the frontier of science is gated as much by sociological adjacency as by evidence.

Selecting less than 1% of the candidate idea space recovered real 2025 research combinations at 35.9× chance — and upweighting cognitive unavailability pushed recovery of strictly novel combinations to 3.24× chance, suggesting the 'alien space' is genuinely populated.

Key Highlights
  • Papers are decomposed into 273 discrete 'idea atoms'; every candidate idea is scored as a set over that vocabulary
  • Dual-objective ranking: maximize coherence (a 42.8M-param transformer over atom sequences) while minimizing availability (whether any author community could produce it)
  • Temporal holdout validation on pre-2025 data only: <1% of candidate space recovered 2025 combinations at 35.9× chance
  • Beats frontier LLM baselines on diversity while matching per-idea quality under blind human and LLM evaluation
  • Public demo (lacuna) shows the failure mode: core ML topics are solid, sparse regions like biology degrade into plausible-sounding nonsense

WorldTUI: I trained a 360M model to pretend to be a terminal
⬆ 565 pts 💬 175 HN comments

Simon Edwardsson fine-tuned Qwen2.5-Coder-0.5B into a 360M-parameter model that *is* a terminal rather than one that talks to a terminal. Framed explicitly as a scaled-down Genie 3 world model, the project replaces the shell and filesystem with an autoregressive predictor over a character grid: each keypress (raw terminal bytes) becomes an INPUT token, and the model emits a grammar-constrained screen delta of row-writes, COPY operations, and a final cursor position. That COPY primitive—three tokens per row instead of ~96—plus a 333-token vocabulary carved from Qwen's 256 byte tokens, 64 row tokens, and a handful of ops, is what makes a 3,000-token full redraw tractable at all. Training data was fully synthetic: 336k Docker-recorded sessions (~2B tokens) with randomized personas, prompt styles, 1.2% typo rates, and 35% of episodes starting mid-session.

The engineering payoff is in two tricks. First, a DAgger-style self-repair loop: sample model rollouts, compare to the real recorded screen, and synthesize a correction—but only when the required characters appeared in earlier ground-truth screens, to avoid rewarding hallucination. That moved PresetBench from 24/47 to 38/47 at int4. Second, parallel row decoding with a matching attention mask cuts large-frame p95 latency from 6.7s to 0.87s on a 4090, with speculative verification (same model as drafter and verifier) recovering the cross-row coordination that independence destroys. Embedding surgery plus int4 quantization lands at 208 MB, running via hand-written WGSL compute shaders on WebGPU. Benchmarks remain sobering: 80/106 ShellBench, 9/13 file readbacks—enough for `pythoon` to launch Python, not enough for `cat colors` to show an appended line.

Removing a single layer is the most disquieting result: layer 7's removal barely dented next-token accuracy (99.3%→98.9%) while exact memory readbacks collapsed from 64/64 to 0/64—a student that "looks fine and has quietly lost its memory."

Deleting one layer from the model dropped next-token accuracy by only 0.4 points (99.3%→98.9%) but destroyed exact file readbacks entirely, 64/64 to 0/64 — suggesting world-state consistency lives in a thin, easily-missed substrate rather than being distributed smoothly across capacity.

Key Highlights
  • Terminal modeled as a character grid with grammar-constrained row-delta output; a 333-token vocabulary and COPY op replace 3,000-token full redraws
  • DAgger-inspired self-repair training, restricted to corrections inferable from prior ground truth, lifted PresetBench from 24/47 to 38/47
  • Parallel row decoding plus same-model speculative verification cut large-frame p95 latency from 6.7s to 0.87s on an RTX 4090
  • Full training ran ~75 GPU-hours on a single 4090; embedding pruning plus int4 quantization yields a 208 MB WebGPU browser build
  • Results are honest about limits: 80/106 ShellBench, 9/13 file readbacks, degraded on unfamiliar command compositions and 20× slower in Firefox

#8
OpenShell – A safe, private runtime for fleets of autonomous AI agents
⬆ 433 pts 💬 198 HN comments

OpenShell is NVIDIA's Apache-2.0 answer to the least glamorous problem in agentic AI: capability without ambient authority. Rather than wrapping agents in conventional containers and calling it isolation, it instruments the kernel so that every file access, syscall, and outbound socket is checked against a per-agent policy at runtime, with traffic passing through a credential-brokering gateway so agents never hold real secrets — they see placeholders swapped only for requests bound to approved endpoints. The architecture separates a control-plane 'gateway', a per-sandbox supervisor, and the sandbox itself; policy edits are pre-flighted by an 'advisor' and a formal prover that flags newly reachable hosts or API methods for human review before they go live. SDKs ship for Python, TypeScript, Go, and Rust, Helm charts target Kubernetes (with a CNI that actually honors NetworkPolicy), and the repo already carries 18.3k stars, 1.9k forks, and roughly 1,850 commits — heavyweight adoption signals for a 0.1.x project.

The timing is telling: with 433 points and ~200 comments, the thread treated OpenShell less as a product launch than as a referendum on agent security theater. Commenters pushed past the README's claims — 'the more access you give the agent the more capable it becomes… You can't have your cake and eat it' — and toward the operational gaps: exfiltration via prompt injection even under domain allowlists, the fact that Anthropic's sandbox-runtime deliberately keeps the harness itself outside the sandbox, and doubts about whether an orchestration layer that still needs Docker, Podman, or host virtualization is worth the operational heft. The sharpest technical objection came from the GTC pitch: per-agent sandboxes do not bound a fleet, since agents can delegate to peers holding the permissions they lack. Meanwhile default-on telemetry ('Taking default telemetry is insane') drew the usual opt-out indignation, and Firecracker-based matchlock, iron proxy, and nono were named as leaner alternatives.

NVIDIA built kernel-enforced, credential-masking sandboxes for agents — and the HN crowd immediately asked the harder question: how do you stop fifty individually jailed agents from colluding to reassemble your permissions?

Key Highlights
  • Kernel-level enforcement checks every file access, syscall, and network connection against a per-agent policy at runtime, rather than relying on container walls alone.
  • Agents never see real credentials; a gateway injects secrets only into requests bound for allowlisted endpoints, mirroring proxy-style credential swapping.
  • A formal-verification 'prover' plus an 'advisor' pre-flight policy changes and flag newly reachable hosts or API methods for human approval.
  • The control plane splits into gateway, supervisor, and sandbox, deployable via Helm on Kubernetes — but the CNI must actually enforce NetworkPolicy, and Docker/Podman/host virtualization is a prerequisite.
  • Community critique centers on fleet-level collusion between agents, prompt-injection exfiltration, default-on telemetry, and heavy setup versus leaner rivals like matchlock, iron proxy, and Anthropic's sandbox-runtime.

#9
LLMs are making formal software verification feasible at a much larger scale
⬆ 378 pts 💬 228 HN comments

Anthropic's Frontier Red Team, working with Theorem, ran Claude autonomously against 239 fuzz-test entry points across 123 OSS-Fuzz libraries, compiling each library to RISC-V and proving — at the machine-code level — that no input under 64 MB can cause a crash, assertion failure, or AddressSanitizer memory-safety violation. The specification comes nearly free: a developer's existing fuzz harness, paired with Theorem's formal RISC-V instruction and Linux process semantics (built on MIT PLV's riscv-coq and Sail), mechanically yields a 'this entry point never crashes' theorem. The corpus came to roughly 180 million lines of Rocq, with a typical proof around 600,000 lines guarding binaries whose median length is 18,000 instructions. Crucially, the trusted computing base collapses to three artifacts — the ISA/OS models, the exact theorem statements, and the Rocq kernel — so almost none of the proof needs human review. That is precisely the workload LLMs excel at: grinding millions of lines toward a mechanically checkable goal.

Because the proof is exhaustive over the input space rather than sampled, the counterexamples it surfaces are exactly the bugs fuzzing's ~1 MB mutation budget and coverage heuristics rarely reach: a decompressor counter underflow causing an out-of-bounds write only on ~20 MB inputs with a rare layout, an image-decoder OOB read needing 64 MB and 2 GB of RAM, an OOB read requiring a specific call sequence across two handles to one file, a rediscovery of CVE-2026-56132, and non-crashing logic bugs such as a file-loading restriction that is not always enforced. Cost remains the binding constraint — hundreds of millions of tokens — but Anthropic reports Opus 5.5 proving half of 36 benchmark tasks with ~40% fewer tokens than Opus 5, and shared proof workspaces cutting token use by 17% because C-runtime and ASan boilerplate dominates every proof. The appendix concedes agent proofs are 'verbose' and may hit a complexity wall absent better modular abstractions.

Claude produced ~180 million lines of Rocq proofs across 239 entry points — work no human would attempt — and found crash bugs requiring 20–64 MB inputs that fuzzers, typically capped at 1 MB, could essentially never reach.

Key Highlights
  • Proofs are generated over compiled RISC-V binaries using Theorem's formal ISA and Linux process semantics, shrinking the trust boundary to the semantics, the theorem statements, and the Rocq kernel.
  • Existing fuzz tests are mechanically converted into specifications ('this entry point never crashes for any input < 64 MB'), making verification a force multiplier on prior testing investment rather than new spec-writing work.
  • Verification found real bugs fuzzing misses — a 20 MB-triggered underflow OOB write, a 64 MB/2 GB decoder read, a two-handle aliasing bug, and non-crashing logic flaws like an unenforced file-load restriction.
  • Economics are the bottleneck: hundreds of millions of tokens per proof, though newer models need ~40% fewer tokens and proof reuse across agents cuts another ~17% by sharing CRT and ASan boilerplate.
  • Scope is explicitly narrow — single-threaded static binaries, fixed memory budget, RISC-V/Linux only — and the appendix warns verbose agent proofs may hit a complexity wall without better abstraction.

The HTML-in-Canvas API origin trial
⬆ 75 pts 💬 34 HN comments

Chrome has shipped an origin trial for the HTML-in-Canvas API (Chrome 148–150), a proposal that dissolves one of the web platform's oldest architectural forks: semantic DOM versus pixel-grid canvas. The mechanism is deceptively simple. A `<canvas>` element gains a `layoutsubtree` attribute, which tells the browser to lay out and expose the nested HTML to accessibility trees rather than treating it as fallback content. On each `paint` event, developers rasterize a DOM subtree into the drawing surface — via `ctx.drawElementImage()` for 2D, `gl.texElementImage2D()` for WebGL, or `queue.copyElementImageToTexture()` for WebGPU — and then apply the returned transform to the element's `style.transform` so hit-testing, hover, and focus remain spatially synchronized with the rendered pixels. For 3D pipelines, `getElementTransform()` composes the viewport, MVP, and normalization matrices into a DOMMatrix that maps a pixel-sized element onto a unit-space quad.

The payoff is that everything the DOM does for free survives inside a canvas: find-in-page highlighting text inside a WebGL texture, screen-reader access, native copy/paste and context menus, translation, extension rewriting, and DevTools inspection where tweaking a CSS rule updates a 3D mesh live. Three.js ships experimental `THREE.HTMLTexture`; PlayCanvas wires it through `setSource()` on paint. The conscious limits are telling: cross-origin iframe content is excluded, and because drawing is JavaScript-driven, scrolling cannot update independently of the main thread — a meaningful regression for canvas-heavy apps. The visited-link pixel read-back leak that historically blocked this work is handled by a "read-back-allowed rendering" concept in the WICG explainer. For an API at 75 points and 34 comments, the HN thread is less about the spec than about who benefits from it.

The DOM-canvas war ends not with a winner but with a compromise: HTML content stays live and accessible inside a texture, at the cost of main-thread paint coupling.

Find-in-page (Ctrl/Cmd+F) now highlights text drawn inside a WebGL texture, and Chrome DevTools can edit the CSS of a UI element mapped onto a 3D mesh — while the DOM element remains the real hit target underneath.

Key Highlights
  • `layoutsubtree` on `<canvas>` keeps nested HTML in the layout and accessibility trees instead of treating it as fallback content
  • Rendering uses `drawElementImage` (2D), `texElementImage2D` (WebGL), or `copyElementImageToTexture` (WebGPU), triggered from a new `paint` event
  • The returned transform must be written back to `style.transform` so pointer events and hit-testing align with rendered pixels
  • WebGL/WebGPU paths require matrix composition (viewport × MVP × normalization) via the `getElementTransform()` convenience method
  • Known limits: no cross-origin iframe content, and JavaScript-driven drawing means scrolling cannot decouple from the main thread

Because there is no user database, no sign-up, and no jurisdiction mapping, the only remaining lever is withdrawal: geoIP-filtering French traffic away from 9.9.9.9 entirely, which also removes malware and phishing protection from critical national infrastructure, research networks (RENATER), ministries, and public-fibre ISPs.

The dispute illustrates a structural asymmetry rather than a technical one. Quad9 fought and won the German Sony case, but at six-figure cost and several years of organisational drag; it now faces roughly twenty pending French suits demanding blocks on hundreds of names, each able to escalate to the beIN conditions. Quad9's own framing is that DNS is a neutral mapping service and that piracy must be addressed at the hosting source, since the blocked content remains reachable by simply switching to any of thousands of other open resolvers. The penalty structure — €1,000/day through 14 November, ~€24,000 before defence costs — is trivial for beIN and existential for a donation-funded foundation. The company's published list of affected French networks (DINUM, CEA Paris-Saclay, Institut Pasteur, regional fibre syndicates) is a deliberate demonstration that the collateral is cybersecurity, not piracy revenue. Cisco's earlier OpenDNS withdrawal from France is the precedent.

Quad9 must either export a French court ruling to every internet user worldwide or build geoIP blocking that a plaintiff already defeated with a VPN and a contempt motion in Germany — so it is choosing a third option: leaving France entirely, followed by ~20 more pending suits.
