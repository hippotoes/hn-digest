Keep Our Servers Running
⬆ 996 pts 💬 260 HN comments

The Internet Archive’s September 2026 campaign, “Keep Our Servers Running,” offers one concrete incentive: new recurring donations of $25 or more are 2:1 matched, converting a $25 monthly gift into $75 of first-month impact. The pitch is refreshingly concrete about what money pays for—servers, storage, power, cooling, and the staff who maintain IA’s self-built infrastructure. Rather than renting commercial clouds, IA says it operates its own systems to keep archiving independent of corporate strategy. That independence protects 210 petabytes of publicly accessible knowledge, but it also makes the organization fully responsible for growing operational costs. The story drew 996 points and 260 comments on HN, effectively becoming a referendum on how the internet should sustain its own shared memory infrastructure.

Top comment threads split between generosity and technical unease. Some commenters report donating or increasing monthly amounts—“I’d been donating $5 for a long time and I just bumped it to $25”—and a volunteer asks experienced engineers to help with Open Library’s Solr read replicas, which lag under load. Meanwhile, several users complain that the Wayback Machine has been serving “timeouts, 429 errors, and Temporarily Offline messages for weeks,” and that donation pages load over VPN while ordinary access is throttled. Skeptics also probe the matching mechanism: no donor is named, prompting “Truly stunning that they omitted this information.” A separate thread questions legal exposure after Hachette v. Internet Archive and asks whether legal fees consumed donations meant for preservation. The overall mood is both protective and demanding: preserve the archive, but hold it to the same reliability and transparency standards expected of critical public infrastructure.

The Internet Archive says it is powered by donations averaging about $25; this September, a new recurring $25 gift is matched 2:1 to become $75, while the organization’s publicly accessible collection stands at 210 petabytes.

Key Highlights
  • A 2:1 match means new September recurring donations are tripled: $25 becomes $75, $50 becomes $150, and $100 becomes $300.
  • Internet Archive stores 210 petabytes on self-built systems, funded by donations averaging about $25 per month.
  • Several HN users report aggressive 429 rate limits, VPN blocks, Chrome-specific throttling, and recurring ‘Temporarily Offline’ failures.
  • Volunteers are needed for Open Library infrastructure work, especially Solr performance and read replicas.
  • Community skepticism centers on who is funding the anonymous match, legal fight costs, and whether cash or engineering help matters more.

Watch Los Angeles get built, one building at a time (1880–2026)
⬆ 309 pts 💬 151 HN comments

An interactive map built by 'rustywasm' renders every addressable building in the City and County of Los Angeles as a simple extruded box on a dark 3D map, introducing each structure in the vintage when county assessor records say it was completed. The underlying data pipeline combines parcel boundaries and heights from the LA County Assessor portal with LARIAC building outlines and city open-data datasets; structures animate on from 1880 through 2026. Static frames hide the twist: because a box appears only if the building survives today, demolition, fires, and replacement vanish from history. Early neighborhoods such as Palms and downtown LA appear artificially empty, and commenters note whole canal-era Venice and the 1890s downtown remain invisible. Nevertheless, the map's visceral vertical growth reveals mid-century skyscraper booms and the wave of postwar sprawl in a way floor plans and charts rarely do.

The discussion immediately turned from cartography to LA's built-environment politics. slg points out the city zoned for about 10 million in the 1960s but only 4 million by the 1980s, and several commenters tie that downzoning, plus Proposition 13, to an artificial housing shortage. Others recall the 1,300-mile Pacific Electric Red Car network that once connected Huntington Beach, Burbank, and downtown; a 97-year-old memory of riding to see FDR in 1935 makes the loss concrete. The developer responded by adding county-wide data and fixing a CloudFlare cache misconfiguration after reports of lag. In a broader sense, the tool resembles earlier Mapbox vector-tile visualizations and even the fictional 1940s LA Noire map, and with LA County's population comparable to Sweden's, it raises whether such animations celebrate growth or reveal policy failure.

LA was zoned for roughly 10 million people in the 1960s and by the 1980s it was zoned for roughly 4 million.

Key Highlights
  • Visualizes LA County assessor footprint data: every surviving building appears with a height estimate as the timeline runs 1880–2026.
  • Critically, it excludes demolished or replaced buildings, making older periods look emptier than they actually were.
  • Commenters connect post-1980s downzoning and Prop 13 to artificial housing scarcity and soaring costs.
  • Discussion also covers LA's lost 1,300-mile Red Car rail system and the cultural memory of car-centric sprawl.
  • Author iterated during thread: added full county data, shared data sources, and fixed CloudFlare-caused lag.

Leaving VMware just got harder after Broadcom pulled VDDK downloads
⬆ 218 pts 💬 106 HN comments

Broadcom has quietly removed public downloads of VMware's Virtual Disk Development Kit (VDDK), a proprietary library used by third-party migration and backup tools to read virtual disks from live ESXi hosts. While no official deprecation notice was published, customers report 404s on version-specific URLs for VDDK 8 and 9, and Broadcom support allegedly confirmed the SDK is no longer generally available. This is more than a developer inconvenience: Microsoft Azure Migrate, Red Hat Migration Toolkit for Virtualization, Nutanix Move, Platform9 and many other VMware exit routes depend on VDDK for agentless, changed-block-tracking aware transfers. Microsoft has already revised its Azure Migrate documentation to steer users toward agent-based migration when VDDK cannot be downloaded. Red Hat cannot redistribute proprietary Broadcom code and is asking customers to contact Broadcom support. The move is widely perceived as another lock-in tactic in Broadcom's controversial post-acquisition strategy, coming alongside the supposed revival of vSphere Standard.

The technical nuance is that VDDK is not the only path off VMware. Proxmox's built-in VMware import wizard can connect directly to an ESXi host and copy VMDKs without VDDK, and several HN commenters point to qemu-img for cold disk conversion, agent-based migrations, or even an archive.org torrent of VDDK. But at enterprise scale, with thousands of VMs and minimal-downtime requirements, generic disk-copying tools are impractical; VDDK's change-block tracking and sparseness awareness were what made agentless migration efficient. Ex-VMware engineers see this as predictable Avago-style milking of an acquired cash cow, while others blame IT leadership for ignoring years of lock-in warnings. The incident underscores how a single proprietary software dependency can be weaponised as an exit barrier, and it accelerates interest in open-source alternatives like Proxmox and KVM-based platforms.

VDDK removal has no announced replacement: migration vendors have gone from 'download VDDK, then migrate' to 'ask Broadcom for permission,' with Microsoft and Red Hat already rewiring their user guides around the loss.

Key Highlights
  • Broadcom removed public VDDK downloads with no formal announcement, causing 404s on version-specific URLs for VDDK 8 and 9.
  • VDDK is integral to agentless migration tools from Microsoft, Red Hat, Nutanix, and Platform9, as well as many backup products.
  • Microsoft updated Azure Migrate docs to recommend agent-based migration when VDDK is unavailable; Red Hat cannot redistribute VDDK and tells customers to contact Broadcom.
  • Proxmox's built-in VMware import tool is unaffected, providing a VDDK-independent migration path.
  • Customers and ex-employees view this as intentional exit-path lock-in by Broadcom/Avago, deepening trust erosion among VMware users.

Emacs Bedrock 2.0
⬆ 138 pts 💬 32 HN comments

Emacs Bedrock 2.0 is a small but opinionated configuration starter by Ashton Wiersdorf that strips away the distro model: rather than providing a framework users must learn around, it gives a copyable early-init.el and init.el in ~/.emacs.d and places optional third-party package configs in an extras/ directory. The 2.0 release intentionally breaks with Emacs 29, requiring Emacs 31 from August 2026. Code changes are conservative but meaningful: lexical-binding is enabled across all .el files, use-package now auto-installs declarations it encounters, which-key is superseded by embark-auto-prefix-help-mode when Embark is loaded, and wgrep is dropped for built-in grep-change-to-grep-edit-mode. Configuration also enables repeat-mode, auto-updates *Completions*, and refines tree-sitter settings.

The release embodies a teaching-first ethos. Ashton spells out that no automatic upgrade path exists, because Bedrock code is supposed to be copied and absorbed into each user's init.el; upgrades are meant to be cherry-picked by diffing upstream. One HN commenter, AloysB, was delighted by the wgrep replacement (“TIL! I might be able to remove my wgrep too”) and described the starter kit as “full of gems.” Another thread questioned whether Emacs 31's newcomers-presets theme makes starter kits redundant, while veterans countered with stories of inherited .emacs files, including stevejb's memory of his father saying “my .emacs file is older than you are.” The comment section overall reflects a cyclical pattern: developers tired of VSCode's telemetry and slowing performance consider returning to Emacs' stable core, demonstrating that Bedrock's target audience is not just newcomers but churn-weary veterans.

“The point of Bedrock is that you learn to own your entire config as quickly as possible.”

Key Highlights
  • Requires Emacs 31, breaking support for the still-common Emacs 29.
  • Base init contains no third-party packages; optional configs live in extras/ for piecemeal adoption.
  • Adds lexical-binding: t across all .el files and lets use-package auto-install missing packages by default.
  • Replaces which-key with embark-auto-prefix-help-mode and wgrep with the built-in grep-change-to-grep-edit-mode.
  • Deliberately offers no auto-upgrade path, because Bedrock is meant to be absorbed into each user’s own config.
