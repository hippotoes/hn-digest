Elevators
⬆ 1301 pts 💬 317 HN comments

John's 'Elevators' is a masterclass in applied scheduling, using interactive simulations to explain how lift systems balance the relentless competition between passenger requests and physics. It starts with the 1961 SCAN algorithm, which sweeps the shaft top-to-bottom, and its more practical cousin LOOK, which reverses at the highest requested floor. The real complexity arrives with multiple cars: Otis's RSR algorithm scores each cab using ETA, load penalties, anti-bunching, and idle bonuses, re-evaluating every five seconds. Surprisingly, benchmarks show that at high passenger flow the simple LOOK can outperform RSR, because when cars are full and stopping everywhere, fancy heuristics lose their edge. The most counterintuitive result is that Destination Dispatch kiosks, which know your destination upfront, generally produce worse wait times—locking a rider into a specific car eliminates the system's ability to adapt, and flexibility beats information.

The HN audience embraced the piece with 1301 points and 317 comments, extending the story with real-world war stories and reservations. Several commenters question whether the DD verdict is an artifact of random destinations: in hotels and offices the traffic is heavily directional, so group travel to common floors might reverse the conclusion. Others recount capacity disasters—a 60-floor tower with three saturated elevators, a non-independent two-car residential bank that won't allocate the accessible car when needed, and a furry convention where full-suit wearers consume double space. The comments also gravitate to simulation nostalgia, from SimTower to Elevator Saga, and to human-factor quirks like pressing both arrows or the impossibility of un-pressing a button. A recurring theme: elevator algorithms are only as good as their assumptions about load, traffic patterns, and the irrational humans pressing buttons.

Turns out the loss in flexibility is not worth the extra information for the optimizer.

Key Highlights
  • SCAN/LOOK are the baseline sweep algorithms; LOOK reverses at highest requested floor.
  • Otis RSR scores cars with ETA, load penalties, anti-bunching, and direction bonuses, re-optimizing every 5 seconds.
  • RSR outperforms LOOK at moderate flow but LOOK wins at high flow and small banks.
  • Destination Dispatch kiosks restrict reassignment and generally increase wait times despite extra destination info.
  • HN comments add real-world caveats: directional traffic, load sensing, human mistrust and game pedagogy.

Getting 25 Gbps Thunderbolt Ethernet on My Mac Studio
⬆ 191 pts 💬 95 HN comments

Jeff Geerling's quest to push his Mac Studio past 10GbE led him to an unexpectedly affordable path: a $160 (originally) adapter combining a server-pulled OCP 2 NIC with a Thunderbolt 3 bridge board. This undercuts Sonnet's $999 Twin25G or ATTO's $1,099 ThunderLink, but requires compromises. The OCP NIC is designed for high-pressure server fans, not passive enclosures, so it ran painfully hot and dropped connections. Geerling had to design a custom 3D-printed fan duct, use a Noctua 80mm fan, and solder power from the PCB. Performance maxed at 20-25 Gbps due to Thunderbolt 3's limits, and real-world SMB transfers only reached 1.4 GB/s reads and 1 GB/s writes—marginal over the built-in 10G port, suggesting the NAS's slow Ampere Altra CPU and macOS's SMB stack, not the network, are the limiting factors.

The project highlights a broader gap in the Mac ecosystem: no PCIe slots, so high-speed networking depends on Thunderbolt dongles and docks with huge markups. The HN comment thread reinforces this, with users sharing success with eGPU enclosures and Mellanox ConnectX-4 cards, while others caution that SMB Direct/RDMA support is still missing on macOS, capping practical throughput. The DIY approach saves money but costs engineering time; as one commenter points out, 'engineering time is mucho $$'—for a professional, the Sonnet's plug-and-play reliability may justify its price. Yet Geerling's solution demonstrates that the maker community can bridge the gap, and the resulting thermal and firmware tweaks add to a growing body of knowledge about pushing consumer Macs into enterprise networking territory.

A $200 DIY Thunderbolt-to-25G adapter gets 20-25 Gbps on a Mac Studio, but SMB copies were only marginally faster than built-in 10G—proof that the network often isn't the bottleneck.

Key Highlights
  • Macs lack PCIe, so high-speed networking requires Thunderbolt adapters that are often wildly overpriced.
  • Server-pulled OCP 2 NICs can be paired with Thunderbolt bridge boards for a fraction of the cost, but they need serious active cooling.
  • Thunderbolt 3's chipset caps sustained speeds at ~20-25 Gbps, even on Thunderbolt 5 ports.
  • Real-world SMB throughput barely improved over 10GbE, pointing to NAS CPU and macOS networking stack as the real bottlenecks.
  • The DIY path is cheap but requires engineering effort; commercial adapters offer reliability at a premium.

The most official water costs $120k a gallon
⬆ 188 pts 💬 148 HN comments

The blog post explores why the 'most official water'—Vienna Standard Mean Ocean Water (VSMOW)—commands $120,000 per gallon. Pure H2O is a misleading concept because hydrogen and oxygen have stable isotopes: protium/deuterium and 16O/17O/18O. Natural abundance varies by evaporation and precipitation, so rainwater differs from ocean water. In 1966, the IAEA selected Harmon Craig's Standard Mean Ocean Water as the international isotopic reference, prepared from Pacific water distilled into VSMOW; SLAP from Antarctic snow is the secondary standard. VSMOW's value is metrological: its triple point at 0.01 °C is reproducible to a few millionths of a degree and defined the Kelvin until the 2019 SI redefinition. A 5-mL ampoule costs $159, yielding a per-gallon price that seems absurd but buys traceability for the world's most precise thermometers and isotope-ratio measurements.

HN commenters quickly connected VSMOW to NIST's broader catalog of reference materials: SRM cigarettes for around $204 per carton and peanut butter at $2.44 per gram are not ultra-pure products but carry detailed datasheets for calibrating industrial processes. One commenter noted that all calibrations in his industry trace back to physical NIST standards, calling it 'a bit frightening' how hidden that dependency is. Others questioned why the standard isn't pure ¹H₂¹⁶O, and replies explained the practical difficulty of isotope separation and the benefits of a stable natural-abundance mixture. A temperature-scale subthread debated Celsius versus Kelvin versus Fahrenheit, while many joke responses contrasted VSMOW's price with deuterium water, tritium water, and xenon—underscoring that the cost comes from reproducibility infrastructure rather than intrinsic material scarcity.

VSMOW, the most official water on Earth, costs $120,000 a gallon and anchors the triple-point cell that defined the Kelvin until 2019.

Golang proposal: container/: generic collection types
⬆ 154 pts 💬 126 HN comments

The Go team has opened umbrella proposal #80590 to bring generic collection types into the standard library, the result of a dedicated Collections working group formed in late 2025. The planned packages—container/hash.Map, container/hash.Set, container/set.Set, container/mapset, container/ordered.Map, and container/heap/v2—aim to fill long-standing gaps such as sets and ordered maps while establishing consistent API conventions. The design is deeply informed by generics (since Go 1.18) and iterators (since 1.23), including F-bounded polymorphism to model abstract set and map interfaces despite the binary-method problem. The proposal emphasizes simplicity and delayed commitment to canonical constraint interfaces; unexported _AbstractCollection/_Set/_Map interfaces only document shared behavior for now. Existing language features can express much of this, but the team argues a standard library implementation reduces boilerplate and provides performance guarantees.

Notable choices: mutation methods report whether size changed; Map.Set/Delete return previous value plus boolean; mapset provides helper functions for legacy map[T]bool sets; set.Set is represented as map[T]struct{} and is expected to become the default set. The functional 'Union/Intersection/Difference' methods are paired with '-With' mutating variants, deliberately separate to avoid accidental mutation and forgotten results (math/big.Int lesson). The working group explicitly rejects adding every operation to interfaces—e.g., Subset is omitted because it is typically O(n) and can be generic, while DeleteFunc is retained for tree structures to avoid O(n log n) deletion. The proposal has sparked a mix of relief and eye-rolling in HN comments: many note 'better late than never' and compare Go to Java's Collections (1998) or Smalltalk (1980s), while others appreciate Go's cautious, deliberate design.

By delaying the commitment to a particular set of methods, we can learn from practice. It may turn out that we don't need to publish canonical constraint types at all.

Key Highlights
  • The proposal adds hash.Map, hash.Set, set.Set, mapset, ordered.Map, and heap/v2 to Go stdlib, plus hash/maphash.Hasher.
  • It relies on Go 1.18 generics and 1.23 iterators, using F-bounded polymorphism to express abstract Collection/Set/Map constraints.
  • Design emphasizes consistency and avoiding binary-method pitfalls; operations return rich info (e.g. Map.Set returns previous value).
  • Methods with '-With' variants separate functional vs in-place mutation, drawing on math/big.Int lessons.
  • The proposal is an umbrella for several linked issues targeting Go 1.28, including hash/maphash.Hasher already released in go1.27.

#9
Progressive Web Components
⬆ 153 pts 💬 32 HN comments

Ariel Salminen, a design systems architect with nearly a decade of experience building enterprise-scale component libraries, has released v1.0.0-rc.7 of Elena, a 2.6 kB zero-dependency library for constructing what he calls Progressive Web Components. The core idea is a two-layer architecture: a base layer of native HTML and CSS in the Light DOM that renders immediately with no JavaScript, plus an enhancement layer that adds reactivity, events, and templating. Elena distinguishes three composition styles—Composite (HTML Web Components), Primitive, and Declarative (utilizing Declarative Shadow DOM)—but treats these as a philosophy rather than a forced API. The library directly targets recurring pain points in the web components ecosystem: layout shifts, flash of unstyled content, poor SSR support, and awkward integration with React Server Components and accessibility tooling.

Elena ships as 13 npm packages under @elenajs scope, including core, bundler, CLI, SSR tooling, an MCP server, and example components. Its SSR model is deliberately simple: components without a render() method are fully SSR-compatible, while those with render() offer partial server rendering and client hydration, with @elenajs/ssr available for more advanced cases. The announcement positions Elena for teams building design systems that must work across Angular, React, Vue, or no framework at all. HN commenters met the release with a familiar debate: some call Shadow DOM 'a death by a thousand cuts' and warn web components are 'terrible for just building apps,' while others praise the 'HTML + CSS first' approach as a long-overdue correction. The discussion underscores that Elena's real bet is not new technology, but a return to progressive enhancement.

“No other approach gives you true cross-framework portability built on what the web platform already provides.” — Ariel Salminen, on why web components still anchor Elena.

Key Highlights
  • Elena is a 2.6 kB zero-dependency library for building 'Progressive Web Components' with HTML/CSS-first rendering and optional JS enhancement.
  • Three component styles: Composite, Primitive, and Declarative (Declarative Shadow DOM), all using native custom elements.

Demystifying DRAM Read Disturbance: RowHammer and RowPress Phenomena
⬆ 51 pts 💬 28 HN comments

RowHammer and RowPress are DRAM read-disturbance phenomena in which repeated activations of one row (aggressor) induce bitflips in neighboring rows (victims). This arXiv paper by Haocong Luo and colleagues, including Onur Mutlu, attempts to harmonize two typically disjoint lines of research: experimental characterization of real-chip bitflips and device-level simulations of physical mechanisms. The authors identify concrete mismatches between existing models and observed behavior across three fundamental metrics: bitflip direction, bitflip count, and ACmin—the minimum number of aggressor activations required to trigger the first bitflip. Using rigorous TCAD simulations, they reproduce several experimentally observed phenomena, propose updated error mechanisms, and expose which simulation parameters matter most for matching real-chip behavior. The work contributes a principled foundation for future studies of DRAM read disturbance, while also cautioning that naive TCAD models can mislead if they do not account for process variation, temperature, and other device-level parameters.

The paper's intended audience is researchers and engineers working on memory reliability and security. It has immediate implications for characterization methodologies: measuring only bitflip counts, for example, can miss directionality and timing effects that reveal the underlying physics. The authors also stress that mitigations—such as TRR (Target Row Refresh) or probabilistic refresh schemes—must be evaluated against mechanisms that match real silicon, not idealized models. On Hacker News, the discussion takes a broader view: commenters argue that RAM susceptible to random bitflips is fundamentally defective, share anecdotes of ECC DDR5 catching multiple flips per day, reference that roughly 10% of Firefox crashes are attributed to bitflips, and note that memory-testing tools once added Rowhammer tests only to disable them by default because too many modules failed. This reaction underscores a persistent tension: even rigorous academic progress may not address the industry-wide acceptance of imperfect memory.

The paper pinpoints ACmin—the minimum number of aggressor activations required to trigger the first bitflip—as a first-order metric that exposes the gap between device-level models and real-chip behavior.
