YouTube to automatically label AI-generated videos
⬆ 946 pts 💬 573 HN comments

YouTube announced two major updates to its AI labeling policy, effective May 2026. First, disclosure labels for photorealistic or significantly AI-altered content will be moved to a more prominent location: below the video player for long-form videos and as an overlay for Shorts. Second, YouTube will introduce automatic AI detection using internal signals when creators fail to manually disclose AI use. If detected, a label will be auto-applied, though creators can override this unless the content was made with YouTube's own AI tools (e.g., Veo, Dream Screen) or carries C2PA metadata. The policy aims to balance transparency with creator control, and crucially, the labels do not affect recommendations or monetization. This builds on mandatory disclosure introduced in 2024, reflecting evolving community expectations around generative AI.

The move addresses growing concerns about photorealistic AI-generated content masquerading as real—such as fake life-advice videos or misleading news clips—that have proliferated on the platform. Commenters on Hacker News highlighted specific pain points: a friend sending an AI-generated history video they believed was authentic (aorth), and family members forwarding photorealistic AI advice videos (goshx). However, many engineers remain skeptical of automatic detection, recalling the failure of text-based AI detectors like ZeroGPT (nickvec) and warning of false positives that could unfairly penalize creators (GodelNumbering). The policy also omits some ambiguous cases like occasional AI b-roll or AI-generated music, which commenters like ArcaneMoose and ellrob88 argue need clearer boundaries. Overall, the update reflects a pragmatic step—prioritizing prominent labeling over perfect detection—while leaving room for iterative improvement.

"Starting in May 2026, YouTube will automatically label AI-generated content if not disclosed, using internal signals—but creators can override, except for content made with YouTube's own AI tools or containing C2PA metadata."

Key Highlights
  • Labels move to more prominent positions: below player for long-form, overlay for Shorts.
  • Automatic detection triggered when creators leave AI use undisclosed; override allowed unless content uses YouTube AI tools or carries C2PA metadata.
  • Labels do not impact recommendations or monetization.
  • Applies to photorealistic and meaningfully altered content; unrealistic or slightly altered content gets label in expanded description.
  • Policy reflects feedback since 2024 mandatory disclosure, aiming to balance transparency with creator control.

I analysed 20 years of my chats
⬆ 156 pts 💬 55 HN comments

The author undertook a massive personal data project: analyzing 1.2 million messages spanning 20 years across platforms like Telegram, Instagram, VK, and Facebook. Using LLMs (Opus and local Qwen3-30B) costing ~$15k in API fees, they parsed the data into structured Obsidian notes. A custom prompt chain filtered noise (41% of messages were fillers or media) and classified life events with <1% false positives. To handle the "Sasha problem"—multiple contacts with the same nickname—the LLM resolved entity ambiguities. Sentiment analysis went beyond VADER by measuring directional sentiment (asymmetric warmth) per conversation-day, yielding 5,695 tagged days across 18 tags.

Key findings include: vocabulary overlap as a proxy for relationship cooling (e.g., 69.5% to 8.7% shared top words); questioning rate inversely correlated with relationship bandwidth; and Dunbar's layers holding true—but contact attrition spiked after moves during 20s (extinction event) versus 30s. The median friendship lasted 3 years, yet 41 reappeared after 3+ silent years. The author discovered they are equally a "supportive" and "advice" friend, and travel increased conversational warmth. Notably, the total conversation-days per year stayed flat at ~360 despite losing 75% of contacts, suggesting a fixed emotional bandwidth.

My social life over two decades fits in 70 MB—smaller than a single iPhone photo burst.

What Apple and Google are doing to push notifications
⬆ 302 pts 💬 305 HN comments

The article traces the evolution of push notifications from a battery-saving solution (APNs in 2009, FCM in 2016) to a heavily intermediated channel where Apple and Google increasingly act as gatekeepers. Over the past five years, on-device language models (Apple Intelligence's 3B-parameter model, Google's Gemini Nano) have begun summarising, reordering, and rewriting notifications before display. This mirrors the earlier transformation of email, where providers like Gmail and Apple Mail introduced category sorting, privacy protections, and AI summaries. The push channel, however, offers senders far less visibility: no equivalent of email's Postmaster Tools, and no API to detect whether a notification was summarised, demoted, or blocked. The article argues that the platform's intervention, framed as defending user attention, primarily protects its own ecosystem and AI showcase, leaving senders with limited levers to influence delivery.

Key data points include Android 13's POST_NOTIFICATIONS runtime permission, which dropped opt-in rates from 85% to 67% (Batch's 2025 benchmark of 800 billion messages), and Apple's Notification Summaries, which after BBC complaints about false headlines enabled per-app toggles but remain on by default. The article details the technical stack: on-device models with LoRA adapters for specific tasks, and the absence of sender-side instrumentation to see these edits. It contrasts push's opacity with email's more mature feedback loops (SPF, DKIM, DMARC, complaint rates). The societal impact is a growing tension between marketers seeking reach and users increasingly valuing control—a shift accelerated by platforms that now act as active editors, not neutral pipes. The article itself, written from a revenue operations perspective, reveals the sender's frustration, but the HN comments overwhelmingly side with the user's right to silence.

Push notifications are now being reworded and ranked by on-device LLMs (Apple's 3B-parameter model, Google's Gemini Nano) with no API for senders to detect such alterations, marking a radical shift from the channel's original design as a simple relay.

Key Highlights
  • Push notifications originated as a battery-saving measure but have evolved into a platform-controlled channel with on-device AI summarisation and ranking.
  • Apple and Google's models (Apple Intelligence, Gemini Nano) run after delivery but before display, using LoRA adapters for tasks like summarisation and prioritisation.
  • Senders lack visibility into whether their notifications were altered—no open rate equivalent, no post-editing instrumentation.
  • User opt-in rates fell sharply after Android 13's runtime permission (from 85% to 67%) and similar iOS changes reflect a broader shift toward user-controlled attention.
  • The community overwhelmingly supports platform intervention, seeing it as necessary defense against spammy marketing notifications.

Go: Support for Generic Methods
⬆ 254 pts 💬 217 HN comments

The Go team has accepted proposal #77273 to add generic methods to the language, targeting Go 1.27. This marks a significant reversal from earlier stances, including the Go FAQ's statement that 'Go will never add generic methods.' The proposal, authored by Robert Griesemer, argues that concrete methods (non-interface methods) are valuable for code organization and method chaining, independent of interface satisfaction. Importantly, generic methods cannot implement interfaces—interface methods cannot have type parameters, so a generic concrete method is ineligible to satisfy an interface. This deliberate limitation avoids the performance and complexity issues of generic interface methods (e.g., runtime reflection or monomorphization of interface calls). The change is backward-compatible, as it only removes an existing restriction.

Technically, the syntax change allows type parameters on method declarations (e.g., `func (r T) Method[P any](p P)`). The existing Go parser already accepts this syntax (though it errors), so parsing changes are minimal. The compiler backend and type checker require more work, and the import/export data format needs updating to handle method-level type parameters—identified as the most disruptive part. Method calls on concrete receivers can be statically resolved, effectively rewriting them as generic function calls. The proposal has drawn over 250 points on Hacker News and extensive community discussion. While many users welcome the feature for practical use cases (e.g., data access, chainable APIs), critics label it syntactic sugar that adds complexity without addressing deeper issues like error handling or enum support.

Generic methods are finally proposed for Go, but they cannot implement interfaces—a deliberate limitation to avoid the complexities of generic interface methods.

Key Highlights
  • The proposal allows concrete methods to have their own type parameters, independent of the receiver type parameters.
  • Generic methods cannot satisfy interfaces because interface methods cannot have type parameters.
  • The change is backward-compatible and accepted for Go 1.27, but requires updates to the compiler backend, type checker, and import/export format.
  • Method calls on concrete receivers are statically resolved, avoiding the need for runtime instantiation or reflection.
  • Community opinions range from enthusiastic adoption (e.g., for data access APIs) to skepticism that it adds complexity without fundamental improvements.
