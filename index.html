Memdoor: A team of 5 Qwen3.8-27B models beats Fable 5 on LiveCodeBench Hard
⬆ 325 pts 💬 158 HN comments

Memdoor's team-solve example is a deliberately minimal orchestration harness: eight YAML files on the 'mario' engine, no Python, no model hardcoded. Five Qwen3.8-27B instances — a planner, two parallel ideators (one proposing the most direct provable algorithm, one from a different family), a solver acting as manager, and a repair worker — each start from a fresh context and coordinate only through files on disk (task.md, plan.md, notes.md, solution.py, test-output.log). The DAG is expressed purely with `requires:` clauses, and crucially the test and verify steps are shell commands, not model judgments: exit code and `diff` against sample tests decide success. It is a reimplementation of the GVS5H method (Lee, 2026), whose 'ledger discipline' the paper credits for the gain — an explicit bet that context hygiene matters more than per-token model intelligence.

The headline numbers: on ten LiveCodeBench Hard problems, five passes each, the workflow scores a 9.8 mean versus 9.6 for the original authors' harness on the same model and 8.0 for 'Fable 5' at one call per problem. Median run took 40 minutes and $0.39; all 50 runs cost $35.79, with a 128K reply cap and inference via OpenRouter rather than local hardware. The author is unusually candid about limits: ten problems is not the authors' 100, 'on a sample this small, 9.8 and 9.6 are the same number,' spring-2025 contest contamination cannot be excluded for any column, and the one lost problem came from the manager inventing a wrong counterexample that overwrote a correct program. The real claim is orchestration parity, not a model breakthrough.

Five 27B instances, a median of 40 minutes and $0.39 per problem produced 9.8/10 on LiveCodeBench Hard — statistically indistinguishable from the 9.6 baseline the author is explicit about: 'the claim is parity with their harness, not a gain on it.'

Key Highlights
  • A five-role agent team (planner, two parallel ideators, solver/manager, repair worker) built entirely from YAML steps with file-based handoffs and no shared chat context
  • Success is decided by shell commands and hidden tests, never by a model self-assessment — test failure never aborts, but verify's nonzero exit does
  • Reported 9.8 mean over ten LiveCodeBench Hard problems × five passes versus 9.6 (authors' harness, same Qwen3.8-27B) and 8.0 for Fable 5 single-call
  • Cost profile: $0.39 median per run, 40 minutes wall clock, $35.79 total for 50 runs, 128K reply budget; Cerebras was tried but caps output at 32K
  • The author concedes small-sample noise, possible training contamination, and that both loop defects (manager-invented counterexample, correct programs overwritten) were inherited unchanged

HackerTube: YouTube built from videos recommended by HN
⬆ 1610 pts 💬 427 HN comments

Andrej Karpathy’s HackerTube is a deceptively simple inversion of YouTube’s recommendation stack: it treats Hacker News comments as a human-curated prior instead of an engagement-optimized model. Using Claude Code with Opus 5.5 on medium effort, Karpathy had an agent download HN comments, filter them, run Sonnet over roughly 40,000 video-recommendation comments in a workflow, strip spam, and distill 6,454 YouTube videos across 150 topics from 2007–2026. The front end wraps embeds in a familiar YouTube UI, replaces uploaded thumbnails with midpoint frames, uses local browser session state rather than accounts, and exports the corpus so other agents can fork it. Architecturally it is a static index plus embed player, not a new hosting platform; the real artifact is the curation pipeline and the agentic ops loop that provisioned, secured, hardened, and optimized a VPS. Karpathy says the only manual steps were an API key, a VPS, and a domain; the build now takes about two hours.

The 1,610-point, 427-comment reaction shows why the demo landed: readers called the feed "refreshing" and "instantly more relevant" than YouTube’s homepage, and one said it resembles their 2010 feed before the algorithm "figured out who I am and started showing me manmade horrors beyond comprehension." Karpathy attributes the gap to gradient-signal economics: HN-like users are "a drop of a drop in the bucket" in A/B tests dominated by mainstream tastes. The thread quickly turned constructive—requests for recency sorting (which Karpathy added), topic playlists, volume controls, and age-restricted handling—and personal: one commenter wanted filtered YouTube for his 87-year-old father stuck in negative WWII recommendations. Others flagged YouTube ToS gray areas, HN taste monoculture, broken export links, and the irony of an AI-built wrapper around the platform whose algorithm it rejects.

40,000 Hacker News comments compressed into 6,454 videos across 150 topics—and commenters found the unpersonalized result more relevant than YouTube’s personalized feed.

Key Highlights
  • Karpathy used Claude Code with Opus 5.5 to build HackerTube, a YouTube clone sourced from videos recommended in Hacker News comments.

TCP Use-After-Free: CVE-2026-98432 / CVSS 9.8
⬆ 578 pts 💬 277 HN comments

CVE-2026-98432 is a CVSS 9.8 remote use-after-free in Linux’s IPv4 TCP handshake, affecting kernels from 4.4 onward and fixed upstream between October 11 and 18, 2026, across stable lines including 5.10.272, 5.15.223, 6.1.190, 6.6.159, 6.12.113, 6.18.56, 7.2.10, and 7.3. The flaw lives in request-socket handling: when an incoming SYN carries IPv4 options, the kernel saves them, hands them to the full connection without copying, then frees them while another CPU may still read them to construct a handshake reply. Eric Dumazet’s fix is a four-line change replacing kfree() with kfree_rcu() in the request-socket destructor, delaying reuse until an RCU grace period. Because the vulnerable path runs before any application authentication, every Linux host accepting IPv4 TCP is theoretically in scope, including container hosts and Kubernetes nodes that share the host kernel.

The operational story is distribution lag. As of October 31, Ubuntu listed every release as vulnerable, with fixes only in “proposed,” while RHEL 8, 9, and 10 remained affected without a shipped kernel; AlmaLinux had testing kernels, and Debian, SUSE, Oracle UEK, and Amazon Linux had fixes. Anthropic researcher Xinyang Ge reported the bug, and Anthropic says Claude wrote a working remote-root exploit for servers with a multipath TCP listener, taking 48 minutes to leak a kernel pointer and five more to reach command execution. MPTCP matters because Go 1.24 enables it by default for Linux listen sockets, and Caddy and Tailscale have been observed opening MPTCP sockets. Interim mitigations drop IPv4 TCP packets carrying options via nftables or iptables, disable MPTCP, or filter at XDP, but Red Hat warns these reduce rather than eliminate exposure.

Anthropic claims Claude produced a remote-root exploit that “takes 48 minutes to leak a kernel pointer and another five minutes to reach the shell command execution”—yet the PoC remains unpublished.

Key Highlights
  • Linux IPv4 TCP use-after-free CVE-2026-98432 is rated CVSS 9.8 and affects kernels from 4.4 onward; upstream fixes landed in stable releases from October 11–18, 2026.
  • The bug frees IPv4 option data on a request socket while another CPU may still read it during handshake reply construction; the fix swaps kfree() for kfree_rcu().
  • Ubuntu and RHEL had not shipped fixed kernels as of October 31, while Debian, SUSE, Oracle UEK, Amazon Linux, and testing AlmaLinux kernels had fixes.
  • Anthropic says Claude generated a working remote-root exploit against servers with an MPTCP listener; Go 1.24 and Caddy can open MPTCP sockets by default.
  • Interim mitigations include nftables/iptables rules dropping IPv4 TCP packets with options, disabling net.mptcp.enabled, and filtering IP-option packets at the edge, but these are incomplete.

ParticleDB's transaction semantics were also opaque: SET TRANSACTION ISOLATION LEVEL was ignored or rejected, while startup --txn-mode values ranged across fast, occ, zero-copy-occ, table-2pl, and serializable aliases whose guarantees were inconsistently documented.

The anomalies are not merely crashes but correctness failures: partially invisible rows that cannot be deleted, predicates over nonexistent columns matching rows, SELECT ... #6
It's the memory latency, stupid
⬆ 226 pts 💬 73 HN comments

Pivot (Epsio Labs) uses a deliberately sabotaged build of its own query engine to demonstrate that a CPU pinned at 100% by `top` is frequently doing almost nothing: on a single-core ClickBench `GROUP BY UserID` over 99M rows (~17M distinct keys), `perf stat -M backend_bound_memory,retiring` showed 50.1% of slots stalled on incomplete loads and only 16.1% retiring micro-ops. The culprit is the latency hierarchy — a DRAM access costs ~50–100ns versus roughly 4–8 instructions per nanosecond, i.e. 400–800 instructions of potential work burned per cache miss — so a monolithic hash table with millions of entries guarantees nearly every probe is a cold miss. Pivot's fix is textbook radix partitioning: hash each key, take the top 12 bits as a partition id (4096 partitions, `hash >> 52`), spill tuples into slab-backed vectors, then aggregate one small partition at a time into a private hash table that actually fits in L1/L2.

The result is a controlled experiment in trading compute for memory traffic. The partitioned version issues *more* retired operations — 19.35B versus 11.43B — yet drops back-end memory stalls from 8.36B to 3.79B slots, lifts retiring from 16.1% to 32.2%, and cuts demand DRAM fills from 76.2M cache lines (~4.5GB) to 25.7M (~1.5GB). Wall clock falls from 3.6s to 2.7s on the second (page-cache-warm) run; the shipped Pivot engine reaches 1848ms and 954MB on one core, and 171.7ms across 16 cores of an AMD EPYC 9124 Genoa with 128GB DDR5. The broader point — memory latency, not instruction count, is the real budget — is exactly the thesis HN readers keep rediscovering, and the comment thread treats the post less as novel research than as a well-instrumented reminder of a craft the industry has been quietly outsourcing to abstractions.

50.1% of CPU slots were stalled waiting on memory and only 16.1% were retiring operations — after radix partitioning, the engine executed 69% *more* instructions and finished 25% faster, because DRAM traffic fell from 4.5GB to 1.5GB.

Key Highlights
  • `perf stat -M backend_bound_memory,retiring` is the decisive tool: 100% CPU utilization can mean half the pipeline is stalled on loads.
  • Radix partitioning trades extra instructions (11.4B → 19.4B retired ops) for cache residency, cutting memory stalls by more than half.
  • DRAM demand fills fell from 76.2M to 25.7M cache lines — 4.5GB to 1.5GB — with warm-page-cache timings improving from 3.6s to 2.7s.
  • Production Pivot, which parallels across cores and adds further tricks, does the same query in 1848ms single-core and 171.7ms on 16 cores.
  • The technique is standard (hierarchical/radix-partitioned aggregation); the post's value is the measurement methodology, not the algorithm.

#7
Electrification Could Cut Global Energy Use in Half
⬆ 164 pts 💬 110 HN comments

Robert Llewellyn's interview with Professor Jan Rosenow (Oxford, Energy and Climate Policy) makes a deceptively simple argument: the energy transition is less about generating more and more about wasting less. Roughly 80% of global final energy still flows through fossil-fueled mobility and heating, and the incumbent system is 'astonishingly inefficient' — thermal generation plus transmission rejects about two-thirds of primary energy as waste heat before it ever does useful work. Electrify that end-use load with technologies that beat Carnot rather than obey it, and the denominator of the whole problem shrinks: total global energy demand could fall by roughly half, not because people consume less, but because the conversion chain stops throwing energy away.

The mechanism is efficiency arithmetic, not austerity. Heat pumps deliver 3–4 kWh of heat per kWh of electricity (COP 300–400%) versus resistive or combustion heat; EVs convert 80–90% of stored energy to motion against 25–35% for an ICE drivetrain; induction transfers heat into the pot rather than the room air. Rosenow also takes on the standard objections: data centers are a sharper but far more regional grid stressor than 100 million EVs, battery materials are heading toward 'urban mining' with 95–97% recoverable content, and China's solar buildout has visibly eroded coal plant utilization. The framing has shifted from decarbonization alone toward energy security and industrial resilience.

The HN thread immediately re-derives the primary-versus-final-energy distinction, then fractures over practical frictions: residential demand charges, retrofit economics, and whether efficiency gains simply get spent elsewhere.

A commenter citing '1kg of coal = 1000 Wh, generator loses half to waste heat' captured the accounting point crisply, while tianji-astrolog's COP-and-Sankey explanation drew the thread's most substantive agreement.

Roughly two-thirds of all energy inputs are currently rejected as waste heat — which is why electrifying heat and transport can halve total global energy demand without anyone consuming less.

Key Highlights
  • Primary vs. final energy accounting inflates fossil demand: replacing coal's 1000 Wh input needs only ~500 Wh of delivered electricity
  • Heat pumps (COP 3–4) and EVs (80–90% drivetrain efficiency vs. 25–35% for ICE) shrink demand at the point of use
  • Data centers are a more acute but regionally concentrated grid challenge than 100 million EVs
  • Battery recycling (95–97% material recovery) reframes 'running out of lithium' as an urban-mining and geopolitics problem
  • China's solar deployment scale and falling coal plant utilization signal the transition is already load-bearing, not hypothetical

One-minute frequency disturbances from synchronization in power systems
⬆ 19 pts 💬 4 HN comments

A Nature Energy paper by Hartmann, De Din, Carta, Witthaut and colleagues identifies a globally reproducible 1-minute oscillation in grid frequency — a narrow Fourier peak present in 12 power systems across five continents, from Iceland's roughly 400,000 customers to the 400-million-customer Continental European synchronous area. The authors separate this from known deterministic frequency deviations: the 60-minute peak from hourly block trading and the 15-minute peak from shorter market intervals, plus inter-area electromechanical swings at 1-10 s and data-centre-driven load ripple. The 1-minute signal, by contrast, is phase-coherent for years and reproducible across unconnected systems — behaviour incompatible with intrinsic machine modes but consistent with drivers locked to an external clock. Mechanistically, clock-synchronised control firmware in battery-backed UPSs and BESS units issues periodic self-tests and set-point updates, producing coincident demand peaks that propagate from distribution feeders up to the transmission interface, where the aggregated swing equation converts them into measurable frequency droop.

The amplitude evidence is the operationally interesting part. British and Scandinavian hourly amplitudes rose sharply after 2015 and peaked in 2020, amplified both by falling rotational inertia as converter-interfaced generation displaces synchronous machines and by pandemic-era demand composition shifts. Scandinavian deviations regularly exceeded 10 mHz, driving turbine wear in hydropower units providing primary control; transmission system operators responded in 2023 by tightening frequency containment reserve prequalification, adding 10-300 s sine-wave tests with stricter damping requirements — after which no >10 mHz episodes occurred in 2025. Hydropower resonance and secondary-control set-point updates can amplify the signal but not explain its cross-system phase coherence. The authors argue mitigation belongs at the source: randomised timer offsets and de-synchronised firmware scheduling, a concern that scales with battery storage, data-centre and IoT growth.

A spectral peak at exactly 1 minute appears in 12 grids spanning five continents — and when Scandinavian amplitudes regularly exceeded 10 mHz, TSOs rewrote frequency-containment rules in 2023, effectively eliminating the >10 mHz episodes by 2025.
