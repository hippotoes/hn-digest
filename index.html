Mistral Large 4
⬆ 1943 pts 💬 1159 HN comments

Mistral Large 4 is a from-scratch, open-weight pretrain positioned less as a frontier-breaker than as an assertion of sovereign capability: per the thread, ML4 was "trained from scratch on 3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs in Mistral's own datacenters in Europe." Note the article body itself 404'd, so everything below is reconstructed from the ~1,160-comment HN thread — benchmark numbers are therefore vendor claims and third-party aggregator reads (Artificial Analysis, Vals Index), not independently verified results. Technically the release is notable for what it *doesn't* do: it exposes only two reasoning settings, "none" and "high," and leans on strength in narrow, high-stakes domains rather than general Pareto dominance — reported 82% on CyberGym-E2E and 42% vs 41% visual grounding (Dense 200) against GPT-6 Astra. The implicit thesis is that a European lab can still buy its way into relevance with one large, capital-intensive run rather than a fleet of fine-tunes over Chinese weights.

The concrete data points are where the thread gets sharpest. chriddyp (Plotly) reports the model going 58%→74% on their data-analytics eval at "10x cheaper than Mistral Medium 3.5 from April" — a generational jump in six months. Pricing is the most contested number: pizlonator cites $0.68 in / $0.07 cached / $2.09 out, and james2doyle immediately corrects that this is a launch discount: "It is 50% off for the next two weeks... After that, it will be much closer to GLM 5.3." jakozaur places it behind GLM-5.3 on the broader frontier (Vals Index 48.05% vs 53.51%; $13.78 vs $7.25 per test) while ahead on cyber. Meanwhile simonw's tooling harness found the reasoning toggle largely cosmetic — '"high" actually produced less output tokens than "none"' — a caution against reading the reasoning-mode label as a capability knob. The community's de facto eval suite remains pelican-on-a-bicycle SVG, Redactle, and whatever the commenter ships to production, with the model cheerfully adding an unrequested sun to every pelican.

simonw's instrumentation found Mistral Large 4's reasoning setting is effectively a no-op: "high" produced *fewer* output tokens than "none" — yet the same run yielded the best pelican-on-a-bicycle SVG he has seen from any Mistral model.

Key Highlights
  • Pretrained from scratch on ~3,800 NVIDIA Grace Blackwell GPUs in Mistral's own European datacenters — capital intensity reframed as a sovereignty argument.
  • Narrow-domain strength rather than frontier dominance: reported 82% on CyberGym-E2E and 42% vs 41% (Dense 200) visual grounding against GPT-6 Astra, but behind GLM-5.3 on Vals Index (48.05% vs 53.51%).
  • Pricing is the live controversy: quoted $0.68 in / $0.07 cached / $2.09 out is a two-week 50% launch discount that doubles thereafter, eroding the cost case against GLM 5.3-flash.
  • Third-party evals show real movement — Plotly's analytics benchmark went 58%→74% at ~10x lower cost than Mistral Medium 3.5 — while AA Omniscience scores regressed, corroborated by weak Redactle trivia performance.
  • The reasoning toggle ('none' vs 'high') and the persistent unrequested sun in generated SVGs both point to cosmetic controls and possible training-set overfitting.

EmbeddingGemma 2: An open, lightweight multimodal embedding model
⬆ 395 pts 💬 41 HN comments

Google DeepMind's EmbeddingGemma 2 extends last year's text-only on-device embedder (which quietly cleared 20 million downloads) into a natively multimodal encoder built on the Gemma 4 architecture and released under Apache 2.0. The design is deliberately modular: a 270M-parameter text tower, an optional 170M vision encoder, and a 300M audio encoder compose into a 740M multimodal model, so text-only deployments pay only for what they use. Output vectors use Matryoshka Representation Learning, allowing dynamic truncation from 768 dimensions down to 512, 256, or 128 — up to 6x storage reduction for local vector stores. The context window quadruples to 8K tokens, enough for ~5.5 minutes of audio, 29 images, or 58 video frames, and it shares Gemma 4's tokenizer and audio encoder so both models can co-reside in one pipeline with a reduced combined memory footprint.

The headline benchmark movement is in code: MTEB Code jumps 9.92 points, from 68.76 to 78.68, while text quality matches v1 and vision/audio land at best-in-class for sub-1B models. On a Pixel 11 Pro with quantization, text-only weights need as little as ~191MB active RAM versus ~567MB for the full multimodal stack. Commenters anchored on this. simonw praised Apache 2.0 on economic grounds — proprietary embedders force costly re-embedding of millions of stored vectors when the vendor retires the model. minimaxir welcomed a credible mid-size embedder, reporting M3 Pro throughput of 78 text embeddings/sec, 4 image/sec, and 6 audio/sec. Skeptics pushed back: jraedisch found no text-retrieval gain, Zambyte's MediaPipe decision demo misfired on 'Cancel my flight and refund my credit card immediately' at p(true)=0.22, and aabhay noted MRL — unlike MatFormers — shrinks vectors but not weights.

MTEB Code improved 9.92 points (68.76 → 78.68) while the full multimodal model still fits in ~567MB of active RAM on a Pixel 11 Pro — and text-only needs only ~191MB.

Key Highlights
  • 740M total parameters split as 270M text, 170M vision, 300M audio — each component loadable independently
  • Apache 2.0 license makes the weights commercially usable and protects against hosted-API vendor lock-in and forced re-embedding
  • Matryoshka Representation Learning truncates 768-dim vectors to 512/256/128, yielding up to 6x storage reduction for local vector DBs
  • 8K token context (4x EmbeddingGemma 1) handles ~5.5 minutes of audio, 29 images, or 58 video frames per input
  • First-party integrations span LiteRT/MediaPipe, transformers.js, WebGPU, Ollama, llama.cpp, vLLM, MLX, Qdrant, and Unsloth fine-tuning

Sharing AI progress in mathematics
⬆ 1104 pts 💬 1173 HN comments

OpenAI published a GitHub repository of mathematical results generated by an unreleased internal frontier model, released with explicit community-governance scaffolding: consultation with the Institute for Advanced Study's Advisory Group on Mathematics and Artificial Intelligence, citation and revision protocols, and Lean formalizations for many proofs. The scope is extraordinary if it holds — commenters identify proofs of the Unique Games Conjecture (recast as the "Unique Games Theorem"), Barnette's Conjecture (problem 180), the three-machine unit-job scheduling problem open since Garey & Johnson 1979, and progress on Riemann, Hodge, Birch–Swinnerton-Dyer, Navier–Stokes, Hadwiger, Baum–Connes, Landau–Siegel zeros, and the Anderson-model extended-states problem. The technical foundation is dual: a frontier reasoning model paired with machine-checkable proof terms in Lean. That combination is what makes the claims auditable at all; as one commenter put it, without encoding theorems in Lean "we would be swarmed with a billion lines of theorems that no one will be able to ever understand and verify anyway."

The disclosed economics are striking: "the average result used the equivalent compute of roughly three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking," alongside ten reasoning-trace summaries and attempt statistics. Commenters immediately stress-tested the artifacts. The polynomial-time results carry absurd constants — an O(n (log n)^(1−κ)) integer-multiplication bound with κ = 2^−182, and a scheduling algorithm whose exponent commenters read as 10^12 — which arguably reveals proof-search residue rather than tight asymptotics; algorias notes the stray "+2" "reduces my confidence that the bound is tight." amluto doubts the model writes English well, citing broken opening sentences in the UGC preprint. Meanwhile zone411's scrape of proofatlas.ai claims full resolution of 90 of the top 500 open problems — met with fair demands about that ranking being LLM-pairwise "vibes." The human cost is documented in real time: a 24-year Barnette obsession, an ERC group whose entire multi-year agenda was solved "in this one post," and PhD advisors telling students their problems are now 15-minute exercises.

"The average result used the equivalent compute of roughly three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking" — a claimed window of three hours of inference reportedly yielding proofs of the Unique Games Conjecture, Barnette's Conjecture, and progress on four Millennium-class problems at once.

Key Highlights
  • OpenAI released frontier-model math results via GitHub with Lean formalizations, IAS-advised release protocols, and citation/revision policies
  • Claimed results include the Unique Games Conjecture, Barnette's Conjecture, three-machine unit-job scheduling (open since 1979), plus progress on Riemann, Hodge, BSD, and Navier–Stokes
  • Compute per result was disclosed as roughly three hours of ChatGPT Pro thinking, with only 10 partial reasoning-trace summaries rather than full traces
  • Commenters flag pathological constants (κ = 2^−182; ~10^12 exponents) as evidence of proof-search artifacts rather than tight bounds
  • Human impact dominates discussion: demoralized mathematicians, pivoting PhD students, and debate over credit, scooping, and whether verification capacity can keep pace

How many GPUs is 1M/B/T tokens?
⬆ 7 pts 💬 0 HN comments

Cedana, a company selling live migration and checkpoint/restore for CPU and GPU containers under Kubernetes and SLURM, has published a capacity-planning calculator that converts token volume into GPU count. The framing problem is real: "tokens per month" has become the industry's headline growth metric, but the number is meaningless without specifying model, hardware, workload mix and utilization. The calculator makes those assumptions explicit and, unusually for a marketing artifact, links every default to a source and exposes a low/base/high sensitivity diagram. The core arithmetic is transparent: 1 trillion tokens over 30 days is 385,802 tokens/second average; a 3:1 input-to-output chat mix applies a 0.475 multiplier to yield 183,256 output-equivalent tokens/second; dividing by 50% average utilization and 1,000 tok/s per H100 gives 367 GPUs. The same formula is recomputed per model preset and per GPU type, with token-mix, utilization, input-cost and multi-GPU-efficiency terms as knobs.

The data points are the interesting part. Llama 3.3 70B on H100 gives 211/367/853 GPUs; B200 gives 47/92/252; A100 gives 436/815/1,966; V100 gives 1,338/2,547/7,122 but needs three GPUs per model copy (77 GB of weights against 32 GB), so Cedana labels it theoretical. Presets span Llama 3.1 8B (3,000–12,500 tok/s H100) to DeepSeek V4 Pro (1,600B total / 49B active, 865 GB weights, B200 at 468–2,800 tok/s). Sensitivity analysis shows the throughput assumption dominates: moving H100 speed from 450 to 1,500 tok/s swings the answer from 814 to 244 GPUs, while utilization (30%→80%) swings 611→229. Cedana warns of roughly 2x error in each direction, combines errors as a root sum of squares in log space, and explicitly excludes prompt caching, speculative decoding, reasoning-token overhead, failures and regional duplication - the very levers that dominate real serving economics.

1 trillion tokens per month on Llama 3.3 70B lands at ~367 H100 GPUs (range 211–853) - but the sensitivity chart shows that changing only the assumed per-GPU throughput moves the same answer from 244 to 814 GPUs, a 3.3x swing driven by a single unmeasured assumption.

Key Highlights
  • The tool converts token volume into GPU count via a fully disclosed formula: tokens ÷ seconds × [output share + input share × input cost] ÷ utilization ÷ per-GPU tok/s.
  • Every model preset carries low/base/high throughput values with provenance labels ('Measured' from inferencex/cerebrium benchmarks vs 'Estimated' scaled from active parameters), and 'Estimate' presets admit no direct benchmark exists.
  • Sensitivity analysis is the most valuable output: throughput assumption dominates (244–814 GPUs), utilization second (229–611), input-token cost third (251–482).
  • MoE models show 4x–21x B200-over-H100 throughput ratios driven by FP4 support and memory capacity, and the ratio is model-specific rather than a hardware constant.
  • Cedana states ~2x error each direction, uses root-sum-of-squares in log space (not a confidence interval), and omits prompt caching, speculative decoding, reasoning overhead, failures and regional duplication.

Tell HN: GitHub refuses to remove cracked copies of my software after a month
⬆ 431 pts 💬 225 HN comments

Photopea, a browser-based Photoshop clone whose entire image-processing engine runs client-side in JavaScript, has become the flashpoint for a structural problem in open-web software distribution. Its author (IvanK_net) reports that third parties have lifted the hosted JS bundle, stripped the ad-injection logic that monetizes the app, and republished it as standalone GitHub repos — frequently under a new product name. The DMCA notice he filed was rejected by GitHub with the reply that it was 'unable to confirm a violation of 17 U.S. Code § 1201,' the anti-circumvention provision, rather than § 512(c), the ordinary notice-and-takedown safe harbor. Commenters (thought-gap, tothrowaway, eli) zero in on this mismatch: 1201 governs 'descrambl[ing], decrypt[ing]... or otherwise avoid[ing], bypass[ing], remov[ing], deactivat[ing], or impair[ing] a technological measure,' and deobfuscation is a contested fit, whereas a plain 512(c) infringement notice citing specific copied files is far harder for a platform to wave off.

The thread's economics are as instructive as its law. DannyBee, an IP lawyer, frames enforcement as a binary: retain a specialist firm to 'play whack a mole' — cheaper than the $500/hour figure others assumed — or 'accept it as normal losses and ignore it.' Several users push back that this is an indictment of the system, not advice (voakbasda: 'there will be no justice here for you'). Technically, the suggested remedies split three ways: obfuscation plus domain-locked heuristics (gwbas1c, with pushback from ravenstine that modern tooling makes this trivial to defeat), migration of core logic into compiled WebAssembly (pdutt111, AbuAssar — countered by gpugreg's observation that 'LLMs can decompile assembly code quite well these days'), and server-side execution, which anakaine notes would destroy the client-compute cost model that makes indie web apps like Photopea viable at all. A GitHub employee (summarity) surfaced the company's public DMCA repository and offered to follow up internally, while IvanK_net confirmed he will now retain a lawyer. Parallel reports (BSVogler) describe Tencent-hosted copies with license checks removed.

GitHub's rejection was framed around the wrong statute: 'we're unable to confirm a violation of 17 U.S. Code § 1201' — and IvanK_net's own conclusion was that 'it would be really cool if I could spend my days writing code instead of dealing with lawyers and stuff.'

Key Highlights
  • GitHub rejected the takedown on § 1201 (anti-circumvention) grounds; commenters argue a § 512(c) copyright notice citing specific copied code is the correct instrument
  • Photopea's architecture — all processing in client-side JS monetized via ads — makes it trivially forkable once the ad logic is stripped
  • A GitHub employee (summarity) located the company's public DMCA archive and offered to escalate internally, illustrating that HN visibility outperformed the support queue
  • Proposed technical mitigations (obfuscation, domain-locked heuristics, WebAssembly, server-side execution) each drew counterarguments about LLM-assisted deobfuscation and loss of the client-compute cost model
  • The case is framed as a bellwether for LLM-era reproduction of source-available software, where functional reimplementation may not constitute infringement at all

Mallet Head Angle
This article from Timber Frame Tools explores the surprisingly nuanced question of the proper angle for a joiner’s mallet head, drawing on traditional timber-framing knowledge. The author argues that because a mallet is swung in an arc—whether from the wrist for light tapping or the elbow for heavy chopping—the striking face must be oriented so that at impact it is normal to the chisel handle. If not, the mallet twists and deflects the chisel, skewing the cut and producing a crooked mortise. The method for finding this angle is elegantly empirical: tape a string to the top corner of the mallet head and to your pivot point (elbow or wrist), mark where the string crosses the lower edge of the head, then transfer that angle to both faces with a bevel gauge.
