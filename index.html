After 600 experiments, here's what I discovered about JEPA world models
⬆ 273 pts 💬 46 HN comments

Haddy Alchaer's post is an unusually hands-on empirical audit of LeWM, the LeCun-affiliated JEPA world model whose benchmark is Push-T: encode a frame into a latent embedding, predict the next embedding conditioned on an action, then search over action sequences in latent space and score them by distance to a goal image's embedding. The headline finding inverts the field's default assumption. Swap LeWM's learned predictor for the simulator's exact physics and long-horizon success at a 75-step goal moves only from 78% to 77% — the learned dynamics are already as good as ground truth along in-distribution paths. The bottleneck is therefore not predictive accuracy but how the trained model is *used*: its reliable planning horizon is about 25 steps, so chaining 25-step searches through subgoals lifts success from 22% to 85% without a single gradient update, roughly matching a planner handed expert subgoals, and worth three times what perfect physics bought.

The remaining experiments push past the demonstration-shaped test set. A latent memory graph over ~460,000 embedded expert frames, with an explicit cost on inter-demonstration jumps, recovers the expert-subgoal score (62%→85%); symmetry-aware transforms (shift, rotate, mirror) fill memory gaps when goal-adjacent states are deleted, restoring performance from 32% to 85%. The most consequential result is a goal-scoring bug: goal images include the expert's pusher, so a planner matching the whole embedding drags the pusher to the wrong place. Scoring the block alone flipped 30 of 50 failures — a ~2-in-a-billion split — and searching over 16 pusher-relocated goal variants doubled success on the harder cross-demonstration test (11%→23%). The author is candid about ceilings: the standard Push-T split leaks (84% of goals have near-duplicates in memory) and seed noise flips 38 of 250 episodes.

Not addressed by any of this is subgoal generation beyond the data — the genuinely open problem.

LeWM's learned predictor matches the simulator's exact physics to within one point (78% vs 77%) on long-horizon tasks — but scoring goals on the block alone instead of block-plus-pusher flipped 30 of 50 failures to successes, a four-fold gain from a change to goal construction, not to the model.

Key Highlights
  • Replacing LeWM's learned predictor with ground-truth simulator physics yields only ~1 point of improvement (78%→77%), showing prediction accuracy is not the planning bottleneck
  • Chaining 25-step latent searches through subgoals raises long-horizon success from 22% to 85% with zero retraining, matching expert-provided subgoals
  • A latent memory graph with per-jump costs over ~460K embedded demonstration frames recovers 85% success using only the model's own embeddings
  • Goal images encode the demonstrator's pusher position, silently corrupting the cost function; excluding the pusher flipped 30/50 failures, and searching over 16 pusher-relocated goal copies doubled harder-test success
  • The standard Push-T benchmark is optimistic (84% of goals have near-duplicate memory states, seed noise flips 38/250 episodes); the open problem is proposing subgoals beyond the demonstration distribution

Jonathan Blow – If AI solved coding, why is my software still the same? [video]
⬆ 381 pts 💬 537 HN comments

Jonathan Blow's talk — titled 'If AI solved coding, why is my software still the same?' and drawn from an interview with Wolf Tivy — poses a deliberately provocative question to a field saturated with claims that LLMs have 'solved programming.' Blow's answer, as reconstructed by the 537-comment thread, is that the artifact-layer of software (tools, editors, languages, delivery models) has barely shifted since the early 2000s despite enormous churn in how code is produced. His argument is essentially a paradigm thesis: faster typing does not constitute a new computing paradigm, any more than replacing a factory's central steam engine with a single electric motor constituted electrification. The thread extends this to a pointed observation about vendor lock-in and OS design — including a detour into /proc, Plan 9 and whether 'everything is a file' was ever a coherent abstraction — suggesting the community reads Blow's thesis as much about systems architecture as about AI.

The replies split along a fault line that is now familiar: capability versus leverage. Several commenters argue the premise is simply premature — 'AI coding has only worked well in the last six months. Two years ago it sucked. A year ago it was flaky' — while others counter that the bottleneck was never code production but problem selection and architectural modelling, which LLMs do not address structurally. The most concrete counter-evidence is personal: engineers describing bespoke replacements for subscription software, a father reporting his eight-year-old 'vibecoded 25 games in three.js,' and multiple 'really amazing TUI tools' written largely by agents. The recurring metaphor is the electrification-of-factories lag: organizations swapped the steam engine for one big motor and took four decades to discover they could route power to individual workstations. Coding, several argue, was never the organizational bottleneck — and most companies do not know what to do with the slack.

'A lot of people are discovering that coding was never the bottleneck in their orgs and don't know what to do differently.'

Key Highlights
  • Blow's thesis: tooling and paradigms have barely changed since the early 2000s, so faster code production is not the same as a new computing paradigm
  • Commenters distinguish coding (now largely automatable) from problem selection, domain modelling and architectural design, which they argue LLMs cannot do structurally
  • Electrification-of-factories analogy: organizations adopted the new motor without redesigning the workflow around it, so the productivity gain lags by decades
  • Anecdotal counter-evidence centres on bespoke personal software — subscription replacements, agent-written TUI tools, an 8-year-old shipping 25 three.js games
  • Skeptics raise maintenance and review debt: code that is never read by humans degrades within a few turns, and PR volume fatigue is already visible

FAA issues national ground stop – lifted 4.5 hours later
⬆ 1598 pts 💬 1213 HN comments

At 3:54 p.m. ET on a Friday, the FAA's Air Traffic Control System Command Center issued advisory ATCSCC 111 ordering all departing traffic at every US airport to remain grounded, citing only "extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety." The stop was lifted at 8:15 p.m. ET, roughly 4.5 hours later, after more than 7,000 flights were cancelled and hubs such as O'Hare and Reagan National hit gate capacity. The proximate cause, per Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy and subsequent CBS reporting, was an update to the private communications network operated by L3Harris — widely understood to be the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure (FTI) — that caused routers to be "overloaded with data," producing inconsistency in radar feeds and controller-pilot radio links across Air Route Traffic Control Centers and TRACONs. DHS stated there was no evidence of a cyberattack.

The technical shape of the failure is familiar to anyone who has run hybrid estates: new IP-based equipment bridged into decades-old analog systems, where retransmit-heavy protocols can amplify load into a broadcast storm that legacy components were never sized to absorb. Commenters drew the obvious historical parallels — the 2009 FTI outage documented in a DOT OIG report, the January 2023 NOTAM failure caused by contract personnel deleting files during a database sync, and the post-9/11 shutdown. Scale matters: roughly 3,500–4,000 aircraft airborne at the moment of the order, ~25,000 daily domestic flights, and an estimated 300,000–400,000 stranded passengers. Duffy used the event to press Congress for a total of ~$45–50B against the $12.5B already authorized over five years, while the FAA's new AI routing tool vendor, Air Space Intelligence, preemptively disclaimed any role for its SMART system.

"Due to extraordinary circumstances and for reasons of safety" was the entire public justification in the 3:54 p.m. advisory — a four-hour national grounding of the world's busiest airspace announced with less technical detail than a routine git commit message.

Key Highlights
  • A nationwide ground stop halted all US departing flights from 3:54 p.m. to 8:15 p.m. ET, cancelling 7,000+ flights and stranding an estimated 300,000–400,000 passengers.
  • Officials attributed the outage to an update on an L3Harris-operated telecom network where routers became "overloaded with data," destabilizing radar and radio feeds to centers and TRACONs.
  • No cyberattack was found; Air Space Intelligence publicly denied its SMART AI routing tool was involved, and Duffy said SMART is "completely disconnected" from the affected equipment.
  • Historical precedents cited: the 2009 FTI outage, the 2023 NOTAM file-deletion failure, and the post-9/11 shutdown — nationwide ground stops remain extremely rare.
  • Duffy framed the event as evidence of a decades-old analog ATC system and asked Congress for roughly $50B total, on top of $12.5B already authorized.

#3
Terminal borders that don't cost a cell
⬆ 340 pts 💬 148 HN comments

Josh McKinney, who maintains the Ratatui TUI library, has published a draft terminal protocol that moves borders off the character grid entirely: instead of spending a cell on a ┌ or │ glyph, a cell carries border segments as an attribute — up to twelve per cell (both halves of each of its four edges, plus four centre arms), each with independent colour, width, placement and style. The motivation is arithmetic. On a ~200x50 terminal, nested panels can burn six to eight rows purely on decoration, box-drawing characters can never sit between two occupied cells (so no underlining a field right beneath its text or outlining a one-row button without padding), rounded corner glyphs inherit a single background colour that bleeds into the panel surround, and selecting text inside a box drags the box along with it into Slack. Because borders live *in* the cell rather than beside it, scroll, erase, insert, delete, alternate screen and reflow all fall out for free.

The implementation is a DCS-namespaced protocol (`ESC P 1 + b cell-border;...`) with a pen held at the cursor, rectangle commands that mutate existing cells, and a capability query followed by DA1 — so unaware terminals simply ignore the string and apps fall back to box characters, exactly as they do today. The Ghostty patch is roughly 1,650 lines including tests; each cell stores a 16-bit border ID inside pre-existing struct padding, pointing into a shared table of ~100-byte border sets, so borderless screens cost nothing and the pathological worst case is 10-15 MB. Prior art is surveyed and found wanting: DECterm ruled lines are a separate layer that survives clears, SGR 51-65 has no colour, joins or bottom edge, and Win32 console grid flags lack a bottom edge and halves. McKinney explicitly frames the spec as a v0.1 two-way door, asking terminal and TUI authors which parts look expensive.

Each cell gains a 16-bit border ID that fits in padding that was already there — "cells don't get any bigger and a screen with no borders costs nothing extra," at the price of a ~1,650-line Ghostty change and a worst-case 10-15 MB table of shared border sets.

Key Highlights
  • Borders as a cell attribute rather than glyphs: up to 12 line segments per cell with individual colour, width, placement and style, rendered over the grid and inheriting scroll/erase/reflow semantics from the cell
  • Protocol is a DCS namespace with a cursor-held pen, rectangle ops, and a capability query before DA1, so unsupported terminals ignore it and apps degrade to box-drawing
  • Shared-edge conflicts are resolved at render time by priority, then width, then right/below — a fix that arrived after the first 'right/bottom always wins' rule cost the focused pane half its outline
  • Switched from APC to DCS after feedback that Kermit 95 has executed APC payloads as commands since the 90s
  • Multiplexers are the hard case: an unaware tmux should drop the DCS and answer DA1 itself, forcing apps inside it to fall back; an aware one must store borders as cell state and redraw

Live airband on all frequencies from KORS. Victoria, CA also ground stopped
⬆ 281 pts 💬 66 HN comments

Orcas Airband is a live aviation-radio web app built by HN user mmaunder from a hangar at KORS, the untowered airport on Orcas Island in Washington's San Juan Islands. The backend is an Ettus B210 SDR feeding a dual RTX 4090 host; a CUDA-accelerated polyphase channelizer splits the entire civil airband into more than 700 channels, demodulates AM voice, discards data transmissions, and passes audio through a custom voice model for transcription. A separate LLM reads transcripts to build a traffic board. The UI exposes frequency lists, ATIS/AWOS, squelch threshold (10 dB default), busiest/last-heard sorting, and playback with explicit 'press play' audio. It became unexpectedly relevant during a ground stop/communications issue affecting Victoria airspace, highlighting how cheap SDR plus GPUs can turn spectrum into queryable operational context—while the site warns its auto-compiled board 'can be wrong or incomplete: not for navigation.'

Technically, the story is a compact case study in GPU-native DSP and AI ops: channelization, demodulation, transmission detection, data-transmission filtering, ASR, and LLM summarization all run in near real time. Commenters compared implementations—one uses Whisper on an RTX 3050 for a police scanner; another decodes ships' DSC via KiwiSDR; pilots asked to host nodes. The thread also surfaced aviation-domain constraints: AM simplex is retained for interoperability and the 'screech' when a transmitter is stepped on, plus pilots hear other pilots for situational awareness; online listeners hear weaker/garbled signals than cockpit or ATC receivers. Specific traffic snippets showed the system's value: at 14:03Z 'Encore 3325' confirmed 'Nothing is departing to land at Vancouver,' while other Victoria/Vancouver Centre clips mentioned communication issues. Yet the title's 'Victoria, CA' ambiguities and disputed ground-stop claims prompted corrections, and users critiqued squelch filtering and AI-ish UI styling.

The system splits the entire airband into more than 700 channels on dual RTX 4090s, then demodulates and transcribes live AM voice—yet its own banner warns the auto-compiled traffic board 'can be wrong or incomplete: not for navigation.'

#6
FM-1 Workbench
⬆ 253 pts 💬 164 HN comments

The FM-1 Workbench is a browser-based editor and manager for the M-Vave FM-1, a sub-$60 portable synthesizer and groovebox that has become an unlikely cult platform for custom firmware. The Workbench uses Web MIDI (Chrome/Edge only) to expose the device's full parameter set: an 8-track, 64-step sequencer; six synthesis engines (FM/DX7-compatible, 303 acid, 808 drums, PSG, Pluck, Sampler); preset and sample management; MIDI routing; and firmware updates. Under the hood, the FM-1 runs a Blackfin DSP with a FreeRTOS-derived SMP kernel and a dexed-compatible FM engine, meaning it can load DX7 patches and, with alternative firmwares, become a generative groovebox. M-Vave has leaned into the community by releasing an official SDK and an open firmware, turning a cheap Chinese gadget into a hackable hardware platform.

The Hacker News discussion (253 points, 164 comments) captures a broader tension in modern music tech: democratized hardware versus perceived low quality. Enthusiasts praise the FM-1 as a 'fun device for the price' and a 'rare bright spot in post-LLM music tech,' sharing firmware recommendations like NACHTFART, DUB-1, Sloop, Jiant and Obervave. Critics counter with reports of 'tons of clipping and a lot of noise on line out,' complaints about 1/8-inch MIDI jacks, a built-in fan, and flimsy construction. A darker thread accuses the ecosystem of 'AI slop'—'some clueless guy going uhh Claude make me a sick-sounding groovebox synth'—and warns of 'the entshitification of everything, the total lack of intention.' Meanwhile, bricking risks during web-based flashing have prompted a community bootloader (m-bootloader). The FM-1 thus serves as a case study in open hardware, AI-assisted development, and the enduring appeal of leaving the laptop behind.

A $50 synth becomes a platform: M-Vave's FM-1 runs a Blackfin DSP with a dexed-compatible FM engine, and its Workbench exposes every parameter via Web MIDI—prompting 164 HN comments debating whether this is democratized hardware or 'AI slop.'

  • The FM-1 is a ~$50-60 portable synth/groovebox with 8 tracks, 64 steps, 6 engines, and a Blackfin DSP running a FreeRTOS-derived SMP kernel.

What happened to 3Com?
⬆ 176 pts 💬 94 HN comments

3Com is a case study in how a dominant hardware franchise can be dismantled by commoditisation, not by a single catastrophic failure. Founded in 1979 by Bob Metcalfe, co-inventor of Ethernet, the company built its reputation on the 3C509 ISA and 3C905 PCI cards: expensive, US-manufactured, famously reliable, and backed by driver support that actually worked — the network-card equivalent of 'nobody got fired for buying IBM.' At its dotcom peak it was a $5.8B company that bought the naming rights to Candlestick Park. Then, per David Farquhar's retrospective, it walked away from enterprise networking, ceding switches to Cisco, overpaid for US Robotics and its dial-up modems and Palm devices just as broadband loomed, and lost its cost position to Intel. From 2000 to 2005 it never turned a profit; HP absorbed it for $2.7B in November 2009.

Three structural forces the article underplays emerge in the HN commentary. First, the integration of Ethernet MAC/PHY into the chipset and later the CPU package collapsed the add-in card market entirely — the NIC chip still exists, it is just sold to motherboard OEMs, not to consumers. Second, Microsoft's NDIS standardised the driver interface, destroying the proprietary-stack lock-in that had justified 3Com's price premium. Third, the low end got good enough: NE2000 clones and RealTek 8139s captured the volume, while DEC's Tulip chipset quietly won the technically demanding niche with multiple simultaneous MAC addresses for VRRP and HA. 3Com kept a premium price with no defensible moat left to charge it against.

The market is fragmenting by product line, not by corporate identity. HPE still ships 3Com-derived Comware switches, and commenters report them running for decades in PoE camera closets alongside Aruba gear with almost nothing changed between generations.

A 3C509 diagnostic tool literally instructs: 'ep0: 3c509 in test mode. Erase pencil mark! ... This is not a joke' — the card's test pad was a graphite scribble between the barcode and the empty BIOS socket.

Key Highlights
  • 3Com exited enterprise switching early, ceding share to Cisco and alienating dealers without saving costs
  • The US Robotics acquisition bet on dial-up modems and Palm just as broadband arrived, burning capital on a shrinking market
  • NDIS standardisation removed driver stickiness; integrated MACs later removed the add-in card market entirely
  • HP bought 3Com for $2.7B in 2009; the pedigree survives only as HPE Comware firmware under a different brand
  • 3c509 ISA and 3c905 PCI cards remain the gold standard for retro computing builds, with lifetime warranties justified by near-zero failure rates

Let's Write a PostScript Interpreter
⬆ 34 pts 💬 8 HN comments

The article is a guided implementation of a useful PostScript subset in deliberately concise JavaScript, framed by the idea that the most trustworthy languages are “human-scale” enough for one person to reimplement. It walks through PostScript’s concatenative stack model: whitespace-delimited tokens, `/`-prefixed literal names, procedures enclosed in braces, an operand stack for data, and a dictionary stack for name lookup. The JavaScript representation maps PostScript numbers, booleans, names, arrays, procedures, operators, and the special `mark` value onto native JS values, objects, and functions. A regex tokenizer plus a recursive parser clusters procedure tokens, while `exec`, `execproc`, and `execfunc` implement dispatch: names are resolved through the dictionary stack, operators are applied by arity, and procedures execute token-by-token. The style is intentionally Forth-like—short, mostly pure routines such as `last`, `iota`, and `mod`—to keep the interpreter comprehensible rather than production-grade.

Implementation details are concrete: `parse` uses a global regex to split comments, delimiters, names, and integers; `execfunc` pops `x.length` arguments and pushes any non-`undefined` return value; primitives such as `def`, `begin`, `end`, `forall`, `repeat`, `roll`, and `index` are installed into the initial dictionary. The article demonstrates a REPL in both Node.js and QuickJS, then explores idioms: recursive Fibonacci, iterative range construction, array reversal, concatenation, and Forth-style aliases like `swap`, `drop`, and `over`. It also flags semantic limits—dynamic rather than lexical scoping, no programmer-accessible return stack, and `bind` as an incomplete substitute for lexical substitution. The Hacker News discussion (34 points, 8 comments) ranges from a PostScript Deflate decompressor and generated puzzle PDFs to Ghostscript performance and the claim that the language core is trivial compared with graphics and font rendering.

The complete toy interpreter fits “on a printed page or two” yet runs genuine PostScript idioms such as recursive Fibonacci and array reversal—showing how much of a language’s semantics can be captured by two stacks and a dispatch loop.

Key Highlights
  • PostScript’s execution model is reduced to an operand stack, a dictionary stack, and token-by-token dispatch.

  • Primitive operators are plain JavaScript functions, applied by arity and installed into the initial dictionary.
