Kimi K3: Open Frontier Intelligence
⬆ 1638 pts 💬 964 HN comments

Kimi K3 is a 2.8 trillion parameter open-weight model released by Chinese AI lab Moonshot AI, built on novel architectures including Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) and Attention Residuals (AttnRes). It uses a Mixture of Experts (MoE) with 896 experts, activating 16 per token via Stable LatentMoE, and features a 1-million-token context window with native vision capabilities. The model claims frontier-level performance, rivaling proprietary models like Claude Fable 5 and GPT 5.6 Sol on benchmarks such as DeepSWE (67.5%), SWE Marathon (42%), and Program Bench (77.8%). It demonstrates advanced agentic capabilities, including autonomous chip design using open-source EDA tools, building a GPU compiler (MiniTriton) from scratch, and generating interactive research reports with thousands of web searches. Full model weights are slated for release by July 27, 2026, alongside a detailed technical report.

The launch triggers intense debate about AI commoditization and geopolitical strategy. Commenters note that Kimi K3 undercuts US labs on performance-per-dollar, with some calling it a 'commoditize my complement' move (softwaredoug) and others framing it as a Chinese national strategy to dominate the commoditized intelligence market (moconnor). Pricing is set at $3/MTok cache-miss input and $15/MTok output, aligning with Anthropic's Sonnet series but higher than other open models. While benchmark scores are strong, skepticism remains: some question whether the model will truly be open-weights given the removal of 'open' language from early materials (wolttam), and others flag training data privacy concerns (dovin). Early adopters report impressive results in practice, with one user noting it outperformed Fable on a bug-finding task (InsideOutSanta), though subscription limits are deemed 'pretty brutal.'

Kimi K3 is the first open model to reach 2.8 trillion parameters, and in a 48-hour autonomous run, it designed a chip for a nano model using open-source EDA tools, achieving 8,700 tokens/s decode throughput.

Key Highlights
  • 2.8T parameter MoE model with 16 active experts and 1M token context, using KDA and AttnRes architectures.
  • Competitive with top proprietary models (Fable 5, Sol) on coding and agentic benchmarks.
  • Demonstrates autonomous agentic feats: chip design, compiler construction, game dev, and deep research.
  • High pricing ($3/$15 per MTok) sparks debate on value versus leading US labs.
  • Full weights promised for July 2026; openness questioned after early language removal.

The LLM Critics Are Right. I Use LLMs Anyway
⬆ 230 pts 💬 240 HN comments

Jeremy Theocharis’s piece captures a profound cognitive dissonance widespread among engineers: they accept the valid criticisms of LLMs—slop, environmental cost, ethical issues, erosion of trust in open source—yet continue to use them daily. He recounts witnessing this tension at Local-First Conf, where speakers decried AI while attendees ran Claude Code. Theocharis argues that LLMs amplify human thought but cannot replace it, requiring deliberate techniques like his "/grill-me" skill to force shared understanding before generation. He warns that the ease of producing low-quality PRs threatens open source, with projects like Zig already rejecting AI contributions. The core problem is trust: in a world where anything can be generated fluently, human credibility becomes the only differentiator.

Theocharis’s own usage is extreme—$10k in a single month on tokens, which he acknowledges sounds insane. He mitigates by using cheaper models for code and reserving frontier models selectively. He highlights the geopolitical risk of US export controls, citing Anthropic’s abrupt cutoff of non-US access to Fable 5, and advocates for local open-weights models as a hedge. The article’s key insight is that LLMs are good at making things higher quality, but only when the human has done the thinking. Theocharis shares patterns: the Ralph Wiggum loop to stress-test ideas, and a blind agent that hallucinates the expected API to validate design. He concludes that the only way to maintain trust is to stand by every output as if reading it aloud on stage.

“The people building a tool for working with LLMs are themselves flooded by their own creation, and in order to protect themselves they are auto-closing it all.”

Key Highlights
  • LLMs amplify existing human thought but cannot replace the thinking itself; they require deliberate scaffolding like the /grill-me technique.
  • Trust is eroded because LLMs make it easy to produce fluent slop, making it impossible to distinguish genuine effort from generated content.
  • Open source is threatened by a flood of low-quality AI-generated PRs, forcing projects to auto-close or block contributions from unknown users.
  • Geopolitical risks from closed models are real, as demonstrated by US export controls cutting off access to frontier models overnight.
  • Local open-weights models provide a fallback when the AI bubble bursts, ensuring programmers remain independent of big corporations.

Decoy Font
⬆ 557 pts 💬 129 HN comments

Decoy Font is a TTF typeface that exploits hybrid image illusions to embed two distinct messages in the same glyphs: a high-spatial-frequency outline legible up close (intended for AI/OCR systems) and a low-frequency blur that becomes readable only when squinting or viewing from a distance (intended for humans). Built on the same principles as the famous Einstein-Monroe hybrid image, the font uses spatial frequency decomposition—high-pass filtering for the 'decoy' text and low-pass filtering for the hidden message—superimposed into a single glyph. The project's creator argues that current LLMs like GPT-4 and Gemini 3.5 with Thinking focus on sharp pixel transitions and thus fail to decode the blurred layer, effectively 'hiding' text from AI scrapers. The font is freely downloadable as a TTF and can be used in any application, making it a practical (if imperfect) tool for privacy-conscious users.

Community reactions are deeply divided. Many commenters successfully tested the font against frontier models, confirming that ChatGPT and Gemini often read only the decoy (e.g., 'SORRY ROBOT') while missing the hidden message ('HAPPY HUMAN'), though adding a hint like 'there is a hidden message' can improve performance. Conversely, skeptics note that simple image preprocessing (downsampling, low-pass filtering) or prompting the model to 'squint' trivially reveals both layers. One commenter even shared a PIL script to extract the low-frequency component. The font is praised as a clever optical illusion and a beautiful piece of anti-AI art, but its practical utility for protecting text long-term is widely doubted. The underlying tension reflects a broader debate: are such 'security through obscurity' measures valuable even if temporary, or do they mainly inconvenience humans while AI rapidly adapts?

“It doesn’t stop AI from reading it, but it sure stops me!” – a commenter perfectly capturing the irony of Decoy Font's human-readability challenge.

Key Highlights
  • Decoy Font uses hybrid-image spatial frequency separation to hide a second message visible only from a distance or when blurred.
  • Frontier LLMs like GPT-4 and Gemini 3.5 often fail to read the hidden text without explicit hints, but simple image processing defeats the illusion.
  • The font is freely available as a TTF and can be installed system-wide, lowering the barrier to anti-AI text obfuscation.
  • Commenters highlight the irony: the decoy message is often more legible to humans than the hidden one, making the font frustrating for human readers.
  • The project fuels ongoing discussion about the arms race between AI content scraping and human-centric privacy techniques.

$100 AI Music Video: Claude Fable 5 vs. GPT-5.6 Sol
⬆ 272 pts 💬 374 HN comments

In a head-to-head autonomous music video production challenge, two frontier LLMs — Claude Fable 5 and GPT-5.6 Sol — were each given a $25 or $100 budget, the song “Uptown Funk,” and access to web search, image/video generation APIs (FAL), and local ffmpeg for editing. The models independently researched tools, generated clips, and assembled a final video. The harness logged every tool call, enabling detailed comparison of their strategies and costs. Notably, GPT-5.6 Sol at the $25 budget adopted a keyframe + image-to-video pipeline (FLUX + Wan 2.2-5b i2v), while all other runs used pure text-to-video. The $100 runs allowed models to spend more on generation ($36.57 for Sol, $48.60 for Fable) but neither exhausted the budget. Claude Fable 5 consistently produced higher-resolution output (1920×1080 vs 1280×720) but at higher LLM token cost ($16.99–$25.05), making it the pricier option overall ($73.65 for the $100 run vs $39.82 for GPT-5.6 Sol at $100).

The resulting videos were universally panned as artistically lacking, with literal interpretations of lyrics (e.g., a dragon for “make a dragon wanna retire man”) and poor tempo matching. None achieved coherent character consistency or narrative arc. Yet the experiment reveals rapid progress in autonomous multi-modal tool use: both models completed the entire workflow in under 50 minutes without human intervention. A striking observation is that neither model used the available Replicate API; all generation went through FAL. GPT-5.6 Sol at $100 diversified across three video models (Wan 2.5, Veo 3.1 Lite, Hailuo 2.3), while Claude Fable 5 stuck with one. The most inventive editor was GPT-5.6 Sol at $25, which overlaid text and animated stills. Despite the low quality, the setup demonstrates that LLMs can now orchestrate a multi-step creative pipeline from research to final render — a capability that, with better video models and self-editing loops, could disrupt low-budget video production.

Despite a $100 budget and full autonomy, neither model spent more than $48.60 on generation, and the best video still suffered from literal interpretations and incoherent narrative — showing both the promise and the gap in AI-driven creative workflows.

The discovery underscores the importance of Lomami National Park's biodiversity, but also highlights tensions in scientific discourse.
