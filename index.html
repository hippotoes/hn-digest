On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem
⬆ 1284 pts 💬 1033 HN comments

OpenAI announced that an internal AI system has produced a proof solving the Navier–Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the Clay Millennium Prize Problems. The proof demonstrates that a smooth, finite-energy solution to the forced 3D incompressible Navier–Stokes equations can develop a singularity in finite time, meaning velocities become unbounded even though energy remains finite. This corresponds to options C and D of the official Millennium Problem formulation. The construction is a vortex tube that stretches and spirals inward, with a precise balance between acceleration, pressure, convection, and viscosity. The result is accompanied by a Lean formalization, and OpenAI notes the work was produced by an internal model more capable than GPT-6 Astra, trained for less than two weeks.

The scale of computation is unprecedented: a coordinating agent swarm with up to 10,000 concurrent agents generated 4.9 million messages and roughly 300 billion output tokens across all attempted Millennium Problems. The Navier–Stokes solution itself consumed 130 billion tokens over about 88 hours, with Lean verification taking an additional 17 hours. OpenAI candidly states it began the effort after hearing rumors that another team had solved a Millennium Problem, and it acknowledges potential indirect contamination by saying it 'cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models.' That sentence, plus the heated dispute with mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster and Levent Alpöge over contensorship and priority, has cast a long shadow over what might otherwise be the single most impressive AI-discovered mathematics result to date.

300 billion output tokens and 4.9 million agent messages produced a formal proof of a 90-year-old open problem in roughly 88 hours.

Key Highlights
  • OpenAI claims an internal model solved the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem by proving finite-time blowup for a smooth forced flow.
  • The solution was found by coordinating agent swarms, with 10,000 agents for the final Navier–Stokes push and ~130 billion output tokens.
  • A Lean formalization was produced, adding rigorous machine-checked assurance to the result.
  • The effort was launched after rumors that another team had resolved a Millennium Problem, prompting an OpenAI race to solve all open problems.
  • Controversy centers on whether OpenAI internal models were trained on user sessions from concurrent researchers, despite OpenAI's claim that no specific user data was accessed.

Benchmarking Qwen3.8 27B quantizations: 4-bit holds up, 1-bit collapses
⬆ 264 pts 💬 128 HN comments

Independent benchmarker Piotr Migdał published a detailed stress-test of Qwen3.8 27B GGUFs, comparing full BF16 (55 GB) against Q8_0, Q4_K_M, UD-Q2_K_XL, and 1-bit UD-IQ1_S/M. Across GPQA Diamond, IFBench, and Terminal-Bench 2.1, he replicates official scores and finds that a 17GB Q4_K_M matches BF16 on all tested tasks, including Terminal-Bench 2.1's 89-task agentic coding suite. The 10.7GB UD-Q2_K_XL loses some agentic accuracy but remains roughly on par with Gemini 3.1 Pro or Opus 4.7. The 1-bit files collapse to random-guessing on GPQA and sometimes emit empty outputs when high reasoning effort expires. Run-to-run variance and effort settings are prominent: xhigh is the default and essential on science tasks. The article deliberately avoids relying on token-level KL divergence because small prediction differences don't necessarily determine task success.

The benchmark campaign burned around $3,000 in Modal GPU time on L40S/H100/H200, exposing an awkward economics for local inference: quantization can preserve quality and fit consumer hardware, but benchmark validation itself is expensive. The author argues that "quantization should be embraced, rather than feared," while noting the nonlinear damage: 4-bit is statistically indistinguishable, 2-bit is degraded but usable, and 1-bit is pathological. HN commenters largely validate the local-use case, sharing configurations on RTX 5060 Ti and RTX 5090, though several call out missing measurements: no 3-bit or KV-cache quantization results, and Unsloth's v2 files were replaced mid-experiment, making exact reproducibility imperfect. Others explain that low-bit post-training quantization is inherently limited unless models are quantization-aware trained from the start.

A 17GB Q4_K_M quantization of Qwen3.8 27B matches the 55GB BF16 model on Terminal-Bench 2.1; the 6.2GB 1-bit quant falls to chance-level GPQA scores and longer reasoning makes it worse.

Key Highlights
  • Q4_K_M at 17GB matches full BF16 across GPQA Diamond, IFBench, and Terminal-Bench 2.1.
  • 2-bit UD-Q2_K_XL at 10.7GB degrades noticeably but remains comparable to Gemini 3.1 Pro / Opus 4.7 on agentic coding.
  • 1-bit quantization collapses to near random chance; high reasoning effort can make results even worse.
  • Benchmark validation on rented GPUs cost roughly $3,000, highlighting the practical costs of rigorous open-model evaluation.
  • Community asks for Q3 coverage, KV-cache quantization benchmarks, and reproducibility caveats around changing quant files.

Mercury 2.5
⬆ 216 pts 💬 38 HN comments

Inception Labs has released Mercury 2.5, the latest iteration of its diffusion-based large language model. Mercury 2.5 is described by the company as the most capable diffusion LLM in the market and, to its knowledge, the largest diffusion language model ever trained. Unlike conventional autoregressive LLMs that predict tokens one at a time, a diffusion LLM generates the full sequence in parallel and then iteratively denoises it, which can yield radically lower compute-bound latencies. Inception claims a 40% increase in abstract 'intelligence' over Mercury 2 — measured through custom evals informed by production failures from search, voice, and coding workloads — while preserving the same low-latency, low-cost serving profile. With a throughput of 1,107 tokens per second on widely available NVIDIA GPUs, the model is explicitly positioned as a cost-optimized frontier alternative to GPT-5.6 Luna (Low), Gemini 3.5 Flash-Lite, and Claude Haiku 4.5.

The model makes bold economic and system-design claims: 260K-token context, $0.20/$0.75 per million tokens, and an 80% launch discount to $0.04/$0.15, plus tunable reasoning, parallel tool calls, and schema-aligned JSON. The blog highlights a voice agent (OpenCall) whose median model latency dropped to ~170ms and P99 from minutes to one second, and Augment Code cutting context-compaction latency by 82% at 90% lower cost. Inception also previews Mercury Voice and Mercury Router, a diffusion-LM-based routing layer. However, the HN thread shows a community split: speed and architecture genuinely excite, while comments caution that the model is not frontier-grade, lacks open weights, and appears weakest in agentic tool use; a handful of users also report that reasoning mode degrades creative writing.

Mercury 2.5's diffusion LLM serves 1,107 tokens per second and can power voice-agent responses at roughly 170ms median latency — for an introductory price of just $0.04 per million input tokens.

Key Highlights
  • Mercury 2.5 uses diffusion-based generation rather than conventional token-by-token autoregressive decoding, enabling full-sequence parallel denoising.
  • Inception claims a 40% intelligence gain over Mercury 2, matching the cost-optimized tier of frontier models like GPT-5.6 Luna (Low).
  • The model hits 1,107 tokens/sec on common NVIDIA GPUs with a 260K-token context, at $0.20/$0.75 per million tokens (80% lower at launch).
  • Customer evidence includes OpenCall's voice-agent median latency dropping to ~170ms and Augment Code cutting compaction latency by 82% and cost by 90%.
  • Diffusion architecture and price create a plausible fit for search reranking, code subagents, and fast voice loops, but open-source availability remains a major community concern.

#11
Large language models develop novel social biases through adaptive exploration
⬆ 180 pts 💬 95 HN comments

The paper, hosted on OpenReview, investigates a new failure mode: LLMs can develop novel social biases against artificial demographic groups (Tufa, Aima, Reku, Weki) even when those groups are statistically indistinguishable. The authors construct a sequential hiring task: the model is told it is a consultant filling 40 job vacancies, each time choosing one of four applicants identified only by group membership, followed by immediate success/failure feedback. Using an exploration-exploitation framing, they claim frontier LLMs overgeneralize from early stochastic outcomes, become overconfident, and fail to explore enough to discover that the groups are equal. This is significant because most bias research focuses on static pretraining contamination, not biases that emerge dynamically through multi-step interaction with the world—crucial for agentic AI.

The HN comments are deeply polarized. Several commenters attack the premise, arguing the prompt injects the assumption that clan membership is a meaningful selection criterion. themgt quotes the task verbatim to highlight the absence of any other applicant information, and WatchDog asks how the model is supposed to interpret the intent other than as a demand to discriminate on village patterns. Others object to anthropomorphism: angoragoats states, 'LLMs do not make decisions, or hold beliefs. Can we please stop anthropomorphizing the token generator?' Yet a few commenters see value: joshuamorton draws analogies to human psychology experiments where participants also overgeneralize with small samples, and FailMore introduces 'context nudging'—a droplet of idea in an agent's context shifting its output—which resonates with practical agent behavior.

LLMs can spontaneously develop novel social biases about artificial demographic groups even when no inherent differences exist.

Key Highlights
  • LLM hiring agents infer spurious group-performance associations from sparse feedback, reproducing human-like overgeneralization.
  • Bias is not just static pretraining contamination; it emerges during sequential adaptive exploration.
  • The task forces selection based solely on group labels, so critics call the setup a self-fulfilling prompt.
  • Standard de-biasing focuses on outputs, not dynamic exploration strategies.
  • Agent-based deployments can accumulate 'context nudge' biases that are hard to reverse.

Navier-Stokes – Tristan Buckmaster [pdf]
⬆ 1789 pts 💬 742 HN comments

The story centres on a bitter dispute between mathematicians Tristan Buckmaster (NYU) and Levent Alpöge (Anthropic) and OpenAI over the provenance of a new Navier–Stokes-related result. Buckmaster and Alpöge had been using LLMs (both Claude and GPT systems) to develop a counterexample in a forced variant of the Navier–Stokes equations, a route originally opened by Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa. They did not solve the $1M Clay Millennium problem, but they found finite-time blowup with smooth forcing for incompressible porous media, Boussinesq, and forced 3D Euler. After sharing progress with OpenAI during publication coordination, OpenAI's internal model unexpectedly produced a closely related result. Buckmaster alleges that private Codex/ChatGPT logs containing his drafts may have been used in training or accessed directly; OpenAI concedes it 'cannot rule out' de-identified data influenced its models but denies any specific user data was accessed. An alleged threatening conversation with OpenAI researcher Sébastien Bubeck has further inflamed the community.

Beyond the binary of cheating versus independent discovery, the comments expose deeper anxieties. Many see this as a sign that scientific ideas shared with AI tools can be silently absorbed and repackaged, undermining academic norms around priority and consent. OpenAI's carefully worded statement — particularly the phrase 'while unlikely, we cannot rule out' — is read by many (including golly_ned and 20k) as either a legal hedge or an admission. Skeptics counter that the timeline is too short for model training, and that OpenAI may simply have used public progress and massive compute to front-run the result. The episode is already shaping behaviour: commentators say academics are moving to local models and closed-source drafts, and institutions may restrict proprietary AI use. Terence Tao's warning that purely AI-generated proofs could be 'net negative for the progress of mathematics' frames a broader debate about whether AI transforms mathematical discovery into industrialised mass manufacturing.

OpenAI's core disclaimer — 'While unlikely, we cannot rule out that de-identified data derived from their usage of our products helped improve our models' — became the flashpoint for trust in AI-assisted research.

Key Highlights
  • Buckmaster & Alpöge claimed finite-time blowup results for forced Navier-Stokes variants, not the Millennium problem itself; route builds on Córdoba/Martínez-Zoroa.
  • OpenAI responded that no user data was specifically accessed but said it 'cannot rule out' contribution from de-identified product usage.
  • Buckmaster says an OpenAI researcher threatened him with career damage if he went public; Bubeck issues vague denial.
  • Many commenters see the 'cannot rule out' wording as a red flag; skeptics say timeline and data-access limits make direct theft unlikely but don't rule out front-running.
  • The affair is accelerating a freeze on academic use of proprietary AI and cloud services for unpublished research.

#3
AlphaGenome Atlas: a high-resolution map of human DNA
⬆ 574 pts 💬 124 HN comments

AlphaGenome Atlas is a new resource from Google DeepMind announced Sep 08 2026. It is built with the AlphaGenome model, which predicts the functional regulatory consequences of genomic sequences. The Atlas precomputes effects for all ~9 billion possible single-nucleotide variants across the human genome, including non-coding DNA, a key underserved area since only ~2% of the genome codes for proteins. Each variant receives an AVI score (AlphaGenome Variant Impact) that integrates coding and non-coding predictions into one queryable value. The underlying data amounts to a 1-petabyte database, accessible via a no-code web portal. Google reports two early case studies: Broad Institute used AVI to find a splice-site variant in DNM1 and solve a rare disease; a UK Biobank analysis reported 22% more non-coding genetic associations and 19 BMI-linked regions.

The Hacker News reaction is split between enthusiasm and domain skepticism. Enthusiasts celebrate DeepMind's scientific mission and the improved usability: "this may not be anything new but it makes using several Google/DeepMind resources a lot less painful." Critics from bioinformatics argue AlphaGenome is basically a cached version of prior predictions and "provides essentially zero improvements over the previous SOTA, Borzoi"; others counter the benchmark claims. Several commenters fear the non-commercial license and corporate gatekeeping ("science is being slurped up"), while experts point to deeper limits—epistasis, insufficient human variation, and the need for direct mutagenesis experiments. The debate centers on whether a massive, user-friendly predictive map is truly a biological breakthrough or a repackaged tool for prioritizing candidate variants.

AlphaGenome Atlas predicts the regulatory impact of all ~9 billion possible single-letter changes in the human genome, covering the 98% of DNA that does not code for proteins, in a 1-petabyte open database.

Key Highlights
  • AlphaGenome Atlas contains precomputed AVI scores for every possible single-nucleotide variant in the human genome, covering coding and non-coding regulatory impact.
  • The database is designed for no-code access, giving wet-lab biologists and clinical researchers instant querying over ~9 billion variants without computational infrastructure.
  • Early validation highlights: DNM1 rare disease splice-site solution at Broad Institute, and 22% more non-coding associations plus 19 BMI regions from UK Biobank.
  • Compressed controversy: HN commenters question whether AlphaGenome is a major step or a re-cached dataset with little improvement over Borzoi/Enformer.
  • Terms of service and planned commercialization via Isomorphic Labs / pharma partnerships raise concerns about public-benefit genomic science.
