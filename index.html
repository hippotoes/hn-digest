#1
Claude Opus 5.5
⬆ 1633 pts 💬 999 HN comments

Anthropic's Claude Opus 5.5 is the first release in a new 5.5 family and represents a deliberate collapse of its former tier hierarchy: it is claimed to match Claude Fable 5.1 on most work while costing 40% less to serve than Opus 5. The story here is token economics more than raw capability deltas — input/output at $4/$20 per million tokens, cache writes at $5, and cache reads at $0.20, a 60% reduction that matters most in long agentic sessions where re-read context dominates spend. Anthropic reports 30%+ faster generation and fewer tokens per task, netting the advertised 40% drop. Benchmarks show leadership on Terminal-Bench 4.0 (66.4%), FrontierCode v1.1 (54.4%), CursorBench 4.0 (57.8%), GDPval-AA v2.1 (1846 Elo) and OSWorld 2.0 (81.8% partial) — while Anthropic itself concedes benchmark margins are "a less reliable guide to real-world differences."

The headline numbers are paired with tester anecdotes that are unusually specific: a 680,000-line code migration finished in under a day, a 200,000-line codebase audit in under three hours versus Opus 5's 20-plus, a HAProxy C-to-Rust rewrite completed in 9.5 hours at 51% lower cost than Fable 5.1, and Quantium collapsing a 38-prompt, four-day task into 11 prompts over three hours. Safety and access are now entangled: Opus 5.5 routes flagged cybersecurity work to Opus 4.8 and biology or frontier-LLM-development tasks to Opus 5, which Anthropic admits depresses its own benchmark scores, and access is gated behind Life Sciences and Cyber verification programs. That gating, the claim of being "the first release since we called for pacing the frontier," and heavy marketing framing drew more HN scepticism than the benchmarks themselves, while the promised verbosity fix was contested in both directions.

Cache reads collapse from $0.50 to $0.20 per million tokens — a 60% cut that, combined with fewer tokens per task, yields a 40% cost reduction versus Opus 5 while reportedly matching Fable 5.1's quality.

  • Opus 5.5 reportedly matches Fable 5.1 on most work at 40% lower cost: $4/$20 per million input/output tokens, $0.20 cache reads (−60%), and a $8/$40 fast mode at 2.5x speed.

GPT-6 Sol and Luna
⬆ 1624 pts 💬 779 HN comments

OpenAI has expanded the GPT-6 family with two cost-optimized tiers, Sol and Luna, positioned beneath the flagship Astra that launched earlier in the month. The strategic framing is explicit: Astra introduced the capability frontier, and Sol/Luna exist to distribute it along the cost-intelligence curve. Both were trained with 'similar methods' to Astra, purportedly inheriting its gains in professional work, factuality, coding, computer use, and alignment, then served through cheaper infrastructure. The headline is a flat 50% cut against GPT-5.6 promotional pricing — Sol drops from $4/$20 to $2/$10 per million input/output tokens, and Luna from $0.20/$1.20 to $0.10/$0.50. Supporting work is mostly contextual: higher default prompt-cache hit rates, a 90% cached-read discount, explicit cache breakpoints, and cache-preserving changes to reasoning effort and tool availability.

The benchmark claims are aggressive and carefully cost-weighted. OpenAI reports GPT-6 Sol at xhigh effort beating Claude Opus 5 at max on AutomationBench (33.2% vs 26.9%) at roughly 9% of Opus's cost per task, scoring 56.4% on Agents' Last Exam, and reaching 68.8% on DeepSWE v1.1 against Claude Fable 5's 69.9% at ~80% lower cost per task. Internal factuality evals claim Sol makes 'about half as many mistakes' as its predecessor. A striking admission punctuates the post: at API prices, daily token usage exceeds $600 for the median OpenAI researcher and $7,000 at the 90th percentile. Meanwhile Artificial Analysis and commenters report Luna regressing ~2 points on coding indices, and Sol requiring roughly 2x more reasoning tokens — turning a price cut into a latency and effective-cost story.

'Valued at API prices, daily token usage has exceeded $600 for the median researcher and $7,000 for researchers at the 90th percentile' — OpenAI's own disclosure of internal coding-agent spend, paired with a 50% across-the-board API price cut to Sol and Luna.

Key Highlights
  • GPT-6 Sol and Luna are 50% cheaper than GPT-5.6 equivalents: Sol at $2/$10 and Luna at $0.10/$0.50 per million input/output tokens, with batch processing cutting output costs further to ~$5 and ~$0.25.
  • OpenAI claims Sol (xhigh) beats Claude Opus 5 (max) on AutomationBench at ~9% of the cost per task, and Luna (max) exceeds GPT-5.6 Sol (medium) on OSWorld 2.0 at one tenth the cost.
  • Prompt caching is the quiet infrastructure story: 90% cached-read discounts, explicit cache breakpoints, and cache-preserving reasoning-effort/tool toggles; GitHub reports >50% reduction in freshly processed prompt tokens for Copilot.
  • Skeptics note the price cut may partly reflect a tradeoff — Artificial Analysis reports GPT-6 Luna (max) down 2 points on its Coding Agent Index, and commenters observe Sol consuming ~2x more reasoning tokens (and ~2x latency) than GPT-5.6.
  • GPT-5.6 Terra appears deprecated, and community reports suggest subscription usage allowances rise alongside the API cut, per an OpenAI staffer's X post cited in-thread.

OpenAI GPT–6 Astra breaks Enigma message that has resisted solution since 2005
⬆ 690 pts 💬 407 HN comments

The MVUEH message — Nr. 172 in the SS-Totenkopf Quartiermeister log for 10 July 1941, transmitted by station "2ny" and received at 17:30 — had resisted every attack since its publication on Crypto Cellar Research in 2005. Cryptanalytically it is a pathological case, not merely a hard one: the sender used a key entirely divorced from the day's traffic, the transcription of the ciphertext from the original message form contained several corruption errors, and the left-hand rotor executes a turnover at letter 72, a rare event that shifts the substitution alphabet mid-message and reliably defeats fixed-position Turing Bombe cribs. Worse, the natural heuristic — that MVUEH shares the daily key — was actively wrong, so decades of search were aimed at the wrong region of the key space. Carter Leffer validated the break produced by GPT-6 Astra, which had autonomously triaged the list of unbroken messages, selected MVUEH as most promising, hypothesised a plaintext relationship to the previously broken Nr. 173 (SIPVX), and then built its own Enigma simulator and Bombe in Python and C++ before running the ROSENOW ROSENOW crib. The article is explicit that this was not a retrieval task: "the most astonishing thing about this break is that the GPT–6 Astra did it entirely on its own."

The recovered key is the strongest evidence of genuine search rather than leakage: wheel order 253, versus 512 for the other two 10 July messages, with different Stecker and Ringstellung. The plaintext is near-identical to SIPVX — 82 letters to SIPVX's 94, the difference explained by a mis-enciphered "Bitte" rendered as "Btte" and a repeated sender signature, "Waschbusch" — which is exactly why a cross-message crib was viable once the right hypothesis existed. Astra's logs also show archive-research behaviour: it traced the corpus to a private collection, named Bundesarchiv volumes RS 3–3/20a and RS 3–3/63b, and located Frode Weierud's own July 2026 note on the Nachschubführer message list. Weierud, a career cryptanalyst, notes that "what it has achieved in two days would take a human researcher weeks or even months," while Hedging that he is "still analysing the GPT–6 Astra logs." The HN thread (690 points, 407 comments) split sharply between awe at autonomous tool-building and suspicion that the result was reachable by simpler means or was contaminated by training data.

The MVUEH key used wheel order 253 and a fresh Stecker set — entirely different from the 512 daily key — and its plaintext differs from the already-broken SIPVX by only twelve letters, the gap explained by a mis-enciphered "Bitte" typed as "Btte" and a duplicated "Waschbusch" signature.

Key Highlights
  • Message MVUEH (Nr. 172, 10 July 1941) had been unbroken since 2005 for three compounding reasons: a non-daily key, ciphertext transcription errors, and a rare left-rotor turnover at letter 72.
  • GPT-6 Astra autonomously selected the target, generated its own Enigma simulator and Bombe in Python and C++ (reportedly developed entirely on its own), and used the repeated place name ROSENOW ROSENOW as a crib.
  • The recovered key used wheel order 253 rather than the day's 512, contradicting the assumption that held earlier attacks back; plaintext is near-identical to the SIPVX message broken by Alex Shovkoplyas in 2017.
  • Astra's logs show archive-research behaviour, citing Bundesarchiv volumes RS 3–3/20a and RS 3–3/63b and a private collection, though it is unclear whether it accessed the scans or merely inferred the references.
  • The crypto researcher estimates two days of AI work replaced weeks or months of human effort, while cautioning that log analysis is ongoing and the mechanism of the break is not yet fully understood.

Microsoft killed FoxPro in 2007. Anyway, here's FoxPro revived
⬆ 385 pts 💬 211 HN comments

FoxDev Studio is not an emulator wrapping the 2007 binary — it is a from-scratch reimplementation of the Visual FoxPro 9 language, and the architecture is the story. The runtime is a Rust bytecode VM compiled to WebAssembly, so one machine executes user p-code wherever the application runs; a running program is modelled as a fiber that yields rather than calls out and blocks, which is how MESSAGEBOX() can stop execution without freezing the window behind it and READ EVENTS can wait without spinning. The editor lints through that very compiler, so what it underlines and what the runtime refuses cannot drift apart. A running form is a live object tree rendered by React, with per-property repaints instead of whole-form redraws. A separate 32-bit host process (SET LIBRARY TO fllhost.exe) holds legacy .fll images with synchronous calls, while DECLARE...DLL reaches modern 64-bit libraries in-process. FoxScript then layers LAMBDA blocks and an HTTP server namespace onto the same runtime.

Fidelity here is measured rather than asserted: 1,722 elements of the VFP9 language reference are known to the runtime, 1,534 are exercised by tests comparing answers against the product itself, and only three remain unmet. SAML: A fractal of bad design

Trail of Bits' Matt Schwager argues that SAML—the XML-based Security Assertion Markup Language standardized by OASIS in 2002—has outlived its usefulness and should be deprecated in favor of OpenID Connect. SAML sits atop XML, which itself drags in XXE, billion-laughs entity expansion, DTD-based SSRF, and injection surfaces across XPath, XSLT, and CDATA, before any SAML-specific logic runs. Worse, its enveloped signature model requires canonicalization (C14N)—normalizing arbitrary XML into byte-equivalent representations so signatures verify—a process whose inevitable parser differentials and round-trip bugs have produced a steady 20-year drip of authentication bypasses, from the 2012 'On Breaking SAML' paper to 2025's GitHub Enterprise libxml2 quirks and SAML roulette disclosures.

The article also indicts the protocol's governance history: it fused four competing vendor submissions (Netegrity's S2ML, Securant's AuthXML, VeriSign's X-TASS, Jamcracker's ITML) into a kitchen-sink spec where 99% of real deployments use a shape that avoids 90% of the standard—a violation of YAGNI with permanent security cost. Ossification compounds this: SAML predates HTTP/TLS ubiquity, so it bakes confidentiality and trust into the payload rather than the transport, and it assumed disconnected network topologies. OIDC, by contrast, grew as a constellation of RFCs solving specific problems incrementally—JOSE (2015), PKCE (2015), device grant (2019), DPoP (2023), SPA PKCE (2026)—and can punt transport security to HTTPS while using backchannel exchange to shrink payloads. Schwager's migration prescription is pragmatic: vendors should refuse new SAML integrations, as Fly.io and Tailscale reportedly have, and move existing customers to equivalent OIDC configs on a published sunset schedule.

Thomas Ptacek's framing cuts to the heart of it: SAML 'is mostly straightforward to understand, but it's built on a foundation of sand, bone dust, and ash; it works … if you assume XML signature validation is reliable. But XML signature validation is deeply cursed.'

Key Highlights
  • Canonicalization and enveloped signatures are the root cause of recurring SAML bypasses: modifying the payload you're signing makes byte-equivalent representation nearly impossible, fueling two decades of XML Signature Wrapping (XSW) attacks.
  • SAML's XML substrate imports an entire pre-existing vulnerability surface (XXE, billion laughs, DTD/SSRF, injection classes) that every SAML library must mitigate before implementing any SAML semantics.
  • The protocol is ossified around pre-HTTP/TLS, pre-mobile, pre-zero-trust assumptions, forcing payload-level security and artifact bindings that modern transport-layer trust makes redundant.
  • OIDC's organic RFC-by-RFC evolution (JOSE 2015, PKCE 2015, RFC 8252 2017, RFC 8628 2019, DPoP 2023, RFC 10017 2026) contrasts favorably with SAML's design-by-committee kitchen sink.
  • Deprecation is framed as viable: refuse new SAML integrations (Fly.io and Tailscale allegedly hold the line) and migrate existing customers to OIDC with a published sunset date.
