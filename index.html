GPT-6 Astra
⬆ 1900 pts 💬 1716 HN comments

GPT-6 Astra is OpenAI's new frontier model, bundling pre-training, reinforcement-learning and alignment advances into an agent-centric system. In bold claims, the release states Astra saturates FrontierMath Tier 4 (98%), ARC-AGI-3 (99.9%) and ExploitBench (100%), while scoring 72.6% on OSWorld 2.0 in ~40 min/task vs. Sol's 65.7% in ~75 min, a 47% efficiency jump. The product stories emphasize autonomous computer use—generating and QA'ing websites, manipulating spreadsheets, navigating difficult real-world tasks like finding pediatricians or scheduling DMV appointments. An engineering highlight is Codex's new context-note mechanism: rather than compressing every session into summaries, Astra can retain searchable prior contexts across windows, which matters for complex refactors. On alignment, OpenAI touts a 0% boundary-violation result on an impossible-task test compared to Sol's 48%. But whether those scores are reproducible, or an artefact of a special Responses-API harness, is already contested.

Astra is rolling out first to selected organizations, with broad ChatGPT Plus/Pro/Business/Enterprise and API access in the coming days; API pricing is $10/$50 per million tokens, plus a 2x-speed 'fast mode.' Deep claims also include a newly certified prime-gap result reducing the bound to 186, and a computer-use model that can operate at human parity in ARC-AGI-3 action efficiency. Under its Preparedness Framework, OpenAI now classifies Astra as meeting the 'Critical threshold' in cybersecurity, though production safeguards will refuse advanced exploit PoC development—OpenAI Daybreak will offer less restrictive tools later. K2 Horizon: A connected fleet of six open models
⬆ 305 pts 💬 108 HN comments

K2 Horizon is IFM's bid to prove that frontier-class AI can be released as an open scientific instrument rather than an opaque checkpoint. The release consists of six connected models — 375B-A23B and 36B-A4B sparse MoE designs, a dense 32B, and dense 7B/3.7B/0.9B — all under Apache 2.0, bundled with intermediate checkpoints, training code, configurations, fine-grained logs, and data recipes. The 36B-A4B introduces MoVA, a mixture-of-value-attention layer that adds sparse expert routing to attention, narrowing the gap to dense-32B quality at roughly one-eighth the active parameters. Pretraining used about 20-22 trillion tokens, with nearly 17% explicit reasoning traces and about 10 trillion synthetic tokens generated by millions of diversity knobs and retrieval seeds; Wzip, a new compressor, was built to measure diversity at corpus scale.

The thread reads as a reality check on hype. Community reviewers respect the transparency but question whether benchmarks reflect real-world usefulness: a11r notes the dense 32B trails Qwen3.8 27B on IFM's own chart, and mmastrac criticizes comparisons that swap opponents per task. More worrying are hands-on failures — cogman10 found the 3.7B hallucinated nonexistent APIs and looped on a basic coding test. On the optimistic side, kennywinker highlights that hard-to-ignore 70.6 SWE-bench score for 7B approaches the 70.0 of Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, potentially unlocking coding on 8GB VRAM machines. Launch logistics also drew fire: the submitted URL hit a login wall, some repos appeared empty, and llama.cpp support lagged.

K2 Horizon's 0.9B model—built for watches and glasses—scores above 48 on AIME 2026 while retaining tool-use and agentic skills, a concrete demonstration of how far capability-per-parameter has come.

Key Highlights
  • Six connected models from 0.9B to 375B-A23B, all Apache-2.0, with checkpoints, code, data recipes, and fine-grained logs.
  • MoVA extends sparse expert routing to attention, letting 36B-A4B approach dense 32B quality at roughly 4B active parameters.
  • Pretraining spans roughly 20-22T tokens, including ~17% explicit reasoning traces and ~10T synthetic tokens; Wzip measures corpus diversity.
  • Post-training is released as a branching development tree covering mid-training, SFT, merging, and RL agentic training.
  • Uno diffusion adapters provide lossless parallel decoding acceleration delivered as a LoRA adapter for the autoregressive model.

⬆ 591 pts 💬 196 HN comments

Cerebras has added Qwen 3.8 27B, the 27B-parameter open-weight model, to its public inference endpoint, claiming around 1500 tokens/s. This is part of Cerebras's wafer-scale engine product, which exploits on-chip SRAM to avoid the off-chip memory bandwidth bottlenecks of conventional GPU accelerators. The model runs on the paid tier with up to 128k context, while the free tier is capped at 64k. Qwen 3.8 27B is a reasoning-focused model with a small enough footprint to run locally on high-end consumer hardware, yet Cerebras promises an order-of-magnitude speedup. The company also stresses that models are stored with selective weight-only quantization and dequantized on the fly, preserving full-precision activations and KV cache, and that no architecture-altering pruning is applied to public endpoints.

The HN community is deeply divided. Enthusiasts see the raw token rate as a leapfrog in inference UX, but many developers reject the implementation as impractical. Critiques center on tight rate limits, lack of prompt-caching discounts, and cost behavior for agentic coding. One user reported hitting the 450k TPM limit in 90 seconds and burning $1.10 before the task completed; another's session was 5.6x more expensive than OpenRouter while only 2.8x faster. Local alternatives on RTX 5090 with ninfer reached 200–600 tok/s, removing rate-limit frustration. There is also widespread irritation with Cerebras's business practices: Gemma 4 models disappeared without notice, billing is restricted for some accounts, and the 'sold out' coding plan makes the offering feel more like a demo than a dependable service.

1500 tokens/s sounds amazing until you hit a 150k–450k token-per-minute cap: one developer burned through $1.10 in 90 seconds before the task finished.

  • Cerebras publishes Qwen 3.8 27B at ~1500 tokens/s on its public API, with 128k paid context and ~64k free context.
  • Public endpoints use unpruned models with selective weight-only quantization, dequantized on the fly to full precision for compute.

Porting my 1993 Amiga game to Godot, with an LLM reading the 68000 assembly
⬆ 313 pts 💬 102 HN comments

Rabah Shihab's 2026 project resurrects his 1993 Amiga game Babylonian Twins in Godot via Claude Code (Claude Fable 5). The original was written in pure 68000 assembly, running on a 512KB Amiga 500 in sanctions-era Baghdad, with direct hardware register access, custom copper lists and blitter routines, and no OS calls during gameplay. The LLM operated in a terminal with filesystem access, enabling it to edit sources, run the vasm assembler and FS-UAE emulator, and diff outputs. It reproduced shipped 1993 binaries byte-for-byte, discovered the disk images were actually memory snapshots of a running game, recovered undocumented level formats and cell property semantics, and rebuilt the game in Godot at 50Hz. The control task—porting the author's own 34,000-line C++ engine—took an evening to reach playable state, with several more days of feel-tuning afterward.

A high point: five level maps were rendered pixel-identical once copper sky and water cycling effects were accounted for, with zero differing pixels across 9,600-pixel-wide level 1 maps. The project preserved vintage frame-rate physics too: 1993 runs at 50Hz, the 2010 C++ version at 60Hz, with hand-tuned drag constants untouched so feel doesn't drift. The author notes the most important discovery was that doors were never painted into map data—they were stamped at runtime by code. Shihab calls the game the first commercial release from Iraq; the original 1993 ADFs are now free on itch.io while the new Definitive Edition ships on mobile and later Steam. Some HN readers were put off by AI-assisted prose, but most found the archaeology story too compelling to ignore.

"From then on, every claim about this game could be settled by comparing bytes" — a byte-identical rebuild that the author admits he would never have attempted himself in eighteen years.

Key Highlights
  • Claude Code reproduced 72,758 lines of 68000 assembly byte-for-byte against shipped binaries, bridging ASM-One/vasm dialect differences and the 'memory snapshot' anomaly.
  • The LLM also ported the author's existing 34,000-line C++ engine from 2010 into Godot in roughly a single evening.
  • It reverse-engineered undocumented formats: tile maps, 16-bit cell properties, object tables with routine offsets, and runtime-stamped doors.
  • Temporal integrity was preserved by keeping 50Hz and 60Hz code paths separate; the player avoided Godot's CharacterBody2D to retain the original's custom collision.
  • The game is the first commercial title made in Iraq; original ADFs were released free on itch.io alongside the new Godot-based Definitive Edition.

.name Termination
⬆ 1946 pts 💬 484 HN comments

On 28 July 2026 ICANN approved Verisign's proposal to terminate every third-level domain under the .name TLD. The affected namespace is the older xxx.yyy.name registration model, which predates second-level .name domains. Neil Fraser registered neil.fraser.name in 2002, specifically choosing .name because the registry was then run by Global Name Registry rather than Verisign; Verisign later absorbed it. His quarter-century-old website, email address, APIs, and the many accounts bound to that email are scheduled to disappear in February, despite the domain being registered and paid for until 2040. If the now vacant second-level domains such as fraser.name are released for general registration, someone else could recreate neil.fraser.name and use it to hijack Fraser's accounts, IoT devices, and authentication identity. The article notes that 22,000 people are in the same position.

The technical and governance issues compound each other: Verisign's RSEP application insists the proposal has no effect on the life cycle of domain names, a claim commenters mock as equivalent to an internal changelog entry of 'Risk: none, Rollback plan: N/A'. Signals passing through those irregularities become distorted and diffracted, causing rapid fluctuations in GPS signal strength at ground level. Across roughly 80–120° west longitude, horizontal positioning errors exceeded 10 meters (33 feet). Mid-latitude ionospheric scintillation is usually mild and localized, but an expanding auroral oval dragged high-latitude plasma instabilities far south, disrupting technologies that depend on precision positioning, including autonomous vehicles and precision agriculture.

The stakes are concrete: the researchers note that even one-to-two-meter errors can cripple geofenced delivery operations and automated farming, and the May 2024 solar storm is estimated to have cost U.S. agriculture $500 million. The full analysis, published in Geophysical Research Letters, underscores the need to understand auroral energy flux, expansion velocity, and precipitation scale sizes to build predictive models that can protect radio-frequency systems.
