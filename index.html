Typesafe AI raises $870M at $7.5B
⬆ 402 pts 💬 318 HN comments

TypeSafe AI has raised an $870M Series A at a $7.5B valuation, led by Andreessen Horowitz with Sequoia Capital, DCVC and a roster of angels, and with a16z's Martin Casado taking a board seat. The product at the centre of it is "Jev", a family of decision models rather than a conventional autoregressive LLM: small, machine-native, extremely cheap and fast, emitting structured answers natively instead of paying for output tokens. TypeSafe claims a third of the Fortune 500 as customers and millions in production savings. Notably, the announcement post is deliberately unserious, opening with "we find fundraising announcements incredibly boring" and promising "even more machine-native models" plus enterprise features — a stylistic choice that itself became a thread of commentary.

The commentariat's core objection is commoditisation. As armcat put it, "two days after Jev was released there were a dozen decision models, a week later there are several dozen, mostly open source" — citing OpenAI's own Decisions API, Unsloth fine-tuning recipes, and a same-week Microsoft Decision-1 release. Practitioners report running rivals like Laya on "a couple of vCPU and 6GB of RAM" with ~500ms latency on CPU, and nico points to Jeffy's 68 pretrained classifiers running locally. Meanwhile a second meta-thread alleges manufactured virality via the agency doomers.ai, which published a TypeSafe case study. The unresolved question: is this a bet on team, execution and brand recognition — dovin calls Jev "the Kleenex of decision models" — or on little but "the ability to grab attention in the AI room"?

$870M at a $7.5B valuation for a product whose core technique was replicated by a dozen competitors within days — as one commenter noted, "OpenAI's own Decisions API beats it, and you can easily finetune your own. But as others have pointed out, this doesn't matter."

Key Highlights

Pointing AI at archives found a forgotten meteorite, lost rhinos, and more
⬆ 165 pts 💬 84 HN comments

Jesse Waites, a software engineer rather than a historian, took Benjamin Breen's Opus 5.5 rediscovery of a 1615 dodo eyewitness account as a template and industrialised it. The substrate was GLOBALISE's 4.35 million machine-transcribed Dutch East India Company pages (1600s–1790s), Delpher's Dutch newspapers, two centuries of American newspapers and ship logbooks. His homebrew GPU lab embedded 5.7 million passages into semantic vectors in a single twelve-hour run, sidestepping the fact that 17th-century Dutch spells "rhinoceros" roughly fifteen ways. The pipeline then gated candidates through a cheap 'System One' decision model called Jev — narrow binary judgments at a few cents per million words, ~$3 to triage 59,000 elephant mentions — before Claude Haiku translated and extracted dates/places, and a Claude Code agent diffed transcriptions against original page scans. Crucially, the pipeline had to first rediscover known events (Breen's dodo, Laki 1783, Tambora 1815): the "buried wristwatch" control against which null results were validated.

Findings: an 1812 meteorite near Pandharpur, reprinted in the Batavia Java Government Gazette from the Bombay Gazette ("a stone precipitated from a Thunder-cloud... greatly impregnated with iron... coated with a thin black crust"), absent from six catalogues including Chladni 1819 and today's Meteoritical Bulletin, potentially predating Maharashtra's earliest recorded fall by 26 years. Three live Javan rhinos shipped toward the King of Kandy in 1738–1740 — one died in Colombo's horse stable, two aboard the Loverendaal with sworn crew depositions, then papered over with Dutch pigs. And three eruptions missing from the Smithsonian's Global Volcanism Program: Gamkonora 1722, Ciremai 1712, Slamet 1780. The 1808/09 "Unknown" eruption resisted all searching, leaving 1,235 untranscribed East India Company logbooks as the lead. The workflow is being open-sourced as Antiquity.

Two minutes a page, eight hours a day, five days a week would take ~70 years to read the VOC archive by hand; the GPU embedded all 5.7 million passages in one twelve-hour overnight run — with a filter model triaging 59,000 elephant mentions for roughly $3.

Key Highlights
  • Semantic embeddings over 5.7M passages solved the historical-spelling variance problem that keyword search cannot touch
  • A cheap narrow 'System One' model (Jev) as a gating filter cut cost and latency dramatically versus running every candidate through Haiku
  • The 'buried wristwatch' control — requiring the pipeline to rediscover the dodo, Laki and Tambora before trusting a null result — is the methodological core
  • Three candidate discoveries: an 1812 Maharashtra meteorite, three Javan rhinos lost en route to Kandy, and three unrecorded Indonesian eruptions
  • The 1808/09 'Unknown' eruption search failed entirely, and the open question is whether AI-assisted archival finds count as expert-grade scholarship

What mathematicians should know about the Lean Theorem Prover: reliability & AI
⬆ 148 pts 💬 40 HN comments

In a guest post on Terence Tao's blog, Thomas Hales (of Kepler conjecture fame) offers mathematicians a field guide to Lean, the most popular interactive theorem prover, developed by Leo de Moura in 2013 and built on the Calculus of Inductive Constructions. Lean's mathlib now holds nearly 300,000 theorems, 100,000 definitions, and 2.5 million lines across 700+ contributors, and every proof is elaborated then checked by a small but complex C++ kernel. Hales argues 2026 marks the arrival of autoformalization as a practical reality: AI reading papers and emitting formal Lean proofs, with milestones from Math Inc.'s 24-dimensional sphere-packing formalization to Meta's ATLAS textbook corpus. The foundational bet is Werner's 'Sets in Types, Types in Sets', which shows ZFC can be encoded into CIC and back, giving set-theoretically trained mathematicians a translation guarantee.

The reliability story dominates. Hales documents the 'Summer of Soundness Bugs' of 2026, when frontier AI models in security researchers' hands—Ramana Kumar, Dan Selsam at OpenAI—found kernel flaws, including one yielding an illicit disproof of Collatz, and one that produced a short fake proof of Kepler. Mitigations he surveys span ~25 cross-checking kernels (the 'Lean Kernel Arena'), formal kernel verification bounded by Gödel's second incompleteness theorem, and Joachim Breitner's Con-Leche, a Lean kernel with a formal consistency proof generated by Claude and checked by a dozen provers. Yet deep theory remains open: Carneiro's 2019 thesis contains an error, unique typing and Pi-injectivity are unproved, and definitional equality is undecidable. Hales insists proofs require human statement-fidelity audits—the comparator tool checks for unauthorized axioms—and that 'Lean proofs should never be believed until they have been checked by the kernel.'

Anthropic's autoformalization of Fermat's Last Theorem generated 13 million lines of Lean in 11 days—while the same summer, AI-assisted auditors found kernel soundness bugs that produced a fake disproof of Collatz.

Key Highlights
  • mathlib now contains ~300K theorems, 100K definitions, and 2.5M lines of Lean from 700+ contributors, all kernel-checked.
  • 2026's 'Summer of Soundness Bugs' saw AI security researchers find multiple Lean kernel bugs, including an illicit Collatz disproof.
  • Breitner's Con-Leche is a Lean kernel with a formal set-theoretic consistency proof generated by Claude and cross-checked by a dozen provers.
  • Core type theory is unsettled: unique typing, Pi-injectivity, and Church-Rosser variants remain unproved, and Carneiro's foundational thesis has an error.
  • Formal proofs still need human statement-fidelity audits—kernel correctness does not guarantee the Lean statement matches the intended theorem.

Can you use autoregressive diffusion to generate market data?
⬆ 128 pts 💬 40 HN comments

Jane Street's blog post by James Somers details a 2026 summer intern project by Kavish that asks whether autoregressive diffusion can generate market data. The core challenge is that market data is neither fully continuous like video nor fully discrete like text: order-book events occupy a discrete action space (trade, cancel, BBO update), while prices, sizes, and elapsed time exhibit high cardinality and point masses. The model uses a causally masked transformer encoder to produce latent embeddings, a categorical event-kind head expanded to 20 classes to handle atoms like zero elapsed time, and a diffusion head conditioned via AdaLN for continuous targets. Inspired by Li et al.'s autoregressive image generation without vector quantization, it generates one event at a time and appends it to the stream.

Technically, Kavish found DDPM unstable: 88-95% of sampled values across seven continuous targets fell more than 8 standard deviations from the mean, with exploding denoising trajectories. Switching to rectified flow matching worked out-of-the-box, a finding HN commenter stratos123 links to Heitz 2023 on DDIM's divergent 1/alpha term. Atom smoothing re-sculpts spiky distributions to expose and smooth discontinuities, and a classifier discriminates model outputs from real events. Evaluation used 10,240-event contexts and total variational divergence, but autoregressive rollouts still degrade, with the CME US spread widening. HN discussion ranges from latent-diffusion decoder suggestions to market reflexivity and the 18-month shelf-life of trading models.

Fully continuous diffusion did not lend itself to the jaggedness of real market data — Kavish's attempts with DDPM produced 88-95% of values over 8 standard deviations from the mean before switching to flow matching and atom smoothing.

Key Highlights
  • Jane Street intern Kavish built an event-level autoregressive diffusion model for four years of US equities data, treating order-book events as both discrete actions and continuous features.
  • DDPM diverged with 88-95% of values over 8 standard deviations from the mean across seven continuous targets; rectified flow matching worked out-of-the-box.
  • Market data's sharp point masses — zero-second timestamps, pennying, prices at bid/ask/mid — required atom smoothing and a 20-class categorical head, revealing a scalability tension.
  • Evaluation combined total variational divergence on one-step marginals and a classifier trained to distinguish generated events from real ones, but autoregressive rollouts still degrade and widen the CME US spread.
  • HN commenters split between technical suggestions (latent-diffusion decoders, Heitz 2023 on DDIM divergence) and market-efficiency debates (reflexivity, 18-month alpha shelf-life, RenTech data-gap filling).

Cloudflare acquires Deno
⬆ 1276 pts 💬 661 HN comments

Cloudflare has acqui-hired the entire Deno team, and in doing so effectively ends the independent trajectory of a runtime that Ryan Dahl positioned eight years ago as a first-principles rebuild of Node.js. The announcement is buried under a warm 'joining' framing, but the concrete terms are an orderly shutdown: the Deno runtime gets twelve more months of monthly bug-fix and security releases before development ceases entirely, Deno Deploy goes dark in six months with migration support for paying customers, JSR survives with its infrastructure relocated to Cloudflare, and rusty_v8 is folded toward workerd. The strategic logic Dahl articulates is a progression — Deno, then Deno Deploy, then celld — toward a single programming model where compute, storage and coordination are not assembled per-application but built into the runtime abstraction itself. That model is Durable Objects, and it is where Cloudflare's Workers platform and Deno's team now merge.

The technical crux is less the runtime than the 'cell' abstraction: celld bootstraps durability and coordination off object storage (S3-compatible buckets), letting a fleet of workerd instances coordinate without per-app infrastructure, and Cloudflare's rough-sea confirms the plan is to run 'a fleet of workerd instances that use a bucket for coordination and storage' so that self-hosted and Cloudflare-hosted APIs are identical. Kenton Varda frames lock-in as commercially self-defeating — 'Lock-in actually hurts us — that's why we went open source' — while Dahl explicitly courts agent workloads, where Durable Objects supply cheap serverless execution, persistent state, WebSockets and a JavaScript interface in one primitive. The casualties are real: yt-dlp used Deno as its default JS runtime; Bunny's Edge Scripting runs on Deno; and 661 comments document businesses built on a runtime now on a 13-month clock.

"We will support the Deno runtime for another year with monthly releases containing bug fixes and security updates. After that year we will end our development of the Deno runtime." — a one-sentence sunset buried at the bottom of a post titled 'Deno is joining Cloudflare.'

Key Highlights
  • Deno runtime gets 12 months of maintenance-only releases before development ends; Deno Deploy shuts down in 6 months.
  • The entire Deno team joins Cloudflare to merge celld's object-storage-backed coordination model into workerd and Durable Objects.
  • JSR continues operating, relocated onto Cloudflare infrastructure; rusty_v8 moves toward workerd integration.
  • Cloudflare frames open-source and self-hostability as anti-lock-in business strategy, not charity.
  • Migrations and dependents are affected immediately — yt-dlp's default JS runtime, Bunny's Edge Scripting, and companies that moved off Node.

Compiling Rust to readable C with Eurydice
⬆ 104 pts 💬 32 HN comments

Eurydice, developed since 2023 as part of the Inria/Microsoft-backed Aeneas project, attacks an unusual compilation target: rather than emitting assembly or LLVM IR, it translates Rust into structured, human-readable C. It does not implement its own parser or typechecker; instead, the companion tool Charon extracts rustc's mid-level intermediate representation (MIR) as JSON, which Eurydice lowers into KaRaMeL's IR—the same backend that powers the F* ecosystem's C extraction (KaRaMeL). Crucially, Eurydice preserves the source program's shape, inserting temporaries like uu____0 to honor Rust's evaluation-order and panic-before-side-effect semantics that rustc's optimized output would shred into bit-twiddled loops.

The pitch is high-assurance software: verification, certification, and compliance toolchains still expect C, and post-quantum cryptography routines have already been compiled this way, with ongoing integration into Microsoft's and Google's crypto libraries. But the costs are real—monomorphization duplicates generics that idiomatic C would express via macros or void*; dynamically sized types require emitting two variants (flexible-array and fixed-length) whose conversion technically violates C strict-aliasing, hence Protzenko's -fno-strict-aliasing recommendation. Charon is also routinely defeated by modern features like const generics, so Eurydice currently shines only on small, self-contained programs—precisely the code easiest to rewrite by hand, limiting its practical ROI to projects needing ongoing Rust/C synchronization.

Eurydice trades machine efficiency for human legibility: where rustc turns a two-line GCD/LCM example into entangled bit-twiddling loops, Eurydice emits structurally faithful C—using -fno-strict-aliasing to bridge two representations of dynamically sized types.

  • Key limitations: monomorphization duplicates generics, dynamically sized types need dual representations (breaking C strict-aliasing), and Charon fails on const generics.

Project Timestamper, a two-person effort by Yağmur Şahin and Arthur Edelstein, has cryptographically anchored the entirety of Common Crawl's eighteen-year web archive to the Bitcoin blockchain. The cleverness lies in avoiding a fresh pass over every object: Common Crawl had already hashed each capture into CDX index blocks of up to 3,000 entries, so the team re-hashed roughly 134 million blocks over several weeks and submitted those digests as a single OpenTimestamps batch. A Merkle-style chain of hashes terminates in a Bitcoin block header, giving an immutable not-before timestamp of 2026-09-26 for the whole corpus — proof of existence, not of correctness.

Verification is deliberately lightweight: `npx tsx verify.ts --collection common_crawl_blocks --capture 0 https://en.wikipedia.org` resolves in seconds using only a few hundred kilobytes of downloaded attestation data, reporting that capture 0 belongs to CC-MAIN-2026-39, that its CDX block is anchored by Bitcoin block 968666 (`000000000000000000010fb95a4fa547171edc525077191cbb74dc54acfaf0aa`), and that the WARC payload SHA-1 matches `3I42H3S6NNFQ2MSVX7XZKYAYSCX5QBYJ`. Sharp-eyed readers will recognise that Base32 string as the SHA-1 of the empty string — a detail that says something about the demo payload, if nothing about the scheme's soundness. The framing is explicitly anti-slop: as generative models make plausible-looking data cheap, a timestamped, byte-exact record of what the web actually said becomes the substrate for any future claim about provenance. The trust model nonetheless inherits two assumptions — that Common Crawl's own hashing was honest and intact, and that Bitcoin's proof-of-work chain persists — while SHA-1 means integrity here is archival, not adversarial.
