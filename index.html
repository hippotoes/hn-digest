⬆ 499 pts 💬 456 HN comments

Mark Zuckerberg has reasserted Meta's commitment to open-source AI, attacking 'closed' rivals such as OpenAI and Anthropic for concentrating power and, in his view, undermining safety. The technical backdrop is nuanced: open-weight releases like the LLaMA series dramatically accelerated research, yet their provenance is contested — LLaMA originally leaked before Meta sanctioned it, and the ecosystem owes much to llama.cpp's rapid reverse-engineering. Meta's own open-source credentials, including PyTorch, are strong, but the new rhetoric is as much about competitive positioning as ideology, with Zuckerberg explicitly warning that centralized AI power is historically dangerous.

Reaction on Hacker News zeroes in on the language of the announcement. Critics also question the export-control stance, observing that China releases powerful open weights while Meta has lobbied for restrictions. For the HN audience, this is less a triumph of openness than a strategic retreat from a losing closed-model race, though many concede the countervailing pressure on regulators is genuinely valuable.

As AI eats the web, the internet’s collective memory is disappearing
⬆ 247 pts 💬 235 HN comments

The Walrus article 'Google Search Is Dying' argues that AI search is silently erasing the web's collective memory. As Google and other platforms inject AI-generated overviews into results, users no longer click through to original publishers. That traffic loss removes the financial and social incentives for humans to write, photograph, or code in public. Search engines are also becoming more aggressive about deindexing content deemed 'thin,' while LLMs trained on this shrinking corpus begin to exhibit a self-reinforcing decay: AI slop trains future AI, and the authentic web becomes harder to find. The HN thread expands with anecdotal evidence: Google's 'helpful content update' causing 'Crawled, not indexed' en masse, and users describing Gemini delivering confident but catastrophic car-repair instructions. The underlying technology issue is that search once used deterministic ranking of human documents; now probabilistic LLMs interpolate answers from those documents, breaking the click-driven reward loop on which the web was built.

Commenters are not passive observers. Many have moved to Kagi, a paid search engine that rejects ad-based ranking and, according to one commenter, is 'better than Google search ever was.' Others see the existential problem: if no one publishes because AI will merely remix, then Gemini's usefulness is a one-time drawdown of the existing corpus. lopis notes that AI answers retrieve 'random values from blogs and Reddit,' while comrade1234 admits the no-ad model is 'not long-term feasible.' On the preservation side, cookiengineer describes building OpenZIM-based torrent archives to make wikis seedable for LLMs, and keiferski wonders if printed almanacs could return as immune-to-hallucination records. The legal environment remains hostile: the Internet Archive lost its lending case, which ilamont calls 'self-inflicted damage.' The bottom line is that AI, search, and archiving are now locked in a feedback loop; fixing one part without fixing the incentive structure will only delay the amnesia.

We're building the world's largest library and then locking the doors, letting the bots photocopy everything before the lights go out. — luciana1u

Key Highlights
  • AI overviews and zero-click searches are dismantling the ad-supported web, as content creators see no return for their work.
  • Google's 'Helpful Content Update' has resulted in widespread 'Crawled, not indexed' statuses, effectively shrinking the indexed web.
  • LLM training on a corpus that is itself increasingly AI-generated leads to model collapse; preserving authentic human data is technically urgent.
  • Kagi's subscriber model and anti-SEO search concepts are being proposed as a structural fix, but scalability and pricing remain open.
  • Legal decisions like the Internet Archive case, plus ephemeral social media, are creating a fragmented historical record; torrent/ZIM archiving projects are emerging as decentralized alternatives.

Muse Glimmer: 30B-parameter model optimized for always-on local agent workflows
⬆ 1107 pts 💬 606 HN comments

Meta has released Muse Glimmer, a 30-billion-parameter dense model under Apache 2.0, explicitly optimized for always-on local agent workflows. The architecture is designed to fit within consumer hardware constraints: 4-bit quantization shrinks the language model to under 20GB, leaving headroom for the KV cache, perception encoder, and a DFlash-based speculative decoding drafter. Training proceeds in three phases—logit distillation from MuseSpark, mid-training on long-context agent-heavy data, and post-training combining SFT with on-policy distillation and RL. On agentic benchmarks such as DeepSearch QA, MCP-Atlas, τ-Bench, and SWE-Bench, Meta claims strong performance against Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B, with particular strength in tool calling, long-horizon execution, and failure recovery. The release is positioned as a serious step toward practical local AI agents, with near-term integrations planned for llama.cpp, MLX, ExecuTorch, Ollama, LM Studio, vLLM, and SGLang.

The Hacker News community immediately latched onto competitive timing and real-world viability. Several commenters noted that Meta benchmarked against Qwen3.6-27B, not the imminent Qwen3.8-27B, and speculated that the release was rushed to stake a claim before Qwen's launch. Others highlighted that Glimmer's edge is concentrated in tool calling and agentic scaffolds, while conventional reasoning margins are thin. Early local hardware reports are mixed: some run it on a single RTX 3090 with impressive results, while others on older Macs describe slow token rates or looping behavior on debugging tasks. The strategic dimension also drew attention—Meta may be positioning itself as the leading American open-weights provider in response to Chinese model dominance. Economically, the model reignites the debate about local versus API-based inference, with some predicting a return to the 'server under your desk' era and others questioning whether mid-size dense models make sense versus shared cloud GPUs.

Muse Glimmer crams a 30B-parameter agentic model into under 20GB of memory via 4-bit quantization and DFlash speculative decoding, making real-time local agents feasible on a single consumer GPU.

  • Benchmarks show strong tool use and agentic task completion versus Gemma4-31B and Qwen3.6-27B, but the comparison set is already considered one generation behind by commenters.

Show HN: Needle2: 14MB agentic LLM for phones, wearables, smart home and robots
⬆ 318 pts 💬 114 HN comments

Needle 2 is an open-source agentic LLM that compresses a 45M-parameter model into a 14MB binary and runs a full session in 28MB of RAM. It is built on the 'Simple Attention Network,' which replaces dense MLP projections with a fixed Walsh-Hadamard transform and learned diagonals, and moves world knowledge into hashed n-gram 'engram' tables read only a few rows per token. Weights are trained natively at CQ2-bit precision, not post-hoc quantized, and the engine uses a byte-level grammar compiled from tool schemas to skip up to 98% of the vocabulary projection on structural tokens. The result is 500–1,500 tokens/sec on a Raspberry Pi 5 or VR headsets, and it fits in microcontrollers like ESP32-S3. The project targets sub-$200 devices, arguing that the edge is mostly cheap hardware, not Macs and PCs.

Benchmarks show Needle 2 trading wins with FunctionGemma 270M, LFM2.5 230M, and Apple FM at 5–70× smaller, hitting 63.7% strict exact match on Google Mobile Actions and a 93.4% well-formed rate across BFCL v4—though it struggles on out-of-distribution Java/JavaScript and parallel calls. The release includes a WASM playground and a fine-tuning pipeline that retrains the model 'on your Mac/PC in minutes to a few hours.' HN commenters immediately tested the demo, producing both praise for the tiny footprint and hilarious failures like turning 'dark' into lights on or interpreting 'warmer' as cooling to 65°F. Many see the learned confidence score as a practical routing mechanism to escalate low-confidence queries to the cloud, making Needle a credible building block for privacy-preserving, offline device control.

A 45M-parameter model that fits in 14MB and runs in 28MB RAM, yet trades wins with models 5–70× larger on tool-calling benchmarks.

Key Highlights
  • 45M-parameter model compressed to 14MB with Cactus Quants (2-bit weights, activations, and KV cache all trained for quantization).
  • Custom Simple Attention Network with Walsh-Hadamard MLP, engram memory, and multi-lane residual streams.
  • Post-trained on 38B tokens exclusively for tool calling and structured extraction; off-topic requests return empty calls by design.
  • Runs in 28MB RAM at up to 1,500 tok/s on VR/phone-class hardware and supports Cortex-M, x86, and WebAssembly via a single C++ binary.
  • Competitive with 230M–3B parameter models on Mobile Actions and BFCL while using far fewer MFLOPs per token (70 vs 460 for LFM2.5).

Learning more about Claude's mathematical capabilities
⬆ 191 pts 💬 133 HN comments

Anthropic published a detailed account of an unreleased research version of Claude making a genuine mathematical breakthrough while attempting the Riemann hypothesis. Although it failed to prove the conjecture, Claude improved the known lower bound on the proportion of nontrivial Riemann zeta zeros lying on the critical line from 41.6% to 67.2%. This result extends decades of work by analytic number theorists—specifically combining Baluyot, Goldston, Suriajaya, and Turnage-Butterbaugh's recent extensions of Montgomery's techniques with a 2000 paper by Bombieri. The model generated 650 initial failed ideas before a second session, coordinated 60 subagents, ran 2,400 shell commands, and performed thousands of numerical checks. Two Anthropic mathematicians validated the result, external experts Brian Conrey and Dan Goldston reviewed it, and Claude also produced a Lean formal proof—full verification with independent agent review.

The method itself is notable: Jarred Sumner, a non-mathematician and creator of Bun, did almost nothing except send encouragement like "keep going" and "believe in yourself" after an initial prompt to "take a real stab." Claude's work demonstrates that models can combine abstract techniques from the literature into new, non-obvious arguments—treating the whole quadratic form space with positive- and negative-definite subspaces together, rather than diagonalizing. Yet the result also crystallizes the current frontier: AI can reorganize existing mathematical ideas but likely cannot create radically new ones. The paper's acknowledgements section reportedly thanks individual humans, and HN commenters note the surreal inversion—an LLM being coaxed through an existential crisis by a human cheerleader. Lean-formalized verification is the key trust mechanism, but as commenter johnnienaked warns, without it many AI proofs might remain "well crafted hallucinations." The incident suggests AI is accelerating math research in narrow, technique-heavy subfields.

Claude spent 31 million output tokens and 60 subagents to raise a longstanding lower bound from 41.6% to 67.2%—then wrote its own paper and a Lean formalization.

What's the best programming language for coding agents?
⬆ 167 pts 💬 113 HN comments

Dan Luu's post dismantles a widely cited claim that dynamically typed languages are more token-efficient for coding agents. The original analysis, based on trivial Rosetta Code problems, suggested Clojure and J could cut token counts by 2.6x; Google's AI summary even repeated that 'dynamic languages generally have a lower LLM token cost than traditional statically typed languages.' Luu's own evals—building a complete zstd decoder from the RFC and porting Pandoc—using GPT-5.6 Sol show the opposite pattern. At medium effort, dynamic languages appear cheaper and more successful; at ultra effort, static languages catch up and sometimes dominate. The extreme ratios vanish except for assembly and obscure languages. Luu pre-registered guesses, including 95% confidence that the static/dynamic claim wouldn't hold, and the results validated that skepticism.

Equally important, Luu's eval-design critique highlights confounders in existing benchmarks: one test ran the wrong executable, and a Go agent symlinked a non-existent path, causing all later tests to run Go's binary. Clojure's zstd failures traced to a byte-conversion bug. Popularity showed a weak-to-moderate positive correlation with correctness and cost, supporting the view that mainstream languages benefit from training and RL data. Community comments reinforce the nuance: a MirrorCode study found no inter-language solve-rate differences across 19 long-horizon tasks, while several engineers argue Go's idiomatic simplicity or strong static tooling matters more than token density. The practical upshot for engineering teams is to choose a mainstream language with good tooling and maintainability—one humans will also read—rather than chasing token-efficiency myths. Luu concludes that 'there's no way to look at the score on one eval... and draw a conclusion about programming in general.'

Language popularity, not token density, was the only consistent predictor of agent success, leading Luu to warn: 'There's no way to look at the score on one eval... and draw a conclusion about programming in general.'

Key Highlights
  • Token-efficiency claims for dynamic languages don't generalize to non-trivial tasks; at ultra effort, static languages catch up.
  • Language popularity is a weak-to-moderate predictor of agent correctness, cost, and wall-clock time.
  • Both zstd decoder and Pandoc port evals fail to show a clear static/dynamic divide.
  • Obscure dense languages like J underperform in practice due to lack of synthetic RL data; mainstream languages are safer.
  • The article calls for much larger, multi-task evals to draw any strong conclusions; many 'best language for LLMs' claims remain unsupported.

How Claude marks AI-generated content
⬆ 165 pts 💬 116 HN comments

Anthropic has formally signed the EU AI Act's Article 50(2) Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-Generated Content, pledging that Claude models launched in the EU on or after August 2, 2026 will support machine-readable marking at launch. The scheme combines two complementary layers: an imperceptible, model-level watermark embedded in all generated text, and signed provenance metadata for generated file types such as SVG and PNG. The watermark is intended to survive copy-paste and some light editing. Since Anthropic reveals no implementation details, engineers are inferring a token-bias watermark in the style of Kirchenbauer et al. or Google's SynthID: during sampling, the model nudges token choices toward a pseudorandom green list that a detector can recognize. The strongest technical concern is that watermarking via sampling changes tokens and can perturb code, exact refactors, or low-entropy outputs where the best token is the only correct choice.
