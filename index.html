Show HN: Open-source engine running Gemma 4 26B in 2 GB RAM on any M-series Mac
⬆ 808 pts 💬 284 HN comments

TurboFieldfare is a custom Swift and Metal runtime that achieves Gemma 4 26B-A4B inference on any M-series Mac with just 2 GB of RAM by streaming routed expert weights from SSD on-demand. The model has 26B total parameters but only ~3.88B active per token, with 4-bit MLX quantized weights. The key innovation is a bounded-memory design: the 1.35 GB shared core and FP16 KV cache reside in RAM, while the CPU uses the router's top-8 expert IDs to plan SSD reads via parallel pread calls. An LFU cache per layer with 16 slots reuses ~40% of experts from the previous token and ~57% within two tokens, reducing I/O from 166 ms to 88 ms per token on an M2 Air. The project includes a native Mac app, CLI, and OpenAI-compatible server, and is model-specific rather than a generic wrapper.

Measured decode speeds range from 5–6 tok/s on an 8 GB M2 MacBook Air to 31–35 tok/s on a 24 GB M5 Pro, and 48 tok/s on a 64 GB M4 Max where the entire 12 GB packed_experts set fits in page cache. The largest performance gain came from switching from mmap to parallel pread, boosting speed from 0.5 to 4 tok/s. Commenters noted that the M5's SSD read speed (6,323 MB/s) and larger memory bandwidth (up from ~400 GB/s) explain the wide generation gap. The engine also runs on older macOS versions with a simple Swift flag change, though missing a 2.4× prefill speedup. While limited to Apple Silicon and text-only, the project demonstrates that SSD-streaming can enable large MoE models on low-RAM hardware, with potential for future mobile ports and broader adoption if similar techniques spread to other platforms.

Switching from mmap to parallel pread turned 0.5 tok/s into 4 tok/s — the single biggest optimization in the engine.

  • mmap-to-pread switch was the biggest gain: 0.5 → 4 tok/s.
  • Expert cache reuses ~40% across consecutive tokens, cutting I/O latency by half.

The broader implication is that AI is becoming a powerful cryptanalytic tool, capable of understanding, extending, and even autonomously producing novel attacks. However, verification remains the bottleneck: while the HAWK attack includes runnable code that can be checked in hours, the AES result relies on formal proofs that still need human scrutiny. Green warns against both excessive skepticism and hype: 'If you’re under the impression that these models are “glorified autocomplete” … stop thinking that,' yet also cautions that AGI is not here. For the field, this is a double-edged sword—AI can accelerate cryptanalysis, but it also risks flooding the literature with unverifiable results. The community must adapt, perhaps by embracing machine-checkable proofs and faster peer review cycles.
