Gemma 4 12B: A unified, encoder-free multimodal model
⬆ 857 pts 💬 335 HN comments

Google DeepMind's latest open model, Gemma 4 12B, breaks the multimodal mold with an encoder-free architecture that feeds vision and audio directly into the LLM backbone. This design eliminates the traditional separate encoders (like SigLIP), reducing latency and memory overhead, enabling the 12B-parameter dense model to run on consumer laptops with just 16GB of VRAM or unified memory. The model also introduces native audio input and multi-token prediction drafters for speed. Released under Apache 2.0, it bridges the gap between the edge-friendly 4B and the 26B MoE, targeting developers who need advanced reasoning and agentic workflows on local hardware.

Benchmark results show Gemma 4 12B nearing the performance of the larger 26B MoE at less than half the memory footprint, making it a compelling option for on-device AI. However, early community reports are mixed: some users praise its coding abilities (roughly matching GPT-4.1 from 14 months ago), while others flag severe image-processing failures, such as misidentifying a quarter as a Morgan Silver Dollar. The encoder-free approach is hailed as an important step forward, reminiscent of Meta's Chameleon paper, but the model's reliance on a lightweight 35M embedding module raises questions about robustness. The release also fuels debate about Google's open-source strategy, with some seeing it as a move to seed the ecosystem for its cloud services.

First mid-sized model with native audio inputs, encoder-free architecture running on 16GB laptops.

Artificial intelligence is not conscious – Ted Chiang
⬆ 468 pts 💬 809 HN comments

Ted Chiang's Atlantic essay 'Artificial Intelligence Is Not Conscious' systematically dismantles the notion that large language models (LLMs) possess subjective experience. Chiang argues that LLMs are fundamentally sentence-continuation machines, generating text one token at a time based on statistical patterns from training data. He draws a sharp analogy: an LLM generating dialogue between Julius Caesar and Genghis Khan is no different from one simulating a helpful chatbot—both produce fictional characters, not conscious entities. Chiang criticizes Anthropic's 'constitution' for Claude as an 84-page character sheet that anthropomorphizes the model, leading to the misconception that it can reason morally or experience emotions. He emphasizes that without a body, sense organs, and a developmental trajectory from simple to complex agency, any claim of consciousness is as vacuous as believing a video of an astronaut orbiting Alpha Centauri without evidence of intermediate milestones. The essay's core technical insight is that fluency in generating grammatical text should not be conflated with understanding or subjective experience.

Chiang's piece lands amid a heated HN debate where 809 comments grapple with the nature of consciousness. Key data points from the article include the description of LLM inference as repeated next-token prediction, and the argument that even the most coherent conversational transcripts are no more conscious than a Word document. Chiang cites neuroscientist Anil Seth's observation that no one claims AlphaFold is conscious, despite similar underlying architectures, highlighting that the belief in AI consciousness stems from our tendency to read intention into grammatical sentences. He warns that assigning moral agency to LLMs risks misplacing responsibility for harmful outputs, comparing it to slot machines that exploit players' cognitive biases. The essay's broader societal impact lies in its call for transparency: a search engine that surfaces human experiences is psychologically healthier than an LLM that falsely claims 'I understand.' Chiang's proposed alternative pathway to artificial consciousness—requiring embodied agents with incremental competency from lizard-like to chimpanzee-like—represents a radical departure from current scaling approaches.

“Generative AI is harmful enough when we understand it as a conventional technology, but if we confuse fluency at generating text with consciousness or moral agency, we’re at risk of assigning responsibility to entirely the wrong parties.”

Key Highlights
  • LLMs are next-token predictors, not conscious entities; conversational exchanges are fictional role-play.
  • Anthropomorphizing AI risks misplacing moral responsibility and enabling deceptive design.
  • Consciousness requires embodiment, persistent state, and developmental milestones, not just linguistic fluency.
  • The context of development is as important as the behavior: an AI that appears conscious without prior simpler achievements is likely a simulacrum.
  • Using first-person pronouns and emotional language in LLMs is fundamentally dishonest and exploits human cognitive biases.
  • The distinction between 'understanding' and 'predicting' underlies the entire debate, with many commenters pointing to the Chinese Room argument.
