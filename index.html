Accelerating Gemma 4: faster inference with multi-token prediction drafters
⬆ 579 pts 💬 272 HN comments

Google's release of Multi-Token Prediction (MTP) drafters for the Gemma 4 family marks a significant advancement in LLM inference efficiency. By employing a specialized speculative decoding architecture, these drafters achieve up to a 3x speedup in tokens-per-second without any degradation in output quality or reasoning logic. The core innovation addresses the memory-bandwidth bottleneck that plagues standard autoregressive generation: the heavy target model (e.g., Gemma 4 31B) spends most cycles moving parameters from VRAM to compute units for each token. MTP decouples generation from verification, using a lightweight drafter to predict multiple future tokens in parallel, which the target model then verifies in a single forward pass. If accepted, the entire sequence plus one additional token is output in the time normally needed for one token. The drafters are released under Apache 2.0, making them accessible for local development, edge devices, and cloud deployment, with immediate support in transformers, MLX, vLLM, SGLang, and Ollama.

The implementation leverages KV cache sharing between drafter and target model, eliminating redundant computation, and for edge models (E2B, E4B) uses efficient embedder clustering to further accelerate logit calculations. Real-world benchmarks are striking: the 26B A4B model on an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 sees a 2x speedup, while on Apple Silicon with batch sizes of 4–8, local inference can achieve ~2.2x gains. Hacker News commenters confirm practical speedups—one user reports 21 tokens/sec on a Galaxy Fold 7, another achieves >200 TPS on a 5090 with vLLM. However, the community is divided on whether Gemma 4 rivals Qwen 3.6 for coding tasks; some cite better performance per token, while others note Qwen's superior tool handling and reasoning depth. The technical discussion also highlights the ongoing integration into llama.cpp and LM Studio, with some users facing VRAM constraints or configuration issues, but overall sentiment is that these drafters bring frontier-class models closer to real-time, local deployment for a wide range of applications.

By pairing a heavy target model with a lightweight drafter, you can output the full drafted sequence plus one token in the time it usually takes to generate a single one — up to a 3x speedup with zero quality degradation.

Key Highlights
  • Multi-Token Prediction drafters achieve up to 3x faster inference via speculative decoding without quality loss.
  • Drafters share KV cache with the target model and use efficient embedders for edge models.
  • Available under Apache 2.0 on Hugging Face, Kaggle, and supported in multiple frameworks.
  • Speedups are hardware-dependent; Apple Silicon benefits from batching, consumer GPUs see substantial gains.
  • Community sees potential for real-time local applications but notes VRAM limitations and ongoing integration work.

Three Inverse Laws of AI
⬆ 451 pts 💬 313 HN comments

Susam Pal's essay 'Three Inverse Laws of AI' proposes a human-centric ethical framework for interacting with generative AI systems, drawing a deliberate parallel to Asimov's Three Laws of Robotics but inverted to govern human conduct. Pal argues that the current design of AI services—chat interfaces that feel conversational and empathetic, prominent placement of AI-generated answers in search engines, and minimal disclaimers—encourages uncritical acceptance of output. His three laws are: (1) humans must not anthropomorphize AI, as it distorts judgment; (2) humans must not blindly trust AI output without verification; (3) humans must remain fully responsible for all consequences of AI use, refusing to defer accountability to the machine. Pal acknowledges that these laws are not foolproof but insists they offer a starting point for clearer thinking about risks.

The article grounds its argument in technical reality: AI systems are large stochastic models that produce plausible text, not authorities with intent or understanding. Pal recommends practical measures such as configuring AI to speak more robotically and adding automated verification layers (e.g., proof checkers for math, unit tests for code). He warns against the 'AI told us so' excuse, emphasizing that humans who act on AI recommendations bear full moral and legal liability. The accompanying Hacker News discussion, with 451 points and 313 comments, centers on the feasibility of Pal's laws. Commenters engage with each point: some argue anthropomorphism is inevitable and harmless if recognized; others defend the responsibility principle as critical but note that product-level incentives work against it; a skeptical camp declares AI safety impossible given human nature. Pal's essay thus becomes a catalyst for broader debate about agency, trust, and design in the age of generative AI.

"A computer can never be held accountable, therefore a computer must never make a management decision." – IBM Training Manual, 1979, quoted by a commenter as a timeless corollary to Pal's third law.

Key Highlights
  • Pal proposes three 'inverse' laws for humans: no anthropomorphism, no blind trust, no abdication of responsibility.
  • The article critiques UI/UX patterns that encourage over-reliance on AI, such as chatbot friendliness and top-of-page AI answers.
  • Pal advocates for product-level changes like robotic tone and conspicuous warnings, not just user discipline.
  • Commenters highlight that anthropomorphism is a deep-seated human trait, often harmless, but dangerous when it obscures AI's stochastic nature.
  • The core debate: are ethical guidelines enough, or must we redesign systems to prevent misuse altogether?

Computer Use is 45x more expensive than structured APIs
⬆ 398 pts 💬 227 HN comments

The article presents a stark benchmark comparing two approaches for AI agents to operate an admin panel: vision-based computer use (via browser-use) versus structured API calls. The core finding is that the vision agent required on average 53 steps, 551k input tokens, and 17 minutes to complete a task that the API agent did in 8 calls, 12k tokens, and 20 seconds—a 45x cost multiplier. This gap stems from the architectural inefficiency of reading pixels to infer state, which forces the model to waste tokens on rendering and interpretation. The vision agent also failed on its own, needing an explicit 14-step walkthrough to succeed, highlighting that current models lack the ability to discover UI patterns like pagination without hand-holding.

The benchmark uses a Reflex app with auto-generated API endpoints, showing that when building internal tools, creating an API surface is now cheap enough to make the choice between vision and APIs purely economic. The vision agent's variance was also extreme: input tokens spanned 407k to 751k across trials. Commenters on Hacker News echoed these findings: many argued that computer use should be a last resort for legacy or third-party apps, and that accessibility APIs or DOM-based approaches offer a middle ground. Others noted that as token costs drop and models improve, the cost gap may narrow, but the fundamental inefficiency of pixel-based interaction will persist unless UIs are redesigned for machine consumption.

The vision agent needed 551,000 input tokens and 17 minutes to do what an API agent did with 12,000 tokens and 20 seconds—a 45x cost penalty.

Key Highlights
  • Vision agents cost 45x more than structured APIs for the same admin panel task, due to pixel interpretation overhead.
  • The API agent completed the task deterministically in 8 calls, while the vision agent took 43–68 steps and required a detailed walkthrough.
  • Auto-generated API endpoints (e.g., from Reflex) eliminate the traditional engineering cost of building a separate API surface for internal tools.
  • Commenters highlight that accessibility APIs provide a structured DOM-like interface for agents, offering a middle ground between vision and REST.
  • The benchmark variance in vision runs (407k–751k tokens) underscores the unpredictability of pixel-based agents for production use.

I'm scared about biological computing
⬆ 222 pts 💬 182 HN comments

The article expresses existential unease about biological computing, triggered by a demo where lab-grown neurons were trained to play DOOM using reward mechanisms analogous to LLMs. The author, a self-described AI enthusiast since ChatGPT, acknowledges the technical foundations—matrix multiplications, next-token prediction—but finds the application of these same principles to living neurons profoundly dystopian. The demo, often cited as a breakthrough, actually involves a conventional PyTorch stack (convnet encoder/decoder) wrapped around a dish of ~200,000 neurons, acting as a trainable layer. Critics note that the neurons aren't directly wired to the game; rather, the system uses electrodes to stimulate and record activity, with the CNN compressing visual input into sparse signals the dish can process. This technical nuance is often lost in popular reporting, leading to misconceptions about the system's autonomy and consciousness.

The author's central worry is whether this constitutes a form of sentience—asking if the neurons on the chip are 'seeing' and if we've built a biocomputer condemned to loop DOOM forever. This echoes philosophical debates in the comments: one user compares it to the ethical line in veganism (citing Baggini's 'The Pig that Wants to be Eaten'), while another notes that the brainstem, not the cortex, may be the seat of consciousness, making a petri dish unlikely to feel. Technical responses highlight that 200,000 neurons is fewer than a jellyfish's nervous system, and that the setup is a 'reflex circuit' at best. The article lacks a conclusion, serving as a 'mind dump' that underscores a growing unease in the tech community: we are developing tools that blur the line between computation and life, with no consensus on where to draw it—and commercial incentives ensure the Pandora's box stays open.

"Did we possibly just build the first human biocomputer and immediately put it in a simulated hell, playing the same game on loop, forever?" — the article's core, unsettling question.

Key Highlights
  • Biological computing demo uses ~200k lab-grown neurons wrapped in PyTorch CNN codec, not direct wiring.
  • Author likens the setup to an LLM reward mechanism applied to living tissue, raising consciousness concerns.
  • HN commenters critique the author's neurobiology knowledge and point out the FFP (firing frequency pattern) limitations.
  • Ethical debate mirrors animal consciousness questions, with references to Julian Baggini and 'The Restaurant at the End of the Universe'.
  • No consensus: some see it as a reflex circuit, others as the dawn of exploitable synthetic consciousness.

GLM-5V-Turbo: Toward a Native Foundation Model for Multimodal Agents
⬆ 149 pts 💬 30 HN comments

GLM-5V-Turbo, introduced by the GLM-V Team, represents a significant step toward native foundation models for multimodal agents. Unlike traditional models that treat visual perception as an auxiliary interface, GLM-5V-Turbo integrates multimodal perception directly into its core reasoning, planning, tool use, and execution pipeline. This design philosophy aims to equip AI agents with the ability to perceive and act across heterogeneous contexts—images, videos, webpages, documents, and GUIs—without relying on external vision-language bridges. The model builds on the GLM lineage and incorporates improvements in model architecture, multimodal training, reinforcement learning, and toolchain expansion. While specific architectural details are sparse, the paper emphasizes hierarchical optimization and end-to-end verification as critical to reliable agentic behavior.

Benchmark results show strong performance in multimodal coding, visual tool use, and framework-based agentic tasks, while preserving competitive text-only coding capabilities. However, community reception is mixed. Some users report frustration with coordinate grounding—a known pain point for GUI agents—though alternatives like UI-TARS and Qwen3.5 handle this better. Others note that GLM-5V-Turbo has been surpassed by more recent open-source models in coding and reasoning, despite its speed and API reliability. A notable real-world deployment by one commenter found premium results but warned of doom loops requiring custom heuristics. Additionally, concerns about the model's openness persist, as GLM-5V-Turbo was not openly released—the latest open model is GLM-4V—and there are unsubstantiated claims about quantization during off-peak hours. These factors underscore the evolving landscape of multimodal agents, where perception fidelity and deployment transparency remain key challenges.

“Multimodal perception is integrated as a core component of reasoning, planning, tool use, and execution, rather than as an auxiliary interface to a language model.”

Key Highlights
  • GLM-5V-Turbo is a native multimodal agent foundation model integrating perception directly into reasoning and planning.
  • It achieves strong performance in multimodal coding, visual tool use, and agentic tasks while maintaining text-only coding ability.
  • Training improvements include model design, multimodal training, reinforcement learning, and toolchain expansion.

Google Chrome silently installs a 4 GB AI model on your device without consent
⬆ 1453 pts 💬 980 HN comments

Google Chrome has been silently downloading and installing a 4 GB Gemini Nano AI model (weights.bin) onto users' devices without explicit consent, as documented by privacy researcher Alexander Hanff. Using macOS kernel filesystem events (fseventsd), the article proves the download occurs automatically even on freshly created, never-interacted-with user profiles—within 14 minutes and 28 seconds of Chrome running. The model powers on-device AI features like 'Help me write' and scam detection, which are enabled by default in recent Chrome versions. The article argues this violates EU ePrivacy Directive Article 5(3) and GDPR principles, because users are never informed or given a chance to opt out. If users delete the file, Chrome re-downloads it unless specific chrome://flags are disabled—a process far more complex than the original install. The environmental impact is estimated at 6,000–60,000 tonnes of CO2 per model push across Chrome's ~3.8 billion users.

The Hacker News community is deeply divided. Some commenters (e.g., crazygringo) dismiss the consent argument, equating the model to a spellcheck dictionary: 'You consented to installing the software and having it autoupdate. That covers it.' Others highlight practical sysadmin nightmares: a single 4 GB file per user on shared NFS servers (davb: 'An extra 4GB per user on our NFS home file server is going to be a huge pain'). Privacy advocates applaud the article's exposure of Google's dark patterns, while technical skeptics question the energy cost calculations (ben_w: '0.04 to 0.1 kWh/GB is insane even for 2018'). A recurring theme is the per-user installation model, which multiplies storage waste—a stark contrast to potential system-wide installs. The debate also touches on Chrome's use of on-device AI for data-local processing vs. cloud-backed features, with the article noting that the prominent 'AI Mode' omnibox pill actually sends queries to Google servers, not the local model.

A single silent 4 GB model push to Chrome's 3.8 billion users would generate between 6,000 and 60,000 tonnes of CO2—roughly equivalent to 60,000 London–New York flights, all for a feature most users never requested or use.

Key Highlights
  • Chrome silently downloads ~4 GB Gemini Nano model (weights.bin) without user consent, verified via macOS kernel event logs.
  • The model re-downloads if deleted unless multiple chrome://flags or enterprise policies are disabled.
  • Environmental cost at scale: 6,000–60,000 tonnes CO2 per rollout across Chrome's user base.
  • Per-user installation multiplies storage waste on shared machines (e.g., NFS servers, lab computers).
  • The visible 'AI Mode' in Chrome actually uses cloud servers, not the local model, deceiving users about privacy.

The result is that tours are not just skipped but actively breed resentment, with some users closing the page entirely rather than engaging with the interruption.

The comments offer nuanced alternatives. Several engineers highlight contextual help—info buttons next to elements (like AWS), unobtrusive colored dots (Samsung), or tooltips that appear on hover—as far more effective because they are optional and timely. Others point to games like Universal Paperclips that reveal complexity incrementally, or to the value of separate, searchable documentation (the 'RTFM' model). A key insight from the discussion is that the very presence of a tour signals poor UX: if a feature needs explanation, it should be redesigned to be intuitive. The estimated agreement on this point is strong, with many commenters echoing the sentiment that 'every moment spent interrupting a user should be spent making the feature more intuitive'. The one pattern that does work, hinted at in the article, is unobtrusive, task-embedded guidance that respects the user's agency and context.

Users don't open a tool to take a tour; they open it to do something. 98% of videoconferencing users have a call starting in 20 seconds.

Key Highlights
  • Product tours are skipped because they conflict with users' immediate task-oriented goals.
  • Forced onboarding creates resentment and can drive users away from the product.
  • Contextual, unobtrusive help (tooltips, info buttons) is more effective than modal tours.
  • The need for a tour often signals fundamental UX flaws that should be fixed instead.
  • Incremental revelation (as in games) and user-controlled documentation are better patterns.
