#2
Lovable apps expose passport details and more than 200K private messages
⬆ 921 pts 💬 355 HN comments

Crissy Field GmbH crawled Common Crawl datasets covering December 2025 through September 2026 and identified 159,041 publicly reachable Lovable projects, of which 34,545 used Supabase as their backend. The critical finding is architectural rather than exotic: 24,664 of those sites shipped Supabase database records to any anonymous visitor on first page load, and because the same anon API key embedded in the client bundle also authorised additional read-only queries, the researchers could paginate deeper into tables the operator never intended to publish. The proximate cause is missing or permissive Row-Level Security — Supabase's Postgres-native policy layer that must be correct for every table, every join, and every role before the anon key can be considered safe. When RLS is absent, the frontend-direct-to-database pattern that makes Supabase easy to onboard becomes an unauthenticated REST surface over the entire schema.

The paper is careful about its own limits — the 30% email-bearing-table figure applies only to the 24,664 pre-examined sites, partial downloads understate table sizes, and the sample is not representative — but the concrete cases are damning: a German lifestyle forum leaking 80,000+ profiles plus 200,000+ chat messages; ticketing and food-sales records with payment data and ticket codes; 280,000 customer emails in one instance; AI-generated internal email summaries about tenants; and over 1,000 identity-document records with passport numbers, expiry dates and nationalities. Lovable's head of security attributes the failures to insufficient RLS and points to pre-publish scanning and optional auto-fix; critics note that thousands of operators simply ignored the findings, and that the platform's marketing of "no technical knowledge required" sits awkwardly against terms that shift all data-handling liability to the customer.

A single service provider exposed more than 1,000 identity-document records — passport numbers, expiry dates and nationalities — reachable with nothing more than the anon key shipped in the site's own JavaScript; another app leaked 280,000 customer emails.

Key Highlights
  • 159,041 Lovable projects analysed; 34,545 used Supabase, and 24,664 served database records to anonymous visitors on page load
  • Missing Row-Level Security policies let the client-embedded anon key enumerate far beyond intended public data
  • Exposed data included 80,000+ forum profiles, 200,000+ chat messages, payment details, contracts, invoices and passport numbers
  • Lovable claims pre-publish scanning plus optional SAST and auto-fix; critics argue affected operators ignored warnings and defaults should be deny-by-default
  • Supabase's long-standing anon-key default was reportedly only changed in October, and may not apply retroactively to existing projects

#1
SpaceX catches the Starship to end Flight 15
⬆ 1430 pts 💬 957 HN comments

SpaceX's Flight 15 marked the first recovery of an orbital-class upper stage, with the Ship caught by the launch tower's chopstick arms after reentry. Unlike prior booster catches—occurring around 80 km altitude and ~6,000 km/h—this vehicle returned from ~270 km and ~27,000 km/h, having survived the thermal and structural extremes of orbital reentry. The booster was not caught: an engine relight underperformance among the 33 Raptors caused an automatic divert to the Gulf. Technically, the catch eliminates landing legs, whose mass scales unfavorably under the square-cube law; the ship instead uses lightweight catch pins, and the structure is loaded in tension rather than compression—favorable for a thin stainless steel tube. The heat shield's post-flight condition is the critical unknown for rapid reuse.

Context matters: the Shuttle orbiter was reusable, but its external tank—the largest-volume stage—was expended, and refurbishment was costly and slow. Commenters note Starship aims at line production, with boosters every four weeks and ships every two. Payload figures cited include ~50 t on this flight, 26 Starlink v3 satellites on Flight 14, and 34,100 kg on Flight 13. Falcon 9's booster plus fairings are roughly 60% reusable by cost; a fully reusable system changes the economics even before optimization. Climate concerns were quantified: ~1,000 t of methane yields ~2,750 t CO2 per flight—comparable to a single gas plant's daily output—so cadence, not individual flights, is the relevant variable. The open questions are heat shield durability, ref light without refurbishment, and whether catch reliability scales.

First recovery of an orbital-class upper stage—arguably the first fully reusable orbital stage, since the Shuttle's external tank was never recovered.

Key Highlights
  • First catch of an orbital-class upper stage after reentry from ~270 km and ~27,000 km/h.
  • Booster missed its catch due to an engine relight underperformance; it diverted safely to the Gulf.
  • Catch pins replace landing legs, avoiding a severe mass penalty at Starship's scale.
  • Heat shield inspection and ref light without refurbishment are the next critical milestones.
  • Shuttle comparison: reusable orbiter but expendable tank and costly refurbishment; Starship aims at assembly-line production.

#3
KDE: The documentary [video]
⬆ 534 pts 💬 121 HN comments

The 534-point front-page reception of a fan-made KDE documentary is less about the film than about a 30-year-old codebase finally getting its historical due. KDE's origin is a 1996 Usenet call by Matthias Ettrich for a "Kool Desktop Environment"—a unified, C++-native desktop built on Trolltech's Qt, itself a radical bet that a widget toolkit could abstract X11's raw, inconsistent primitives into a coherent object model. The comments foreground what the film reportedly underplays: the Qt licensing crisis, which was severe enough that Debian could not legally ship KDE at all, and which directly catalyzed GNOME as a GPL-native counterpart on GTK. That schism, born of a licensing deadlock rather than technical disagreement, is arguably the single most consequential fork in free-desktop history—splitting contributor energy, theming conventions, and even init-system philosophy for two decades. The documentary also surfaces a quieter lineage: Konqueror's KHTML rendering engine was adopted by Apple and became WebKit, meaning a volunteer desktop's browser engine is an ancestor of Safari and, via Blink, Chrome.

The comment thread reads as a longitudinal usability study. Veterans trace the arc from KDE 3.5's tightly integrated apps—Amarok 1.4, k3b, kio-slaves—through the Plasma 4.0 trauma, where "the Crash Handler window would pop after doing stupidest and simplest things within the desktop," to the current Plasma 6 consensus of "smooth and invisible." Several commenters credit specific recovery milestones ("at 3.5.x level around 4.6"). Counterweights are specific: "I wouldn't call it stable. There's a lot of small weird bugs around the environment." Donation norms recur—users pledging monthly support alongside FreeBSD, Signal, and Bottles. Technical reach now includes Steam Deck and KDE-in-VR on Steam Frame, Plasma Bigscreen on cheap HTPCs, and KDE Connect as a remote. The most-discussed observation is visual rather than verbal: several interviewees appear to be running macOS on-screen.

KDE's KHTML engine became Apple's WebKit—and thus an ancestor of Blink—and KDE alum Mike McQuaid credits GSoC 2007 as the on-ramp that shaped Homebrew and GitHub Sponsors.

"In a documentary about KDE, it's interesting how many (most) of the people shown using a computer are running macOS..." — a wry observation that drew the thread's most pointed replies.

Key Highlights
  • KDE began as a 1996 Usenet call for a "Kool Desktop Environment" built on C++ and Qt, before Qt's licensing made it undistributable by Debian.
  • The Qt license impasse catalyzed GNOME/GTK as a GPL-native alternative, permanently splitting the Linux desktop's contributor base.
  • Konqueror's KHTML engine was adopted by Apple and became WebKit—an ancestor of Safari and Blink.
  • Commenters measure KDE's recovery from Plasma 4.0 in concrete milestones ("at 3.5.x level around 4.6"), now described as "smooth and invisible."
  • Modern footprint spans Steam Deck, KDE-in-VR on Steam Frame, Plasma Bigscreen, and KDE Connect as a phone remote.

A family tree of software job titles
⬆ 375 pts 💬 188 HN comments

Ian Miell's family tree of software job titles is a genuinely useful piece of applied ontology: 104 titles rendered as a single Graphviz .dot file plus a JSON description map, laid out in five-year rows from the 1940s to 2025 and partitioned into seven lineage columns (Development; Analysis/architecture/leadership; Operations and infrastructure; Networks and security; Data and AI; Quality and test; Support and customer-facing). Solid arrows denote descent, dashed arrows denote influence or common career migration, and each node is independently clickable and URL-addressable. The engineering choice matters as much as the content: because the whole taxonomy is one text file with one node per line, adding a title is three trivial edits (node, row placement, parent arrows), making the artefact forkable and PR-able rather than a static infographic. Miell is explicit that dates reflect when a title became *common*, not first recorded use, and that many carry a 'c.' and a shrug.

The analytical payload is in the recurring structural patterns rather than the timeline. Miell traces Goldstine and von Neumann's 1947 'Planning and Coding of Problems', where 'coding' was clerical translation of a mathematician's plan into machine instructions — work largely done by women, several recruited from the Army's human computers, so the job named the machine and its people named the programmers. He documents the webmaster's 1993 consolidation and ~2005 dissolution into web developers, sysadmins, and editors; the front-end/back-end split at ~2005 and its ~2008 recombination as 'full-stack'; the prompt engineer's 2023 absorption into the AI engineer; and 'systems engineer' denoting two unrelated jobs (IBM 1960s pre-sales vs. post-MCSE senior sysadmin). The forward deployed engineer, hot in 2025 at OpenAI and Anthropic, is Palantir's ~2010 model — and IBM's 1960s systems engineer. SRE and DevOps point at each other bidirectionally; platform engineering has both as parents.

"The Dev/Ops divide has never been resolved. It just keeps getting new names." — and in 1947 coding was the clerical bit while the real work was planning; the coders, Miell notes, turned out to be doing the hard part.

Key Highlights
  • 104 titles in 7 lineage columns, 5-year rows from 1940s to 2025, built as one Graphviz .dot file plus a JSON description map — forkable and PR-friendly
  • Coding began as low-status clerical translation of mathematicians' plans in 1947, largely performed by women recruited from the Army's human-computer pools
  • Titles oscillate between consolidation and fragmentation: webmaster (1993) → web developer/sysadmin/editor → front-end + back-end (2005) → full-stack (2008)
  • Names get recycled with no lineage: 'systems engineer' meant IBM pre-sales in the 1960s and post-MCSE senior sysadmin in the 1990s; 'configuration management' moved from defence hardware tracking to version control to Puppet/Chef
  • The forward deployed engineer (2025) is structurally identical to Palantir's ~2010 embedded engineer and IBM's 1960s systems engineer — old thing, new name

How did Woz write the Apple 1 BASIC before building the computer?
⬆ 262 pts 💬 136 HN comments

The Retrocomputing Stack Exchange question — 'How did Woz write the Apple 1 BASIC before building the computer?' — cuts to the heart of a bootstrap paradox: in 1975 Steve Wozniak produced a working 6502 BASIC interpreter before the machine that would run it existed. The answer, as the comment thread makes clear, is that he did what virtually every 8-bit hobbyist did by default: hand-assembled 6502 mnemonics into raw opcode bytes on paper, tracking branch offsets and zero-page addresses manually, cross-referencing a one-page opcode table. As femto puts it, 'You didn't have money for a second machine, so you wrote your program on paper, you entered it then tested it in situ. Mostly you got it right.' The 6502's absurdly small, regular instruction set — A9 for LDA, 30-50 common opcodes learned by heart — made mental assembly feasible in a way no modern ISA allows.

The discussion then drills into the harder engineering question raised by II2II: hand-assembly explains the source, but not the ROM. How did paper bytes reach silicon? Replies point to a second Stack Exchange thread (Q18321) documenting Woz's use of HP lab equipment to burn PROMs, and to the fact that the Apple I shipped with only the Woz Monitor in ROM — BASIC was typed in via the monitor and saved to cassette. The thread widens into a debate on tooling leverage: dhosek notes assemblers cost '$200, well beyond my financial means'; jgrahamc admits hand-assembling until 1985 and notes 'we have people at Cloudflare hand writing assembly to get specific performance'; david-gpu describes hand-encoding OpenGL ES blending ops for Imagination's SGX 535. Woz later superseded the effort with SWEET16, a 16-bit virtual machine implemented in 6502 code.

'Woz had to be right before he ran anything, and that's a brutal way' to work — the paper-first discipline that produced Apple 1 BASIC, where a wrong branch offset meant re-typing hundreds of hex bytes by hand through a monitor.

Key Highlights
  • Wozniak hand-assembled 6502 mnemonics into opcode bytes on paper because no assembler or second machine was available to him in 1975.
  • Hand-assembly was the era's default workflow, not an anomaly — assemblers cost around $200 and were out of reach for most hobbyists.
  • The 6502's small, regular ISA (roughly 30-50 frequently used opcodes) made memorization and manual encoding practical in a way modern ISAs are not.
  • A sharper follow-up question emerged: how the handwritten interpreter got into ROM, answered by Woz's use of HP lab PROM-burning equipment, with BASIC initially loaded via the Woz Monitor and stored on cassette.
  • Commenters connect the practice to modern engineering — hand-written assembly at Cloudflare for performance and manual opcode encoding for GPU blending on Imagination's SGX 535.

#6
PyPI's redesigned project pages with clearer security signals are now live
⬆ 261 pts 💬 117 HN comments

PyPI has completed the UI overhaul it first previewed in July, and the headline is not cosmetic: the project detail page now encodes a formal provenance model. Every release is classified by inspecting whether each individual file carries an attestation, producing a small state machine — Full Provenance (100% of files attested, via PyPI Publish attestations from Trusted Publishing and/or SLSA Build Provenance linking a file to a source commit, build platform, and build instructions), No Provenance, and a family of warnings: Incomplete (some files attested, others pushed by API token), Inconsistent (files from divergent build systems or repos), Change of Provenance (different repo/workflow than prior releases), and Loss of Provenance (a previously attested project reverting to unattested uploads). The design explicitly implements the OpenSSF Attestations Style Guide, was funded by OpenSSF and built with Kabu Creative, and is grounded in the 2024 digital-attestations work plus a transparency log you can click through from the security tab.

The engineering detail matters more than the paint. Metadata is now ordered by utility rather than verification status and split into 'PyPI data' versus 'Data verified by PyPI'; provenance warnings surface above yanked/archived/pre-release banners, with quarantine taking absolute precedence; the files tab gains per-file provenance columns, search/filtering, and an orange late-file icon for releases where artifacts landed more than 14 days after the initial upload timestamp — a warning scoped only to releases predating the July 2026 rejection policy. With 261 points and 117 comments, HN's reaction splits along a familiar fault line: sigstore-style keyless signing is genuinely popular, but the trust graph terminates in a short list of OIDC-capable identity providers (GitHub, GitLab, Google), which critics read as supply-chain policy that quietly mandates big-cloud CI. Maintainers also flagged operational sharp edges — an incomplete-provenance banner that fires with no email notification, source distributions and wheels showing inconsistent iconography, and Trusted Publishing still lacking reusable-workflow support.

'If you have any of your files published by a trusted publisher with attestations, then you must have all of your files published that way... and CRITICALLY, it does not send you an email or anything like that' — zzzeek's warning about the Incomplete Provenance banner, which woodruffw partly disputes as not a permanent switch.

Key Highlights
  • Releases are classified into provenance states — Full, None, and four warning classes (Incomplete, Inconsistent, Change of, Loss) — computed per-file from PyPI Publish and SLSA Build Provenance attestations
  • The redesign implements the OpenSSF Attestations Style Guide, funded by OpenSSF and executed with Kabu Creative, with a dedicated security tab and transparency-log links
  • Metadata is reordered by utility and relabeled 'PyPI data' vs 'Data verified by PyPI'; provenance warnings outrank yank/archive/pre-release banners, with quarantine taking precedence over all
  • Late-file warnings (orange icon) apply only to releases before July 2026, when PyPI began rejecting new files uploaded more than 14 days after initial release
  • Community pushback centers on OIDC provider concentration, no maintainer email notifications for incomplete provenance, and Trusted Publishing's missing reusable-workflow support

The Telephone Changed the World
⬆ 11 pts 💬 3 HN comments

James Gleick's excerpt from *The Telephone: A New History* uses the 1965 AT&T instructional film *We Learn About the Telephone* as a lens on how a technology becomes invisible. The film—Technicolor, voiced by Mel Blanc, distributed free to classrooms—taught children a sanitized origin myth (Bell, 1876) alongside rotary-dial mechanics: drag the finger wheel to the stop, let it return unassisted, each number. The deeper argument is infrastructural: the Western Electric model 500, a four-pound thermoplastic shell over a metal base containing two brass bells and a clapper, was a standardized, company-owned terminal on a monopoly network. Its two-second ring, four-second pause cadence was uniform across the country. AT&T's genius was not the handset but the feedback loop—subsidized books, lectures, films—that made the Bell System appear natural, eternal, and author of its own history.

The piece's real analytical payload is the network-effect business model stated bluntly in century-old ad copy: every new subscriber enlarges your personal contact, and "if you have not, every new telephone makes you the more isolated"—Metcalfe's law as moral threat. By 1965 the US had one telephone per four people and roughly 100 billion calls a year. Gleick traces second-order effects: suburbs freed from proximity constraints, regional accents softened into nationhood (Pupin's "consolidating power"), a female operator workforce, and Jonathan Sterne's "Ensoniment"—sound re-centered against text. Against technological determinism he stages George Daniels ("the violin doesn't play the violinist") versus McLuhan, then concedes that using a technology changes both person and culture. The phone booth, party line, and busy signal become archaeology.

Proust, quoted by Gleick, on the telephone as "a supernatural instrument before whose miracles we used to stand amazed, and which we now employ without giving it a thought, to summon our tailor or order an ice cream"—the canonical statement of technological invisibility.

Key Highlights
  • AT&T's 1965 film and 24-page school booklet standardized telephone literacy—dialing mechanics, etiquette, eight-to-ten rings—for a device nearly everyone already owned
  • The Western Electric model 500 was a leased, monopoly-manufactured terminal: no on-off switch, uniform bell cadence, ~4 lbs, functionally unchanged for decades
  • The business model was explicit network-effect marketing: each new subscriber adds value to all others, with isolation as the stick
  • Second-order effects: suburbanization, national consolidation of speech, the first large female workforce entry as operators, and Sterne's 'Ensoniment' re-centering hearing over text
  • Gleick refuses pure technological determinism (Daniels: 'the violin doesn't play the violinist') while insisting that use reshapes both psyche and culture
