Caveman: Why use many token when few token do trick
⬆ 760 pts 💬 334 HN comments

The Caveman project is a Claude Code skill that enforces concise, caveman-like speech in AI outputs, reducing token usage by approximately 75%. Technically, it functions as a plugin that post-processes or guides the LLM to eliminate filler words, articles, and pleasantries while preserving technical terms and accuracy. This approach is grounded in the observation that verbose language in LLMs often contains redundant information, and constraining output brevity can, as per referenced research, enhance performance by focusing the model on substantive content. By targeting only visible completion tokens without affecting internal reasoning processes, Caveman leverages a viral meme to make token efficiency both practical and engaging for developers.

Benchmarks from the article demonstrate token savings ranging from 22% to 87%, with an average reduction of 65% across various coding tasks, such as dropping from 1180 to 159 tokens for explaining a React re-render bug. Implementation details include installation via npx or Claude plugin marketplace, triggers like '/caveman', and three intensity levels (Lite, Full, Ultra) for customizable conciseness. Societally, this reflects a trend towards optimizing AI interactions for cost and speed, but it sparks debates on whether such compression degrades communication quality or, conversely, improves information accessibility by removing noise, as highlighted in the paper 'Brevity Constraints Reverse Performance Hierarchies in Language Models'.

By forking from an established codebase, Modo leverages existing AI chat, inline edit, and tool-use capabilities while adding layers of orchestration to mitigate the brittleness of direct prompt-to-code systems.

Technically, Modo's implementation includes a per-project .modo/ directory for specs, steering, and hooks, enabling persistent task management and rule injection without repetitive prompting. Key features such as parallel chat sessions, subagents for multitasking, and installable 'powers' for domain knowledge demonstrate a focus on scalability and customization. Data points like its MIT license, 55 GitHub stars, and community-driven maintenance model highlight its accessibility and potential for ecosystem growth. Societally, by open-sourcing an AI IDE, Modo lowers barriers for developers to experiment with agentic coding workflows, fostering innovation in AI-assisted software engineering and challenging the dominance of closed commercial platforms, though its impact hinges on adoption and ongoing contributions from the open-source community.

Modo transforms AI coding from prompt-to-code to prompt-to-requirements-to-design-to-tasks-to-code, introducing a spec-driven workflow that enhances agent reliability and project consistency.

Why Switzerland has 25 Gbit internet and America doesn't
⬆ 470 pts 💬 360 HN comments

The article dissects the telecom infrastructure models in Switzerland, Germany, and the U.S., arguing that the disparity in internet speeds stems from how each country handles the natural monopoly of fiber networks. It posits that true competition requires recognizing infrastructure as a shared, neutral asset, akin to utilities like water or electricity. Switzerland's regulated approach mandates point-to-point architecture with four dedicated fibers per home, enabling open access for multiple ISPs. In contrast, the U.S. relies on territorial monopolies with shared, point-to-multi-point networks, leading to stagnation, while Germany's focus on infrastructure competition results in wasteful overbuild, both failing to foster genuine market dynamics.

Specific data points highlight Switzerland's 25 Gbit/s symmetrical, dedicated connections at competitive prices, facilitated by a 2008 regulatory standard enforced by authorities like COMCO, which fined Swisscom for deviating to P2MP. The U.S. model, exemplified by providers like Comcast and AT&T, uses shared architectures where gigabit speeds are often diluted during peak times, with prices rising faster than inflation. Societally, this impacts innovation and consumer choice, as seen in Swiss cities with dozens of providers versus American households with single options. The article emphasizes that effective regulation, such as mandating open access and point-to-point designs, can redirect competition to service layers, enhancing capitalism rather than hindering it.

In Switzerland, you can get 25 Gigabit per second fiber to your home, symmetric and dedicated, not shared with your neighbors, while in the U.S., territorial monopolies and shared architectures lead to higher prices and lower speeds.

Key Highlights
  • Telecom infrastructure is a natural monopoly requiring regulation to enable competition, not free-market chaos.
  • Switzerland uses a point-to-point, open-access fiber model with four strands per home, fostering ISP competition.
  • The U.S. suffers from territorial monopolies with shared P2MP networks, reducing speeds and increasing costs.
  • Germany's emphasis on infrastructure competition leads to overbuild, wasting resources without improving access.
  • Regulatory enforcement, as seen with Swiss authorities, is crucial to maintain open standards and prevent monopolistic practices.
