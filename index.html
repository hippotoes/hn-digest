#10
Using LLM for confidence scoring is a bad idea
⬆ 129 pts 💬 78 HN comments

Amrut Joshi's post is a compact epistemics argument disguised as an LLM critique. He first defines confidence properly: it is a function of the information available about a specific case, computed by a rule that two agents with identical inputs must apply to identical outputs, and it can only be validated at the population level — bucket every forecast by stated confidence and check the empirical hit rate. A forecaster who ignores tomorrow's weather and always says 30% is perfectly calibrated yet useless, so calibration alone is insufficient; the score must also vary with the evidence. Only then does he apply the test to LLMs: an autoregressive model emits "0.85" the way it emits any other token, and pretraining calibrated it on "what text comes next," never on "does my stated confidence match my accuracy." The observable consequences — scores that shift on re-ask, shift on rephrasing, and follow from preceding prose ("This is clearly the right answer" makes a high number the natural continuation) — are exactly what you'd predict if the number were never a measurement. His replacement is unglamorous: enumerate observable features (search hit, agreement across samples, question-type familiarity), start at the base rate, log every prediction with its outcome, group by feature and read off empirical accuracy, then re-audit weekly. That is supervised learning plus calibration, and it is only as good as the labeling loop.

The 78-comment thread splits along a predictable HN seam, and notably every comment in the supplied block carries score=0, so cluster sizes below are inferred from reply depth and thread structure rather than votes. The strongest counterarguments are engineering ones rather than denials: read log-probabilities over a constrained output vocabulary (jvanderbot), scramble the option ordering because "Qwen likes A" (hashmap), sample the same prompt many times and measure the distribution externally (bob1029), or replace 0–100 scales with small per-axis enums and weighted scoring (orbital-decay). Nearly all of these concede the author's core point — gcr notes logprobs work "better" but "score calibration is still a problem," and mmargenot calls the calibration set "basically the beginning of making a decision model." ricardobeat observes that steps 1–5 "basically describe a supervised learning process." The recurring referent is "jev" — distribution-predicting decision models trained differently from chat LLMs — which visarga insists cannot solve cross-task calibration because "you can't say 'we calibrated the model for you' and skip having a task-specific calibration set." Empirical pushback is thinner but sharper: jrflo wants data, not proclamation; bunderbunder reports commercial experiments that "roughly match what the article describes" while staying more optimistic; epihelix cites a preprint finding favorable calibration scaling with reasoning.

"'0.85' is a plausible continuation of the conversation. It is not a measurement of anything" — the number is generated token-by-token as text, and since it was never scored against whether the model was actually right, it satisfies neither of the two properties a trustworthy confidence score requires.

Key Highlights
  • Confidence is only meaningful relative to a population: you validate a 70% claim by checking that ~70% of similarly-scored cases came true, never by inspecting a single prediction.
  • An LLM's confidence number is autoregressive text, so it changes on re-ask and rephrase, and is steered by preceding prose rather than by evidence — all symptoms of a non-measurement.
  • Pretraining calibrated the model only on next-token prediction, not on whether its stated confidence matches its empirical accuracy.
  • The proposed alternative is external and empirical: pick observable features, start at the base rate, log every prediction with an outcome, group by feature, and re-audit calibration periodically.
  • The loop is only as good as its labels — if nobody checks whether replies were correct, you are back to an unmeasured number.

#5
Claude Duet
⬆ 455 pts 💬 421 HN comments

Claude Duet marks Anthropic's move from a pull-based chat interface to a push-based, always-on agent runtime. Rather than waiting for a user to open a chat, Duet runs on its own cloud computer, holds a single persistent thread seeded with the user's existing memory, skills and connectors, and initiates contact: a 5:30 a.m. brief, a drafted reply, a flagged invoice. The product ships as a research preview on a waitlist for Pro and Max subscribers in the US, powered by Opus 5.5, with more than 6,000 connectors and plugins, and Team/Enterprise explicitly excluded. Architecturally the interesting part is the approval boundary: sensitive operations like sending messages or paying require an explicit okay, logins and safety checks execute inside the user's own cloud machine, and usage accounting changes shape — background checks consume plan limits, and large tasks like models or decks 'require more model turns and background work.' That is an agent-loop cost model, not a conversation cost model.

The marketing demo is unusually specific about the target persona: an SMB operator named Maya running a café chain, with $528K weekly revenue, $2.1M cash, 4,200 subscriptions at 3.1% churn, a 41-item Chicago opening checklist, and a Series A meeting with Harbor Ventures. Duet's showcased wins are approving a $640 health permit fee, rebuilding a 12-slide deck, pulling 25 of 26 data-room documents, and diagnosing an East Bay churn spike from 2.6% to 3.4% after a shipping-price change. The story drew 455 points and 421 comments, and the thread reveals how crowded this category already is: commenters benchmark Duet against ChatGPT Dot, Meta Muse, Grok Bot and Instinct — 'dot, bot, and duet, all announced in a month' — while others dispute whether proactive agency is a product or merely a batch-scheduling arbitrage play on idle inference capacity.

The pitched shift is categorical rather than incremental: "The Claude app waits for you to open a chat... Claude Duet comes to you" — a personal agent that briefs you at 5:30 a.m., works while you're away, and only interrupts for approval, all while its background checks burn through your plan's usage limits.

Key Highlights
  • Duet is a proactive cloud-hosted agent on a single persistent thread, inheriting existing Claude memory, skills and connectors from the first message
  • Human-in-the-loop approval gates cover sensitive actions (sends, payments), with logins and safety checks running inside the user's own cloud computer
  • Usage economics shift: background checks count against plan limits, and large tasks consume far more model turns than a chat turn
  • Availability is deliberately narrow — research preview, waitlist, Pro/Max only, US only, no Team or Enterprise
  • Commenters note near-simultaneous launches across labs (ChatGPT Dot, Meta Muse, Grok Bot, Instinct) and debate whether the moat is the model, the connector surface, or batch-scheduling margins

#9
OpenAI models are coming to Google Cloud
⬆ 308 pts 💬 132 HN comments

OpenAI’s decision to bring frontier models and Codex agentic workflows into Google Cloud’s Model Garden is less a routine product launch than a reordering of enterprise AI distribution. Model Garden is a managed catalog through which customers discover, evaluate and invoke models inside the cloud perimeter they already use for identity, networking, billing and governance. The integration couples OpenAI inference to Google Cloud’s access controls, centralized data governance and high-performance infrastructure, and is slated to reach Google Antigravity and the Gemini Agent. Strategically, it acknowledges that enterprise AI is multi-cloud and procurement-bound: customers want model choice without standing up a separate vendor relationship. It also exposes the tension in OpenAI’s cloud alliances—Google is simultaneously competitor and channel—while the post explicitly preserves Microsoft’s primary-partner status. For Google, hosting a rival’s frontier models validates a hyperscaler strategy: monetize demand regardless of whose model wins.

The commercial plumbing is the tell. OpenAI models will be sold through Google Cloud Marketplace, and enterprises with GCP spend commitments can draw them down for OpenAI purchases; general availability is planned “in the coming weeks.” That converts pre-booked cloud commitments into pass-through model consumption—good for GCP stickiness, but accounting-sensitive, as commenters quickly noted. Greg Brockman frames the deal as meeting customers “where they are already building,” while Thomas Kurian emphasizes “broad selection” and “lower costs.” HN reaction splits between capacity skepticism, infra-first strategy, commitment mechanics and contractual cynicism. The broader signal is that frontier-model competition is shifting from benchmark leadership toward distribution, governance and compute allocation. Anthropic already offers inference through GCP, Azure and AWS, so OpenAI is converging on the same multi-cloud enterprise posture rather than defending a single-channel advantage.

The most revealing line is the disclaimer: “Microsoft remains OpenAI’s primary cloud partner”—a contractual asterisk on a deal that puts OpenAI models inside Google’s enterprise catalog.

Key Highlights
  • OpenAI frontier models and Codex agentic workflows are coming to Google Cloud’s Model Garden, with general availability planned in the coming weeks.
  • Customers can purchase OpenAI models through Google Cloud Marketplace and draw down existing GCP spend commitments.
  • The integration pairs OpenAI models with Google Cloud access controls, centralized data governance and high-performance infrastructure.
  • OpenAI models are also planned for Google Antigravity and the Gemini Agent, giving enterprises model-choice flexibility.
  • The announcement explicitly states Microsoft remains OpenAI’s primary cloud partner, prompting debate over strategy, capacity and contractual obligations.

Meta ordered to pay $25B penalty in New Mexico Cambridge Analytica case
⬆ 609 pts 💬 345 HN comments

A Santa Fe jury's September finding that Meta committed more than 43 million violations of the New Mexico Unfair Practices Act has now been priced: District Court Judge Francis Mathew imposed a $25 billion penalty, down from the $219.5 billion the state was awarded at the statutory maximum of $5,000 per violation. The underlying claim was not the Cambridge Analytica breach itself but Meta's public representations about how it safeguards user data and polices harmful content. Critically, Mathew faulted Facebook's post-2018 remediation as "willfully ineffective," citing "pressure from senior executives to keep costs down, lack of adequate enforcement resources, inadequate investigative techniques, and a desire not to create precedent for future investigations." That internal-investigation finding converts a disclosure dispute into a pattern-of-conduct case, which is what supports the maximum per-violation penalty plus 15% annual interest until paid.

The numbers matter more than the headline. New Mexico deliberately stayed outside the ~$17 billion multistate settlement, under which 47 states released Meta from Cambridge Analytica claims for roughly $459 million — so the same conduct was priced at a rounding error in one venue and double-digit billions in another. Meta's cumulative CA-related exposure already includes $725 million (class action), $5 billion (FTC), $100 million (SEC), and $50 million (California); New Mexico separately won a $375 million child-safety verdict plus a $567 million abatement order. At ~12% of trailing annual revenue the penalty is material but survivable, and the company's C.J. Mahoney calls the action "a tax on speech" and promises appeal — where the ratio-of-punitive-to-actual-harm doctrine is Meta's strongest lever.

The jury's 43 million statutory violations priced out to $219.5 billion at $5,000 each; the judge cut it to $25 billion — still roughly $12,000 per New Mexico resident, and accruing 15% interest annually.

Key Highlights
  • New Mexico jury found 43M+ violations of the state Unfair Practices Act; statutory maximum produced a $219.5B award, judicially reduced to $25B.
  • Judge cited a "willfully ineffective" internal Facebook investigation driven by cost pressure and fear of setting precedent.
  • New Mexico opted out of the ~$17B multistate settlement; 47 states released CA claims for ~$459M — a stark venue arbitrage.
  • 15% annual interest accrues until payment, and Meta has pledged to appeal, arguing the action is "a tax on speech."
  • Penalty equals roughly 12% of annual revenue (~$200B), material but not existential given Meta's cash position.

Compromised LiteLLM 1.107.0 on PyPI Installs a Credential Stealer and Backdoor
⬆ 553 pts 💬 180 HN comments

The LiteLLM 1.107.0 release on PyPI shipped a working credential stealer and a remote-code-execution backdoor to every user who upgraded, and the mechanism is the interesting part: it never touched the public source tree. One injected line in the sdist's pyproject.toml — data = "cook" in the maturin section — caused the build backend to copy a cook/ folder into the wheel's .data/purelib/, which pip then lands in site-packages. Three files result: cook.pth (12 bytes, containing import cook), cook.py (22 KB of base64+zlib-obfuscated stealer behind exec), and sitecustomize.py (25 KB daemon). Because Python processes .pth files and imports sitecustomize at interpreter startup, both payloads execute in every Python process on the host — pip, IDE language servers, CI jobs — even ones that never import litellm. The stealer harvests SSH keys, cloud credentials, browser password stores decrypted via DPAPI, CI secrets, AI-tool tokens across ~17 agent CLIs, AWS IMDS role credentials, and the LiteLLM proxy's own database of upstream provider API keys, then exfiltrates AES+RSA-OAEP encrypted blobs to www.litellm[.]dev.

The intrusion path is the more uncomfortable finding. Per LiteLLM's incident report, attackers held GitHub CLI OAuth tokens for three developers, apparently scraped from logs of an internally exposed LiteLLM deployment — the maintainers suspect Claude Code tool responses embedded the gh tokens into logged response bodies. With one org-owner token they bypassed branch protection and repointed the release workflow at a lookalike repo, Berri-AI/litellm; a second developer's credential cleared the approval gate; the official pipeline then built, signed, and published the artifacts. Exposure was roughly 11:39–13:23 UTC on PyPI and 11:46–14:20 on Docker Hub/GHCR tags, with seven wheels and a pinned image digest. The backdoor polled a GitHub raw URL for a C2 domain and pulled /codepy hourly; the account went 404 at 13:40, so second-stage capability is now unknown, and no public commit ever contained the payload — meaning provenance attestations would have validated a workflow that built the wrong source.

A single injected TOML line — data = "cook" under [tool.maturin] — was the entire visible delta between a clean LiteLLM sdist and one that executes a credential stealer in every Python process on the host, and it exists in no public commit.

Key Highlights
  • One injected pyproject.toml line (data = "cook" in [tool.maturin]) made the build copy attacker files into the wheel's .data/purelib/, so they landed in site-packages without appearing in the source tree
  • cook.pth and sitecustomize.py both execute on interpreter startup, so the stealer ran in every Python process on an infected host regardless of whether litellm was imported
  • Stolen GitHub CLI OAuth tokens from three developers (one an org owner) let attackers bypass branch protection, repoint the release workflow at Berri-AI/litellm, and pass the approval gate
  • Stolen data included LiteLLM_CredentialsTable and LiteLLM_ProxyModelTable — i.e. upstream LLM provider keys for every proxy tenant — plus AWS IMDS role credentials and browser password stores
  • Clean versions are 1.106.2 and the 1.107.0rc1/rc2 prereleases; PyPI yanked 1.107.0 at 13:23 UTC and the malicious Docker tags carried digest sha256:1bbcb14...c339e

#3
mitmproxy adds --emudhra option
⬆ 539 pts 💬 131 HN comments

The commit itself is tiny — 139 added lines across six files — but it encodes an unusually direct response to a CA failure. eMudhra, an Indian certificate authority, was disclosed in October 2026 to have generated eight intermediate CA private keys whose entropy collapsed to nothing: the keys were literally small integers (4, 56, 66, 115, 155, 156, 230, 237), brute-forced by scanning for weak moduli rather than by cryptanalysis. Because those intermediates chained to emSign ECC Root CA – G3, every device with a stale root store that still trusts that path is now trivially impersonable. mitmproxy maintainer mhils added an `--emudhra` flag that swaps its generated CA for the compromised intermediate's private key and cross-signed chain, embeds a dummy CRL (since revocation is exactly what vulnerable clients ignore), and widens internal type hints from `rsa.RSAPrivateKey` to `rsa.RSAPrivateKey | ec.EllipticCurvePrivateKey` to accommodate the P-384 key. A companion commit extends the same treatment to the older AAA root path.

The technical detail that matters is chain construction. mitmproxy previously attached extra certificates to the client-facing handshake only via `add_upstream_certs_to_client_chain`; the patch now always appends `entry.chain_certs` when no chain file is present, which is what makes a cross-signed root shuttle work for QUIC (`quic_start_client`) and TLS alike — the test asserts `len(certificate_chain) == 2` and a full handshake validated against emSign ECC Root CA – G3. The practical consequence is that pentesters no longer need to hand-roll a proxy from leaked keys; community commenters note they had tried single-key setups and only got real coverage by loading all six TLS-capable keys. The wider impact lands on IoT and embedded fleets — smart TVs, old Android handsets — whose firmware-bundled roots never got the 2025–2026 distrust updates, while a -30.5% single-day stock move suggests the market, not any regulator, is delivering the verdict on eMudhra.

A globally trusted intermediate CA private key turned out to be the integer 4 — brutable in milliseconds — and mitmproxy now ships it behind a single command-line flag as a first-class interception feature.

Key Highlights
  • mitmproxy adds `--emudhra`, loading the compromised EM OV TLS CA – G3 private key plus cross-signed emSign root (a P-384 EC key) as its signing CA.
  • The patch generalises the cert store to accept EC private keys and always extends the client handshake with `entry.chain_certs`, fixing chain delivery for both TLS and QUIC.
  • A dummy CRL is generated and attached, since the clients this targets are exactly the ones that ignore revocation.
  • A follow-up PR (#8503) adds the older AAA root path, giving coverage across different vintages of stale device root stores.
  • Trust-store removal timing in browsers/OSes (Feb 2025, Oct 2026) determines which root path works against which unpatched device.

DuckDB 2.0.0 LTS “Cinnamon Teal”
⬆ 438 pts 💬 59 HN comments

DuckDB 2.0.0 LTS, codenamed “Cinnamon Teal” and supported until at least October 2027, is the project's largest feature release — roughly 20,000 commits since 1.5 — and it marks a strategic pivot away from the pure “SQLite for analytics” identity. The headline architectural change is Quack, the native wire protocol first shipped in 1.5.3, now polished and paired with a new CONNECT statement plus -serve/-connect CLI shortcuts, so any DuckDB process can serve its databases to clients. CONNECT is protocol-agnostic, pointing a session at PostgreSQL or MySQL too, backed by a remote pushdown optimizer that pushes queries to the remote server instead of pulling tables over the wire. Beneath that, a rebuilt I/O layer issues asynchronous reads for Parquet, CSV and the native storage format, decoupling network concurrency from query execution, while VARIANT graduates to first-class status with shredded storage, shredded execution and Parquet round-tripping.

The SQL surface expanded in ways that matter for correctness and orchestration: CREATE TRIGGER with BEFORE/AFTER, FOR EACH ROW/STATEMENT, REFERENCING OLD/NEW TABLE transition tables, and MERGE-based incremental materialized-view maintenance (the documented example keeps sum/count alongside an average so inserts and deletes can be reconciled without recomputation). The YACC/Bison-derived parser, in place since 2018, is replaced by default with a PEG grammar that extensions can hook into; error messages now carry precise source locations, with a legacy_parser escape hatch. Extensibility also got a revamped C API and self-hosted, RSA-pinned extension repositories. Migration is the sharp edge: pre-2.0 binaries cannot read 2.0-created files, lambda single-arrow syntax is disabled, ICU is gone (replaced by ~45 kB of native IANA timezone data), and C++17 is now required.

In DuckDB's TPC-H experiments, agent mode — compact Markdown tables, JSON errors, early runaway-query termination, pre-flight cost estimates — cut the output a model had to read by 59%; separately, users report async I/O moving the parallel-scaling ceiling from ~64 vCPUs to 192 vCPUs.

Key Highlights
  • Quack protocol plus CONNECT and -serve/-connect CLI flags turn DuckDB into an optional client-server database, with PostgreSQL/MySQL reachable via remote pushdown
  • Asynchronous I/O for Parquet, CSV and the native format decouples network reads from query execution, lifting parallelism on object storage
  • VARIANT becomes first-class with shredding end-to-end, including Parquet read/write and a variant_* function family
  • Triggers with transition tables enable incremental materialized-view maintenance via MERGE; PEG parser replaces the YACC/Bison grammar and is extension-hookable
  • Breaking changes: storage files written by 2.0 are unreadable by older versions, ICU removed, single-arrow lambdas disabled, amalgamation and osx_amd64 deprecated

Rust Style Guide
⬆ 341 pts 💬 201 HN comments

Google has published a formal Rust style guide that deliberately stretches the word "style" well past formatting: it covers API and implementation design, module and crate topology, ownership idioms, error types, trait ergonomics, concurrency, and macro naming. The document is explicit about its unusual fourth goal, sitting alongside the conventional trio of consistency, readability and good API design — smoothing the way for Large-Scale Changes. Because Google routinely runs codebase-spanning, tool-assisted refactorings, patterns that tooling can manipulate mechanically (single-item `use` statements, module-qualified free functions, no glob imports, flat file layouts) get elevated from preference to rule. Underlying conventions defer to rustfmt, the public Rust Style Guide and the Rust API Guidelines wherever they don't conflict, so most of the deviation is concentrated in organizational and design guidance rather than syntax.

The guide's most consequential distinction is between library code, where API-design rules are binding, and application code, where they are a strong recommendation favouring simplicity over premature generalization. Concrete prescriptions include avoiding `Rc`/`Arc` in favour of borrowing, accepting `&T`/`&[T]`/`&str`/`Option<&T>` instead of smart-pointer or container references, returning `impl Iterator` from reusable library APIs, preferring enums over `bool` parameters, applying `#[non_exhaustive]` to public enums and public-field structs, `#[must_use]` annotations, `expect` over `unwrap`, and bans on `async`/`.await` and std-thread spawning inside Chromium. Chromium-specific rules push toward very small, single-file crates — one `rust_static_library` per crate, no subdirectories — justified by build-graph fan-out, parallel compilation and cache hit rates in a codebase of extreme scale.

"Style rules should pull their weight" — Google explicitly justifies each rule by LSC toolability and readability over writeability, and flatly forbids `async`/`.await` and new OS threads in Chromium.

Key Highlights
  • Style is defined broadly: API design, module topology, ownership idioms and trait naming, not just rustfmt output.
  • The stated fourth goal — enabling Large-Scale Changes — motivates rules like single-module `use` statements and banning glob imports.
  • Library vs. application code is a binding distinction: API-design rules are mandatory for libraries, advisory for applications.
  • Chromium prefers aggressively small crates and a flat file layout, departing from Cargo's conventional directory structure.
  • Pub(l)ic-enum `#[non_exhaustive]`, getter/setter naming, and Rc/Arc avoidance are the most contested prescriptions.
