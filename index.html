Elephant-Goldfish Model
⬆ 10 pts 💬 9 HN comments

The Elephant-Goldfish Model (EGM) is a Google-published, Apache-2.0 plugin that formalises a context-asymmetry protocol for AI-assisted planning: an "elephant" drafting assistant holds the full conversation, goal, and background, then emits a written plan that is the *only* artifact a set of "goldfish" reviewers ever see. Those reviewers start from zero context and are tasked solely with reporting what is missing or unclear. The name comes from Dave Rensin's essay "Elephants, Goldfish, and the New Golden Age of Software Engineering," and the design lever is deliberate information loss: the plan becomes a serialisation boundary, and anything the author failed to write down surfaces as a reviewer finding rather than as silent tacit knowledge. EGM ships as a harness plugin across ChatGPT/Codex desktop, Claude Cowork (via a Google marketplace), Claude Code, Codex CLI, and Antigravity, installed with one-line marketplace commands — an explicit bet that this workflow should live inside existing assistants rather than as a bespoke application.

The site's illustrative example is a university study-space opening plan whose reviewers flag three specific defects: "The plan names no person and no rota," "the plan refers to opening hours but does not state them," and "the plan depends on a decision that the reader cannot see." That is precisely the class of gap a same-session critic cannot find, because it shares the author's context and silently fills the holes. HN commenters immediately recognised the manual version of this: paulglx says reviewing in "fresh sessions with low to no context avoids the model collapsing over its own slop," while dcss_gardener calls it "overengineered... it really grinds through tokens for something that has such limited tools and context" and bob1029 argues a frontier model "on xhigh would probably run circles around any manifestation of this." Google is careful to label it "not an officially supported Google product."

The core insight is the deliberate amnesia of the reviewer: the plan is the only channel, so every tacit decision the author forgot to write down becomes a reported defect — "Who staffs the desk? The plan names no person and no rota."

Key Highlights
  • EGM splits AI-assisted planning into a context-rich 'elephant' drafter and context-free 'goldfish' reviewers who only see the written plan
  • The plan acts as a serialisation boundary: undocumented tacit knowledge surfaces as reviewer findings, not silent assumptions
  • Ships as an Apache-2.0 plugin for ChatGPT/Codex desktop, Claude Cowork, Claude Code, Codex CLI and Antigravity via one-line marketplace installs
  • Derived from Dave Rensin's 'Elephants, Goldfish, and the New Golden Age of Software Engineering' and explicitly not an officially supported Google product
  • HN debate centres on token cost, whether a plain review subagent suffices, and whether harness-level tricks survive the next model generation

Carnegie Mellon is putting computer science courses on YouTube for free
⬆ 2096 pts 💬 280 HN comments

Carnegie Mellon's Griffin School of Computer Science has launched CS Learn, publishing full-semester lecture recordings from current CMU courses on YouTube at no cost, with one course premiering per day this week and a January slate to follow. The framing is deliberately institutional rather than experimental: lectures first, then self-paced courseware with instant feedback and badges, then embedded AI tutoring. CMU is candid that MIT's OpenCourseWare got there roughly 25 years ago; the claimed novelty is the production pipeline. A handful of students, supported by ScottyLabs, built tooling that automates much of the capture and edit work, letting a small team ship a semester of lectures quickly while still having humans review every recording. That is the real story — open courseware is no longer a grant-funded charity project but a lightly-staffed software pipeline, which is what makes a sustainable release cadence plausible.

The catalogue is technically serious: William Cohen's 10-605 Machine Learning with Large Datasets and Franceska Xhakaj's 15-110 Principles of Computing are live, with Tianqi Chen and Zhihao Jia's 15-442 Machine Learning Systems, Daniel Fried and Graham Neubig's 11-768 AI Agents, and Christian Kästner and Bogdan Vasilescu's 17-645 Machine Learning in Production queued behind them. The forward slate includes Mor Harchol-Balter's 15-259 Probability and Computing and Louis-Philippe Morency's 11-777 Multimodal Machine Learning. The gaps are equally visible: audio captured from a single fixed microphone in 15-110, a nested-CSS gate that locks out mid-vintage Firefox, undetermined licensing ("public on YouTube is not the same as open"), and no assignments yet. The longer-term threat is that lectures alone are a commodity an LLM can summarize — the moat is graded work, feedback, and credentials.

"Lectures are nice, but without the projects and some kind of way to get feedback on it, lectures can fall flat a bit" — the community's diagnosis is that CMU's real future asset is the unshipped courseware, not the 2,096-upvote announcement.

Key Highlights
  • CMU's Griffin School of Computer Science launched CS Learn, releasing full-semester lecture recordings free on YouTube under a CC-style open-courseware model.
  • The differentiator claimed by program lead Phillip Compeau is student-built production automation with human review, not the concept itself — MIT OCW predates it by ~25 years.
  • Course rollout spans AI/ML systems, software construction, ML in production, and AI agents, with courseware, badges, and AI tutoring promised as phase two and three.
  • Critics flagged a single fixed microphone in 15-110, a nested-CSS browser lockout, hidden/unavailable videos, and no stated open license.
  • Commenters want assignments, slide decks, advanced courses (database internals, distributed systems, compilers) and non-CS fields to follow.

Optimizely compromised, served malware on Zoom, GitLab
⬆ 572 pts 💬 225 HN comments

On 16 November 2026 an attacker obtained write access to the Project JavaScript field of Optimizely Web customer projects and injected a loader into snippets served from cdn.optimizely.com — with Optimizely's own certificate, from a hostname every affected site already trusted in its CSP and its users' browsers. Scanning 1,611 snippets, Report URI found 188 belonging to 178 organisations carrying the payload, including Zoom, GitLab, Salesforce, CNN, the BBC, IKEA, HSBC, Equifax, Ledger and Optimizely's own login pages. This was not cache poisoning: the CDN faithfully served what it was given, so the integrity failure sits entirely in the tag layer — the same tier that Google Tag Manager normalised a decade ago by handing marketing departments unmediated script execution. The loader came in three variants (plain script tag to 1688360.cc/track.js, base64-plus-atob obfuscation, and a full inline eval fallback), and the payload itself was rebuilt 26 times during the day, adding wallets and features without ever removing any.

The payload is a multi-purpose commodity kit with an unusual reach: a checksum-validating crypto clipper covering BTC, ETH/EVM, TRON, Solana, TON, LTC, BCH and DOGE — rewriting page text, form fields, the clipboard, fetch/XHR bodies, window.open, and the iOS share sheet; Luhn-checked card skimming exfiltrated via navigator.sendBeacon to /card.php (hosted payment iframes escaped it, a site's own form fields did not); cookie and html2canvas screenshot theft; and, on iOS, version-gated routing into the Coruna (iOS 13–17) and DarkSword (18.x) exploit chains via throwaway .icu hosts, with pages on iOS 18.6 or earlier visibly reloading every 60 seconds. Verified theft was modest — roughly $7,040 across BTC, LTC, TRON, ETH and BSC, swept to exchanges at 13:03 UTC — because the clipper requires a victim to copy an address and paste it elsewhere within the window. Cleanup was fast once detected: infected snippets fell from 169 at 20:56 UTC to 1 (TalkTalk) at 21:52 UTC.

The structural lesson is that SRI is effectively unusable against continuously-updated vendor snippets, that CSP must constrain script and connect-src provenance rather than origin hygiene, and that no amount of first-party code review protects a site whose trust boundary extends to a third party's admin console.

188 snippets across 178 organisations, rebuilt 26 times in a single day, all served with a valid certificate from a host every victim site already whitelisted — yet verified losses were only ~$7,040, because a crypto clipper needs a victim to copy an address and paste it somewhere else before the tab closes.

Key Highlights
  • Attacker wrote into Optimizely's Project JavaScript field; the CDN served legitimate, correctly-signed responses — no cache poisoning, so origin-side integrity checks would have seen nothing
  • 188 of 1,611 scanned snippets infected across 178 orgs spanning banking, telecoms, retail, media, crypto and gambling
  • Payload combined crypto address swapping, Luhn-checked card skimming via sendBeacon, cookie/html2canvas screenshot exfiltration, and iOS exploit-kit delivery (Coruna, DarkSword)
  • Verified theft ~$7,040 in nine hours, swept to exchanges; the TRON wallet had been active since March 2026, indicating a persistent actor
  • SRI is impractical for rapidly-updating vendor snippets; CSP in Report-Only mode with script/connect-src monitoring is the pragmatic detection layer

Jiff 1.0: A datetime library for Rust
⬆ 429 pts 💬 92 HN comments

Andrew Gallant (burntsushi) has shipped Jiff 1.0, a Rust datetime library whose organizing principle is the "pit of success": time-zone handling is pushed into the center of the API rather than bolted on as an afterthought, on the thesis that datetime difficulty stems from "poor modeling of the domain" rather than intrinsic complexity. The type hierarchy is an explicit onion — `civil::Date`, `civil::Time` and `civil::DateTime` are location-free; `Timestamp` is nanoseconds since the Unix epoch; `TimeZone` maps between the two; `ZonedDateTime` binds all three plus an offset. Ambiguity from DST folds and gaps is not silently resolved but exposed through a strategy API (`compatible`, `earlier`, `later`, `unambiguous`), and durations split into `Span` — fieldwise-preserving, so two hours is a distinct value from 120 minutes — versus the 96-bit `SignedDuration`. The 1.0 promise is indefinite semver stability, because Jiff is designed to appear in other crates' public APIs.

The adoption case is already made: uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, mise, Jujutsu, uutils/coreutils and AWS LLRT's Temporal implementation run Jiff in production, which is why the maintainer calls a breaking change an ecosystem-wide cost. Gallant is notably honest about scope — Jiff implements only the Unix time scale, has no TAI or leap-second support, and directs astronomical users to `hifitime`; IANA identifiers, meanwhile, are "generally for programmers," a limitation raised in comments. The strongest social signal in the thread is succession: chrono's long-time maintainer writes that burnout drove him out and thanks burntsushi for "the datetime library rust deserves," with chrono's original author replying in kind.

Jiff reaches 1.0 already embedded in uv, Cargo, Starship, fd, Nushell, Jujutsu and AWS LLRT — with a maintainer pledge of indefinite API stability precisely because the crate shows up in other libraries' public signatures.

Key Highlights
  • Jiff models time as a layered onion: civil date/time types are location-free, Timestamp is an instant, TimeZone maps between them, ZonedDateTime combines all three.
  • DST folds and gaps are handled explicitly via compatible/earlier/later/unambiguous strategies rather than being silently resolved.
  • Span preserves calendar and clock units fieldwise (2 hours ≠ 120 minutes); SignedDuration is a compact 96-bit nanosecond type for physical time.
  • 1.0 comes with a semver-stability commitment because Jiff is intended as a public dependency of other crates.
  • Scope is deliberately bounded: Unix time scale only, no TAI/leap seconds, and IANA identifiers remain programmer-oriented rather than user-facing.

Grazing sheep in fields with solar arrays
⬆ 318 pts 💬 318 HN comments

Cornell's College of Agriculture and Life Sciences has published three years of field data from the Cascadilla Community Solar Farm in Dryden, New York, treating sheep grazing and pollinator habitat as a single coupled system rather than two separate policy questions. The headline result, in Environmental Research Letters, is a stocking-rate response curve: at two sheep per hectare, wild bee, honey bee and lady beetle abundance rose 38% over a mowed control — driven almost entirely by an 83% jump in honey bees — while at eight sheep per hectare vegetation suppression was maximised and flock health held, but honey bee abundance fell 45%. Four sheep per hectare behaved like mowing for insects while beating two sheep on vegetation control. The paper's framing is that 'people, energy, agricultural animals, plants and insects are all part of this ecosystem,' which turns a mowing-cost line item into a multi-objective optimisation over biodiversity, fire/fuel load and animal condition.

A second paper, from Cornell's SC Johnson College of Business and Duffield College of Engineering in Manufacturing & Service Operations Management, attacks the economics with a spreadsheet-based flock-management model that optimises procurement and sales against target flock size, costs, anticipated revenue and expected vegetation at specific sites — the implicit penalty being that under-stocked sites force mechanical mowing. Commercial uptake is already visible: United Agrivoltaics folded the tool into onboarding, and one consultant runs 700 ewes across New York and Vermont installations, describing the inversion where 'you get paid to graze as opposed to having to pay to graze.' The macro context is import substitution — roughly half of US lamb and mutton is imported from New Zealand and Australia — so solar leases become the capital base for rebuilding domestic flock scale, processors and rural credit. The technical caveat the HN crowd seized on: these are trade-off curves, not a free lunch.

At two sheep per hectare beneficial insect abundance rose 38% versus mowing, but pushing to eight sheep per hectare — maximum vegetation control — cut honey bee abundance by 45%: the same acreage cannot simultaneously be a pollinator refuge and a lawn service.

  • A Cornell operations-research spreadsheet model optimises flock size, procurement and sales against site vegetation, and is already used in United Agrivoltaics onboarding
  • Economics invert the usual grazing cost structure: farmers are paid to graze rather than paying for land, letting them reinvest in barns, equipment and labour
  • US imports roughly half its lamb and mutton, so solar lease revenue is framed as seed capital for rebuilding domestic flock scale, processors and rural credit

Nimony: A New Compiler for Nim
⬆ 149 pts 💬 71 HN comments

Nimony is Andreas Rumpf's from-scratch reimplementation of the Nim compiler and the reference implementation for what the project calls "Nim 3." The headline language changes are substantial: borrow checking with prefix exclusion (a borrowed path cannot be mutated until the borrow ends, while sibling fields remain writable), explicit nilability, sum types expressed as `case` sections inside `object` declarations with exhaustive ML-style pattern matching, and generics that are type-checked at definition site via concepts rather than only at instantiation — duck typing survives behind an `{.untyped.}` pragma. Architecturally the more interesting bet is NIF, an interchange format that every pipeline stage both reads and writes, which enables inspecting, diffing and transforming intermediate output, and is the mechanism underlying incremental and parallel builds. It also opens a plugin surface well beyond Nim's macro system: custom validators for deadlocks, races and stack-hungry recursion, plus custom backends targeting GPUs or FPGAs.

The engineering claims are unusually specific. Nimony ships native code generation for x86_64, ARM64, ARM32, AVR and RISC-V independent of LLVM and GCC, deliberately sidestepping the undefined-behavior assumptions those toolchains inherit from C, while still compiling to C, JavaScript and WebAssembly from one codebase. Its static-contract feature is the standout: indexing into arrays, seqs and strings carries a `requires` contract checked at the call site; a provably out-of-range index is a compile error, and a module marked `{.feature: "staticContracts".}` must prove every access in bounds — eliminating both buffer overruns and residual runtime checks. Version 0.6.2 landed 2026-09-08 with nightly toolchains for four platforms, and `nimony n` removes the C compiler dependency entirely. Against 149 points and 71 comments, the reception is warm but not uncritical: several readers note sum types already arrived in Nim 2.4.0, and skepticism focuses on migration cost and duplicated memory-safety machinery.

Long-time Nim users treat Nimony as vindication of a decade of patience — but the thread's sharpest disagreements are about whether borrowing Rust's safety model is an upgrade or an unnecessary tax, and whether the website's polish signals anything about the compiler's maturity.

"An index that is provably out of range is a compile-time error, and a module with {.feature: 'staticContracts'.} must prove every access in bounds — no buffer overruns and no run-time checks left in the binary."

Key Highlights
  • NIF, a read/write interchange format at every compiler stage, is the substrate for incremental and parallel builds as well as third-party validators and code generators
  • Native backends for x86_64, ARM64, ARM32, AVR and RISC-V avoid LLVM/GCC undefined-behavior assumptions while retaining C, JS and WASM targets
  • Static contracts make provably out-of-bounds indexing a compile-time error, and `{.feature: "staticContracts".}` can eliminate all runtime bounds checks
  • Generics are checked at definition site via concepts by default, with `{.untyped.}` restoring duck typing
  • Sum types, incremental compilation and SSO strings have already shipped in Nim 2.4.0, muddying the Nim 3 differentiation story

Show HN: VennDB: append-only in-memory Rust DB for rows queried using bit flags
⬆ 29 pts 💬 2 HN comments

VennDB is a narrowly-scoped Rust library that turns a plain struct into an append-only, in-memory database optimized for one workload: data loaded once at startup and queried forever with simple binary filters. The author, gdcbe, built it roughly three years ago as an internal piece of the rama proxy project (ramaproxy.org), where every incoming request must pick a random upstream proxy matching a handful of booleans plus a country or city. Iterating a Vec got slow, and SQLite felt like excessive machinery for 'give me a random row with these flags.' The implementation is a derive macro: annotate a struct with #[derive(VennDB)] and it generates a companion DB type, a query builder, key lookups, and custom validation hooks. Bool fields become bitmask columns; non-bool #[venndb(filter)] fields become 'filter maps' mapping each distinct value to a set of matching rows; a query is an intersection of those sets — literally a Venn diagram evaluated over bitvecs. Crucially, matches are never materialized as a slice before .any() is called, so random selection stays O(1)-ish rather than paying to collect candidates.

The benchmark table is the real argument. At 100k records, three boolean filters resolve in 246.7ns versus 653.7µs for a naive Vec scan, 1.568µs for a hand-rolled roaring-bitmap index, and 4.135ms for in-memory SQLite with indices. At one million records the gap widens dramatically: 192ns for venn versus 42.23ms for SQLite, with index memory roughly comparable to roaring (61.79MB vs 59.49MB) and database construction roughly 3x to 30x faster (121.3ms vs 390.9ms vs 3.838s). The API surfaces deliberate footguns-as-features: keys must be unique and non-optional; filter keys must implement Eq + Hash + Clone, with the docs warning that a raw String for country means 'bE != Be != BE != Belgium'; an #[venndb(filter, any)] type designates a wildcard row that matches every value, which the FAQ defends on fairness grounds — letting an any-row also match a specific value would statistically rig random selection. RuntimeColumn, computed columns, FilterSet for HashSet/BTreeSet, and predicate fallbacks cover the escape hatches. The obvious trade-off: no mutation of existing rows, and every query must go through generated, statically-typed accessors.

At one million rows, three boolean filters execute in 192 nanoseconds — roughly 220,000x faster than in-memory SQLite with per-filter indices (42.23ms) and about 36,000x faster than iterating a plain Vec.

Key Highlights
  • Derive-macro ergonomics: #[derive(VennDB)] on a struct generates the DB type, query builder, key index, and validator, eliminating hand-maintained bitmap-index code.
  • Bools compile down to bitmasks; non-bool filter fields become value→rowset 'filter maps' that intersect at query time like a Venn diagram, with no candidate slice materialized before .any().
  • Benchmarks at 1M rows: 192ns for 3 bool filters vs 13.79µs (roaring), 42.23ms (SQLite), 6.984ms (naive Vec).
  • Deliberate constraints: append-only, unique non-optional keys, no filter-and-key overlap (compile error), and HashSet/BTreeSet rather than Vec for multi-value filters.
  • Escape hatches: RuntimeColumn for lock-free mutable flags, db.column() for computed properties, any_where(predicate), and per-row validators.

Exploiting Replay Protected Monotonic Counters to Brute-Force Windows Hello PINs
⬆ 9 pts 💬 1 HN comments

Windows Hello's security model is not built on PIN entropy but on the anti-hammering lockout enforced by the TPM: after 32 failed attempts, a guess is permitted roughly every ten minutes, which converts a 4-digit PIN from trivially brute-forceable into a 69-day ordeal. On the overwhelming majority of modern x86 machines, that TPM is not a discrete chip but a firmware TPM running inside the CPU's security processor — AMD's PSP (or Microsoft Pluton on newer parts), Intel's CSME, or Pluton itself — which persists its non-volatile state, including the failed-attempt counter, encrypted on the external SPI flash. Encryption alone does not defeat replay: if an attacker can restore a previously captured image of that NV state, the fTPM reverts to a world in which the failed guesses never happened. The paper's contribution is showing that the JEDEC-standardized Replay Protected Monotonic Counters (RPMC) mechanism, which is supposed to anchor rollback prevention in the flash chip, is broken in shipping silicon.

Using low-cost interposer hardware straddling the physically accessible SPI bus, the authors — Christian Werling, Matti Finder, Hans Niklas Jacob, Julian Prein, Niclas Kühnapfel and Jean-Pierre Seifert — document several implementation flaws, two of which are structural. Winbond NOR flash, with over 25% market share, leaks its HMAC key under voltage glitching, permitting arbitrary counter forgery; and AMD's NV controller simply does not verify RPMC increments, so an interposer can suppress them silently. On two AMD desktops without RPMC flash, reflashing an old image reset the fTPM lockout counters outright, implying no NV rollback protection at all. The end-to-end result is stark: a locked, encrypted device with a 4-digit Hello PIN falls in 40 hours instead of 69 days, and BitLocker's default 6-digit TPM+PIN pre-boot protector collapses from 19 years to 20 days — while the recovered PIN also unlocks device-bound keys and Hello-based enterprise SSO.

An interposer plus voltage glitching on a Winbond SPI flash turns a 4-digit Windows Hello PIN from a 69-day brute force into a 40-hour one — and BitLocker's 6-digit TPM+PIN pre-boot protector from 19 years into 20 days.

  • Windows Hello PINs are safe only through TPM anti-hammering (32 attempts, then one every 10 minutes), not through entropy

  • RPMC is meant to anchor rollback prevention in the flash chip, but this is the first practical analysis on shipping x86 systems
  • Winbond flash leaks its HMAC key under voltage glitching; AMD's NV controller fails to verify RPMC increments — both allow counter rollback
