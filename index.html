#3
The State of Context Engineering
⬆ 231 pts 💬 58 HN comments

DeBruig's AGNTCon talk, published as "The State of Context Engineering," is really a genealogy of a discipline. It traces the field from the late-2024 "RAG is dead" optimism—where Gemini 1.5's million-token window (and Llama 4 Scout's underwhelming 10M-token claim) was pitched as a substitute for retrieval and agentic scaffolding—through the discovery that long contexts are not a panacea. He enumerates four canonical failure modes: context poisoning (a bad fact or hallucinated to-do item the model cannot escape, illustrated by Gemini 2.5 trying to bribe a Pokémon guard with iced tea while playing the 1996 original), context distraction (context overwhelming parametric knowledge), context confusion (irrelevant facts shifting model assumptions about the user), and context clash (conflicting tools, skills, memory). Crucially, he argues the six-tool fix set—augmentation, tool loadout, quarantine, pruning, offloading, summarization—has remained stable while migrating out of prompts and into deterministic harness code, demonstrated in ~16 lines of DSPy around a ReAct while-loop.

The second half is where the talk earns its title. Model builders now optimize for long-horizon agency—METR-style horizons of roughly 3.4 hours for GPT-5, 6.4 for Gemini 3.1, and ~12 for Opus 4.6, all measured with a harness—which produces two novel failure modes. "Fighting the weights" describes overfitting to training-time tool environments: Claude Code's system prompt repeats emphatic, sometimes all-caps instructions to parallelize tool calls because models were trained on largely serial harnesses. "Context inertia" describes fixation on a hypothesis or subtask past the point of usefulness: Anthropic's Memento harness resets context every 90 minutes while preserving the environment; Microsoft's Fork-and-Flush and Berkeley's Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting show the same forget-then-improve sawtooth. His 2027 prediction: accumulating, self-authored agent memories whose skills and instructions drift into mutual contradiction, making strategic forgetting the next unsolved problem. The through-line: knowing when to be deterministic and when to be probabilistic.

Buried in the Gemini 2.5 technical report's appendix: once context grew significantly beyond 100,000 tokens, the Pokémon-playing agent began repeating actions from its history instead of synthesizing new plans—and Anthropic's fix for the same fixation is to wipe the agent's context every 90 minutes while keeping the sandboxed environment.

Key Highlights
  • Four failure modes—poisoning, distraction, confusion, clash—persist regardless of window size; the 200k–300k token mark is the author's personal reset threshold
  • The six context fixes (augmentation, tool loadout, quarantine, pruning, offloading, summarization) are stable and increasingly implemented as deterministic harness code (DSPy ReAct loop, ~16 lines)
  • New agentic failure modes: "fighting the weights" (overfitting to training-time tool schemas, e.g. Claude Code's repeated parallel-tool-call instructions) and "context inertia" (fixation past usefulness)
  • Anthropic's Memento, Microsoft's Fork-and-Flush, and Berkeley's Scaling Discovery Requires Forgetting converge on the same fix: periodic context clearing with environment/artifact preservation
  • Predicted 2027 problem: long-running personal agents whose self-authored memories and skills accumulate conflicting fixes, requiring strategic forgetting nobody has solved

#5
Attention Is Not What You Need (2025)
⬆ 160 pts 💬 55 HN comments

Zhang Chong's arXiv preprint 'Attention Is Not What You Need' attacks the deepest assumption in modern sequence modeling: that explicit multi-head self-attention is necessary for strong performance. The paper's framing is its real contribution — attention is recast as 'tensor lifting,' where hidden vectors are mapped into a high-dimensional space of pairwise interactions and then constrained by gradient descent. That mechanism is expressive but, the author argues, mathematically opaque: after many layers the computation resists description by any small family of explicit invariants. The proposed alternative replaces the L-by-L attention matrix with a 'Causal Grassmann layer' that linearly reduces token states, encodes local token pairs as two-dimensional subspaces on a Grassmann manifold via Plücker coordinates, and fuses these geometric features back through gated mixing. Information propagates via controlled deformations of low-rank subspaces over multi-scale local windows, with claimed linear scaling in sequence length at fixed rank.

The empirical footprint is deliberately modest, and that is the flashpoint. Purely Grassmann-based models of 13–18M parameters land within roughly 10–15% of size-matched Transformers on Wikitext-2 validation perplexity, while a Grassmann-Plücker head bolted onto DistilBERT edges out a Transformer head on SNLI at 0.8550/0.8538 validation/test versus 0.8545/0.8511 — a 0.0027 test-accuracy delta. The submission is a single-author, 16KB v1 with no code link surfaced. The 55-comment thread split predictably: 160 upvotes against arguments that 18M parameters on an ancient benchmark proves little, that the pairing operation (z_t, z_{t+Δ}) may leak the next token, and that Grassmannian encoding discards the linear map's basis in exchange for lower rank. Meanwhile practitioners note Qwen 3.5 reportedly runs ~75% linear-attention layers — the hybrid future may already be here.

A Grassmann-Plücker head on DistilBERT beats a Transformer head on SNLI by 0.0027 test accuracy (0.8538 vs 0.8511) — a rounding-error margin carrying a very large theoretical claim.

Key Highlights
  • Attention is reframed as 'tensor lifting' into pairwise-interaction space — expressive but interpretable only through constrained invariants.
  • The Causal Grassmann layer avoids materializing an L×L matrix via Plücker-encoded 2D subspaces and gated mixing, claiming linear scaling in sequence length at fixed rank.
  • Results are small-scale: 13–18M parameters, Wikitext-2 perplexity within 10–15% of size-matched Transformers, single-author 16KB preprint with no code link visible.
  • A commenter flags a potential causality bug in the (z_t, z_{t+Δ}) pairing — next-token leakage — though others read the construction as causal-by-construction.
  • The thread's real subtext is the bitter-lesson debate: whether architecture matters at all when Qwen 3.5 reportedly uses ~75% linear-attention layers.

Why I Am a Programmer
⬆ 283 pts 💬 87 HN comments

Alfie John's memoir traces a pre-mainstream-Internet apprenticeship shaped by scarcity, friendship, and illicit distribution. Computers were rare enough to be hidden from thieves; knowledge moved through whispered networks, drilled-out 3.5-inch floppies, hex-edited save files, BBS listings, and ZMODEM transfers. The pivotal artifact is TUT1-9.ZIP by Denthor of ASPHYXIA: Pascal source tutorials that demystified VGA demo effects from pixel plotting through sprites, wireframe 3D, and solid 3D. That curriculum matters technically because it exposes the graphics pipeline at a hardware-adjacent layer—memory layout, timing, rasterization, coordinate transforms—before modern engine and GPU abstractions. Cracktros acted as both pirate signatures and demoscene precursors, while FILE_ID.DIZ metadata served as compact distribution manifest and identity signal. The essay frames programming as sorcery: direct control over a constrained machine, with 'elite' status earned through visible mastery.

The Hacker News thread converts that origin story into a collective oral history. Commenters recall Conan the Barbarian and Below the Root on an Apple IIe, TI-83 programs as a 'gateway drug,' ACE cracktros for International Karate, and Denthor directly: 'Denthor got me into programming too.' The discussion then splits along a familiar fault line. Some celebrate craft autonomy—christophilus cites '100% autonomy' solving real problems; SA9G likes building without 'skinned knuckles or solder smoke.' Others describe professional erosion: sevenzero says the game-like nature is 'completely gone,' vconnor says the only life improved was the boss's, and marcus_holmes satirizes standups, JIRA tickets, and making products worse in tiny increments. Late entrants such as ifh-hn call automation a 'special power,' while aidotguru jokes about becoming a farmer after AI. The thread is less about coding than about preserving agency over computation.

Denthor's 1993 TUT7 quote—'I want to write demos... reach out of the screen and grab them'—captures the demoscene ambition: programming as perceptual manipulation, not merely utility.

Key Highlights
  • The essay maps a pre-web path into programming through floppy piracy, BBS culture, hex editing, and Denthor's Pascal VGA tutorials.
  • Cracktros and the demoscene functioned as a meritocratic technical showcase wrapped inside illegal game distribution.
  • Commenters replay their own origin stories on Apple IIe, C64, TI-83, ZX Spectrum, and Amiga hardware.
  • A major countercurrent contrasts childhood creative autonomy with corporate JIRA, standup, and ticket-throughput work.
  • Non-traditional entrants and automation users still describe programming as a 'special power,' even as AI anxiety surfaces.

Show HN: A Truly Modular Keyboard
⬆ 223 pts 💬 71 HN comments

Keybytes, a Show HN project by NotARoomba, inverts the standard keyboard architecture: instead of a single PCB with a matrix of switches scanned by one microcontroller, every key is a self-contained 1u (19.05mm²) board called a Keybit carrying its own CH32V003 RISC-V MCU, a Cherry MX footprint, and pogo-pin contacts on all four edges delivering 3.3V, GND and I2C. A separate Keyword main board — a two-PCB stack with an STM32WB55RGV6 for Bluetooth LE and USB, a W25Q16JV 2MB QSPI flash to hold distributed key firmware, BQ25895 charging, TPS631000 buck-boost and a ceramic antenna — anchors the assembly and programs the Keybits over three dedicated pogo pins. Magnetic cases hold modules together, so layouts are physically rewired rather than remapped in software. The motive was aesthetic as much as engineering: the author wanted triangular and circular keyboards, which no vendor sells.

The project is deliberately early-stage: 223 points, 71 comments, 40 commits, five assembled keys imminent, and a bill of materials landing near $6 per key — roughly $600+ for a full 104-key board, before switch and keycap. Technically the interesting choices are the four independent I2C buses (to cut parasitic capacitance and to infer physical topology, since each key plans to remember its 'parent' neighbor) and the per-key MCU firmware, whose CH32 code is not yet in the repo. Commenters surfaced the real fault lines: pogo-pin polarity reversal when joining arbitrary sides, the 127-address and bus-hang limits of I2C at scale, structural rigidity for longer runs, and the absence of staggered or columnar offsets that ergonomic users need. It is a beautiful distributed-systems demo whose product viability remains unproven.

Each key is a $0.20 CH32V003 RISC-V microcontroller on its own 19.05mm PCB — a full 104-key layout would mean 104 networked MCUs, roughly $6 of silicon and magnets per key and a ~$600+ board before switches.

Key Highlights
  • Every key is an independent 1u PCB with a CH32V003 RISC-V MCU and pogo pins for 3.3V, GND and I2C on all four edges
  • STM32WB55RGV6 main board provides Bluetooth LE, USB-C, 2MB QSPI storage for key firmware, LiPo charging and buck-boost
  • Four separate I2C buses reduce parasitic capacitance and let firmware infer each key's physical neighbor topology
  • Author cites ~$6 per key at volume; only two keys built so far, with staggered rows, blank spacer modules and extra sensors planned
  • Open technical questions: pogo-pin polarity when joining arbitrary sides, I2C address/hang limits at 100+ slaves, and rigidity of long layouts

Dolly Parton's Imagination Library
Dolly Parton's Imagination Library is a deceptively simple intervention: enroll a child at birth, mail one age-appropriate, high-quality book per month to the home, addressed in the child's own name, until the age of five. Founded in 1995 in Sevier County, Tennessee, and inspired by Parton's father's illiteracy, the program now operates in the US, Canada, UK, Ireland and Australia under the Dollywood Foundation, a 501(c)(3). The architectural trick is that the central organization does not pay for the books: it curates the title list, negotiates volume pricing, manages the mailing and registration infrastructure (including a bilingual Spanish track and a "BOS" back-office login for affiliates), while per-book costs are underwritten by thousands of local partners — Rotary clubs, United Ways, school districts, state grants. Central standardization, distributed financing. The economics are the interesting part. Commenters report a wholesale cost around $2.5 per book, achievable because a single title selection aggregates demand across tens of thousands of children into print runs no individual library system could match.
