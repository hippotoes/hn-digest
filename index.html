GPT-5.5 hallucinates 3x more than MIT-licensed GLM-5.2
⬆ 174 pts 💬 49 HN comments

The article challenges the prevailing industry assumption that scaling model parameters invariably improves AI quality. Drawing on data from the AA-Omniscience benchmark, it reports that GLM-5.2 (753B params, MIT licensed) hallucinates only 28% of the time on unknown questions, while GPT-5.5 (estimated 1-2T params) hallucinates at 86%—a 3× gap. The author contends that massive models, trained on enormous factual corpora, learn to always produce an answer rather than admit ignorance. This phenomenon is illustrated with a Python coding experiment: DeepSeek V4 Pro (1.6T params) spent nearly four minutes crafting a confidently incorrect solution, whereas GLM-5.2 quickly identified the problem's unsolvability. The piece posits a trilemma facing modern LLMs: raw capability, uncertainty calibration, and computational efficiency cannot all be optimized simultaneously.

The underlying cause is attributed to training data composition: models saturated with factual, well‐formed Q&A pairs never learn that 'I don't know' is a valid response, and large parameter counts inadvertently amplify confident hallucinations. The article also contextualizes the US government ban on Claude Fable 5 as a symptom of uncalibrated risk. Commenters debate the nuance of hallucination metrics—some noting that rates are conditional on model difficulty—while others share personal experiences of degraded performance with newer GPT versions. Notably, the author and some commenters advocate for smaller, specialized models (like GLM-5.2) that balance honesty and efficiency, mirroring a broader industry pivot from brute-force scaling toward calibrated, resource‐aware AI design.

GLM-5.2, with 753B parameters and MIT license, hallucinates 28% vs GPT-5.5's 86%—three times less despite being half the size.

Key Highlights
  • Larger models (1T+ params) hallucinate more than smaller, well-trained models due to lack of uncertainty calibration.
  • GLM-5.2 achieves comparable benchmark scores to GPT-5.5 with far fewer parameters and lower hallucination rate.
  • Training on predominantly factual data encourages models to always answer rather than say 'I don't know'.
  • The article presents a 'trilemma' of capability, calibration, and efficiency that cannot be fully balanced.
  • Hugely increases in reasoning tokens (e.g., DeepSeek V4 Pro) don't guarantee correct outcomes; they can reinforce errors.

Project Valhalla, Explained: How a Decade of Work Arrives in JDK 28
⬆ 596 pts 💬 370 HN comments

Project Valhalla, a decade-long effort to bring value types to the JVM, has finally landed in JDK 28 with JEP 401: Value Classes and Objects. The core idea is to allow developers to write classes that 'code like a class, work like an int'—combining the expressiveness of objects with the flat memory layout and performance of primitives. This is achieved by eliminating object identity: value objects have no headers, no pointer indirection, and can be scalarized or flattened into arrays and fields. The JVM can now store them densely, improving cache locality and reducing garbage collection pressure. However, this is just the first phase; the highly anticipated specialized generics (universal generics) remain a future JEP. The pull request alone adds over 197,000 lines across 1,816 files, and the feature is currently in preview and disabled by default.

The article notes that value classes are still reference types and can be null—non-nullable types are deferred to a separate JEP. A critical constraint is atomicity: flattened data must fit within a platform's atomic write size (typically 64 bits), limiting optimizations for larger value objects. This has sparked debate: some argue the performance ceiling is lowered, while others see it as a sensible incremental step. Community reactions are mixed—ranging from excitement about long-awaited improvements to criticism of the article's quality (accused of being AI-generated) and concerns about the impact on existing patterns like synchronization. Brian Goetz's warning that 'they’ll never ship it' crowd will pivot to 'but they didn’t ship the most important part' resonates, highlighting the iterative nature of large-scale language evolution.

The PR for JEP 401 adds over 197,000 lines of code across 1,816 files; after a decade of work, Valhalla finally arrives in JDK 28 as a preview feature disabled by default.

Key Highlights
  • Value classes use the 'value' modifier and eliminate object identity, enabling flat memory layout and pointer-free arrays.
  • Heap flattening and scalarization are key optimizations, but require data to fit within 64-bit atomic writes (including a null flag).
  • Value objects are still reference types and can be null; non-nullable value types are deferred to a future JEP.
  • The == operator for value classes compares by value (recursive field comparison), not identity, breaking encapsulation for implementation details.
  • This is the first of multiple phases; specialized generics (universal generics) are not included in JDK 28.

#4
There are no instances in ATProto
⬆ 453 pts 💬 228 HN comments

Dan Abramov's article "There Are No Instances in ATProto" dismantles a persistent misconception in decentralized social media discussions: the conflation of ATProto's architecture with Mastodon's instance-based model. Abramov clarifies that ATProto separates hosting (Personal Data Servers, or PDS) from aggregation (AppViews), akin to RSS readers aggregating blogs. This decoupling means users own their data independently of any app, enabling seamless migration—Abramov himself moved his PDS from one provider to another with minimal friction. The article argues that the Mastodon-inspired focus on counting instances misses the point: ATProto's decentralization is about data portability and app plurality, not running many copies of a monolithic server. This structural difference allows users to switch hosting providers, build new apps on the same data, and avoid the O(n²) federation overhead of Mastodon. However, critics note that the protocol still relies on centralized components like the PLC directory and that the cost of running Relays and AppViews may hinder widespread adoption.

The Hacker News discussion reveals deep technical debates. Some commenters appreciate the clarity of Abramov's RSS analogy but question its completeness—for instance, RSS feeds don't replicate full content like ATProto's PDS does, which raises concerns about data permanence and control. Others highlight that while ATProto decouples services, it introduces new centralization points: Bluesky PBC operates the primary AppView and Relays, and the single PLC directory is a governance risk. Commenters like timbray worry about venture capital funding (Bluesky raised $100M from Bain Capital) and the lack of a sustainable business model. Proponents counter that alternative AppViews (e.g., Blacksky) and Relays exist, and the protocol's design allows for gradual decentralization. The debate often boils down to trade-offs: ATProto offers a smoother user experience and data portability but relies on more expensive infrastructure for full participation, while ActivityPub prioritizes easy self-hosting at the cost of consistency and migration complexity.

The article's key insight: "Coupling hosting and apps was the original sin, and the fix is simple. Keep our stuff outside the apps; let the apps aggregate over it."

Key Highlights
  • ATProto separates data hosting (PDS) from app aggregation (AppViews), unlike Mastodon's coupled instances.
  • The RSS-like model allows users to migrate hosting seamlessly without losing identity or social graph.
  • Critics note centralized components: single PLC directory, Bluesky-run AppView and Relays, and venture capital dependence.
  • Alternative AppViews (e.g., Blacksky, Tangled) and Relays exist, but adoption faces cost and discovery barriers.
  • The article reframes decentralization from 'number of instances' to 'portability of data and apps'.

How many of the 170k English words do you know?
⬆ 378 pts 💬 463 HN comments

VocabOwl is a web-based vocabulary assessment that claims to provide a "scientific word count" by testing a user's knowledge of 100 English words, selected from a larger corpus of ~170,000. The test uses multiple-choice questions with four definitions, and the underlying algorithm estimates total vocabulary based on the pattern of correct and incorrect answers. However, the Hacker News community has raised significant technical and design concerns: the multiple-choice options exhibit clear biases (opposite pair structure, longest answer often correct), the calibration suffers from a statistical error (perfect scores yield only 85,000 instead of 170,000), and the user experience is cumbersome—requiring two clicks per word with no "I don't know" option. The word selection also favors Greco-Latin roots, giving Romance language speakers an advantage, as noted by multiple commenters. The app appears to be built with a static word list and lacks adaptive difficulty, leading to a flat experience.

The discussion reveals a sophisticated engineering audience deconstructing the app's methodology. For instance, commenter stbullard identified a structural error: the algorithm halves the estimated vocabulary because it assumes a normal distribution that doesn't match the Zipfian distribution of word frequency. Others pointed out that the distractor answers are often AI-generated, with telltale patterns (semicolons, length). Several users offered alternative approaches, such as using Elo ratings or Glicko-2 for adaptive testing (salamo), or referenced more robust tools like myvocab.info. The community consensus is that while the concept is appealing, the execution has serious flaws. Many requested keyboard shortcuts, skip/submit flow improvements, and a "don't know" button to avoid forced guessing. Overall, VocabOwl serves as a case study in how poor design and statistical naivety can undermine an otherwise engaging idea.

"Getting all 100 correct only yields an estimate of 85,000 words due to a structural error: the algorithm halves the expected value, as explained in the 'How is this calculated' page."

Key Highlights
  • The test uses 100 multiple-choice questions with four definitions, but the options are often trivially distinguishable (e.g., opposite pairs, longest answer).
