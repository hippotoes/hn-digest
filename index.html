Ternary Bonsai 2.5 27B
⬆ 516 pts 💬 170 HN comments

PrismML's Ternary Bonsai 2.5 27B is a post-training compression release, not a new pretrain: it takes Qwen3.8 27B and distils it into a roughly 1.58-bit-per-weight ternary checkpoint whose weights live in {−1, 0, +1}, held at the same 5.9 GB footprint as the previous Bonsai 2 27B. The engineering claim is density — preserving reasoning and tool-use behaviour while collapsing a 50-plus-GB full-precision model to a file that fits comfortably in a 24 GB laptop's unified memory with room for a 128K context window. The stated focus of this point release is agentic reliability over long horizons: fewer wasted turns, less redundant thinking, and fewer of the doom-loops that plagued the 2.0 generation, plus stock llama.cpp support on NVIDIA and Intel GPUs and MLX on Apple silicon, under Apache 2.0.

The benchmark table is the argument and also the controversy. Bonsai 2.5 closes much of the gap to its teacher — SWE-bench Verified 60.8 → 75.6 against Qwen's 80.6, AppWorld 51.2 → 90.5 against 93.2, Terminal-Bench 2.1 52.8 → 60.1 against 70.8, DeepSWE 0 → 27 against 30.1 — while LiveCodeBench (83.0 vs 83.8) and BigCodeBench (60.3 vs 57.2 for Bonsai 2) sit flat, a reminder that ternary quantisation taxes some capabilities unevenly. Throughput is the real product: 291 tok/s generation and 3,200 tok/s prompt processing on a single H100, 204 tok/s on an RTX 4090. Commenters reported 7-30 tok/s on M-series Macs, a Tetris clone built autonomously at 7-8 tok/s, and one custom long-horizon suite where completion time fell 29% (107 → 76 minutes) at identical 5-of-8 pass rates.

Ternary Bonsai 2.5 compresses a 50+ GB Qwen3.8 27B teacher into a 5.9 GB ternary file yet recovers SWE-bench Verified from 60.8 to 75.6 — within 5 points of full precision at roughly a ninth of the memory.

Key Highlights
  • 1.58-bit ternary weights hold a 27B-class model at 5.9 GB, delivering 291 tok/s on H100 and 204 tok/s on a consumer RTX 4090
  • SWE-bench Verified jumps 60.8 → 75.6 vs Bonsai 2, but still trails the 80.6 full-precision Qwen3.8 base; DeepSWE goes 0 → 27 vs 30.1
  • Long-horizon agentic behaviour is the headline fix: fewer turns, fewer redundant tokens, and mitigation of Bonsai 2's repetitive-loop pathology
  • Community reports 7-30 tok/s on Mac mini/M-series hardware with 128K-256K context fitting in 24 GB, including a fully autonomous Tetris build and a 2048-needle retrieval pass at 256K
  • Sceptics counter that benchmark-maxxing, missing quant/engine metadata, and prior-version doom loops make real-world parity with frontier models implausible

Predicting LoRA size for any dataset
⬆ 52 pts 💬 4 HN comments

Engram's post treats LoRA rank selection as a compression problem rather than a hyperparameter sweep: the size of a dataset is its negative log-likelihood under the base model, and an adapter's capacity is the number of bits it can absorb. Framing fine-tuning through Shannon's source-coding theorem, the authors derive a closed-form capacity curve C(r) ≈ (1/2ln2)·E[Σσᵢ(Z)²] for a rank-r update to an m×n weight matrix, where Z is Gaussian. Capacity grows roughly linearly at low rank at (√m+√n)²/(2ln2)·r, then plateaus as singular values decay, converging at full rank to mn/(2ln2) — recovering Wilks' theorem. For an 800M Qwen3.5 v_proj (512×1024), that predicts ~378k bits, or 0.48 bits/parameter at full rank and 1.3–1.4 bits/parameter at low rank, matched closely by actual training runs.

Because deep transformers defy closed-form analysis, the team inverts arithmetic coding: they decode N random bits into text using the model's own likelihoods (neural linguistic steganography) to manufacture datasets of exactly known size, then fit a log-logistic curve to extrapolate infinite-training capacity. Two findings stand out. First, capacity is super-additive across layers — 1.5 bits/parameter for a single adapted layer rising to 3.3 bits/parameter across 24 MLP layers, well above the 1.30 theory line. Second, a striking 'conservation of capacity': across all runs, models forget roughly as much as they learn (forgetting/learning ratio 1.00), consistent with zero-mean gradients on fresh samples. Real datasets are then measured by summing log-likelihood over 2M-token YaRN windows, yielding rank predictions within 5% error on workspaces including swebench-django, Tulu 3, MMMLU, GSM8K and MATH-500.

Adapting 24 layers stores 3.3 bits per parameter versus 1.5 for a single layer — and every bit learned costs roughly a bit forgotten, with a measured forgetting/learning ratio of 1.00.

Key Highlights
  • LoRA capacity is modeled as compressible bits under the base model, giving a closed-form rank→capacity curve derived from Fisher information and random-matrix singular values.
  • Arithmetic coding run in reverse generates synthetic datasets of exactly N bits, enabling empirical capacity measurement on full transformers where theory fails.
  • Multi-layer adaptation is super-additive: 3.3 bits/parameter at 24 layers versus 1.5 for one layer, far exceeding naive per-matrix summation.
  • Forgetting tracks learning at roughly 1:1 across all settings, suggesting capacity is 'freed' by overwriting prior knowledge rather than added for free.
  • Real-world validation predicts required rank within 5% across datasets spanning GSM8K, MATH-500, Tulu 3, MMMLU and swebench-django, with 2M-token YaRN windows.

Launch HN: Versine (YC F26) – MCP to send and receive money
⬆ 101 pts 💬 98 HN comments

Versine, a YC F26 company, launched on Hacker News with an MCP (Model Context Protocol) server that lets AI assistants move real money on a user's behalf — sending and receiving payments between people rather than merely paying for APIs or services (a contrast the founders explicitly draw with x402). Architecturally the product is a thin agent-facing protocol layer over a self-custodial stablecoin wallet: each user holds USDC on Base, and every payment is gated by a passkey signature, which the founders claim is constructed over the exact amount and recipient rendered on screen, so neither the agent nor Versine's own server can substitute a different transaction between display and approval. Fiat rails are abstracted away — Apple Pay, Google Pay, and cards for on-ramp; Plaid plus Bridge for bank off-ramp — and the business model is explicitly not peer-to-peer payments, which the team concedes 'isn't profitable,' but a wedge toward an 'agent-first bank' monetized through deposits and credit.

The launch exposed the fault line in agentic finance: authorization ergonomics versus transaction finality. Versine mitigates agent overreach with a trusted-contacts allow-list plus a rolling seven-day spend cap, and handles unclaimed transfers as sender-side escrow that expires after seven days so funds never sit orphaned on-chain. But when asked directly about the dispute flow, the founder answered that payments 'come straight from the user's own wallet so once sent they're final and we have no ability to truly reverse the transaction by design.' Commenters with payments backgrounds noted the hard part 'was never sending the money, it was the dispute after,' while others flagged the phishing surface of any 'money waiting in Versine, connect your bank' claim link. Regulatory friction is concentrated in the off-ramp — money transmission and KYC per payout jurisdiction — which commenters argue caps the product at US-only for the foreseeable future.

Asked what happens when an agent pays the wrong person, the founder admitted the design has no reversal path: 'once sent they're final and we have no ability to truly reverse the transaction by design' — prompting one commenter to call combining agentic AI with one-way crypto 'next level free soloing.'

Key Highlights
  • MCP server exposes send/receive money primitives to AI assistants, distinct from x402-style pay-per-API flows
  • Wallets are self-custodial USDC on Base; passkey signs the exact displayed amount and recipient, not a session challenge
  • Trusted-contacts allow-list is bounded by a per-7-day cap enforced alongside global limits
  • Claim links expire in 7 days and funds stay in the sender's wallet until claimed, cancellable at any time
  • Bank off-ramp via Plaid + Bridge; US-only launch because money transmission and KYC burden sits on the fiat side

Porting Rockbox to an Undocumented $9 Aliexpress 'YP3' MP3 Player with LLMs
⬆ 97 pts 💬 51 HN comments

A blogger going by Jayden Milne pointed a heavily tooled agent stack—Claude Opus with Ghidra-MCP, a webcam for eyes, a line-in for ears, and a Python monkey-test harness—at a $9.71 unbranded 'YP3' MP3 player and produced a working Rockbox port for hardware nobody had documented. The SoC is mystery meat: a 'Smartlink SL6801' with dual Cortex-M4 r0p1 cores at 192 MHz, 512 KiB SRAM, 2 MiB flash, execute-in-place with no cache at all, a 128x160 RGB565 panel, and an undocumented Bluetooth stack burned into ROM. Serving 512 KiB against Rockbox's previous 2 MiB floor meant cutting every buffer to the bone, sharing one 128 KiB arena between codecs and plugins, dropping dircache, voice, DSP effects, and background database refresh, and moving hot loops by hand-tuned profiling between flash and SRAM. The only human-supplied breakthrough came from ilyakurdyukov, who found the DFU download mode and wrote flash tools—removing the need for a soldering iron.

The workflow is the actual story. A bidirectional Rockbox USB shell with injected keypresses, screenshots, and radio debugging let an agent reboot into recovery, flash, test, and diagnose overnight; a prior Codex/Sol attempt had spun out into 55 GB, 3.87 million files, and a paranoid block-verifying flasher that took 15 minutes to write 2 MiB. Cleanroom separation—dirty Ghidra agent, referee, implementation agent—was retrofitted for licensing hygiene. Results are concrete: a new mpegplayer, enhanced RDS, Bluetooth HID for PS4 pads, 357 new plugins mostly via Arduboy/Pokitto/Flipper Zero compat shims, a ~1M-parameter LLM running off SD card, battery within an hour of stock, and a follow-up TS47 port in four hours using the generalized auto-rockbox toolkit. The author's honest framing—'I was barely involved'—is the provocation.

The ported firmware lands within about an hour of stock battery life (20.07h wired vs 21.29h stock), and a follow-up player went from unboxing to usable alpha in four hours—while the earlier Codex run produced 3.87 million files before Claude threw the tooling away for a few Python scripts.

Key Highlights
  • Rockbox ported to a 512 KiB dual-Cortex-M4 'Smartlink SL6801' with XIP flash and no cache, requiring aggressive buffer cuts and hot-loop profiling
  • Entirely software-only port using Ghidra-MCP, DFU download mode, and cleanroom agent separation—no soldering or UART hunting
  • Bidirectional debug shell plus webcam/line-in/Bluetooth test rig enabled fully autonomous overnight development and torture testing
  • Feature parity is impressive but compromised: no plugin/playback concurrency, no voice, no dircache, playlist cap of 400, many codecs dropped
  • Author generalized the approach into an 'auto-rockbox' toolkit and reports several more sub-$20 players already booting, with TS47 done in ~4 hours

Is your DNS ready for a bigger namespace?
⬆ 49 pts 💬 63 HN comments

ICANN's next gTLD application round has produced 1,614 applications that collapse to just 1,126 unique strings after duplicate bids are merged — a near-doubling of a root zone that already carries roughly 1,437 active TLDs. The technically interesting question is not the count but the collision surface. Each new string must be evaluated against ICANN's name-collision framework, because a delegated TLD can silently change resolution for anyone whose resolver, search list, or split-horizon internal DNS already answers for that label. Commenters immediately flagged the recurrence of `.php`, `.class`, `.conf`, `.log`, `.sys`, `.git`, `.lan` and `.private` — the same class of mistake as the `.zip` delegation, where a new public suffix shadowed a filename extension and forced patch-and-pray behaviour across search paths, proxy PAC files and internal zone hierarchies.

The commercial and governance detail is equally telling. `.agent` drew 12 applications, `.bit` and `.hub` nine each, `.api` and `.robot` eight; `.con` drew four separate applicants, all of whom answered 'no' to the harassment/confusion question — an obvious typosquat of `.com`. Corporate applicants are now the norm rather than the exception: OpenAI filed for 15 strings (`.openai`, `.gpt`, `.mcp`, `.agi`, `.model`), Anthropic for two. Defenders are already rationing effort — one commenter notes Disney's legal team long ago stopped paying 'extortion prices' for defensive registrations, while others cite 300% renewal increases and 'premium' names that re-bill annually rather than selling outright. The namespace is expanding faster than anyone's ability to monitor it.

1,614 applications resolve to only 1,126 unique strings — layered on top of ~1,437 existing TLDs — with `.agent` alone drawing 12 competing bidders and `.con` drawing four, all of which denied any confusion risk.

Key Highlights
  • 1,614 applications collapse to 1,126 unique TLD strings, versus ~1,437 TLDs already delegated — a namespace near-doubling in one round
  • `.agent` (12), `.bit` (9), `.hub` (9), `.api` (8), `.robot` (8) are the most contested strings; `.con` has 4 applicants, a direct `.com` typosquat
  • Corporate brand TLDs dominate: OpenAI applied for 15 strings, Anthropic for 2, and generic words like `.beam`, `.bolt`, `.pixel`, `.epic` are being claimed as private brands
  • Name-collision risk is the concrete technical hazard — `.php`, `.class`, `.conf`, `.log`, `.git`, `.lan`, `.private` echo the `.zip` precedent and threaten split-horizon and search-list DNS
  • Defensive registration economics have broken down: 300% renewal hikes, permanently 'premium' pricing, and legal teams refusing to buy typosquat insurance
