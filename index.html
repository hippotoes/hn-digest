#1
Microsoft and OpenAI end their exclusive and revenue-sharing deal
⬆ 883 pts 💬 760 HN comments

Microsoft and OpenAI have dismantled the cornerstone of their landmark partnership: the exclusive right for Microsoft to resell OpenAI's models and the associated revenue-sharing arrangement. In exchange for ending its cloud sales exclusivity, Microsoft will no longer pay OpenAI a portion of revenue from its Azure-hosted AI services. This restructuring frees OpenAI to pursue deals with rival cloud providers like AWS and Google Cloud, dramatically altering the balance of power in the AI infrastructure market. The move signals a maturation of the foundation model business, where compute providers realize they are increasingly becoming commodity suppliers to AI labs that command outsized leverage. OpenAI's ability to multi-cloud will likely accelerate enterprise adoption and intensify competition among hyperscalers for AI workloads, while Microsoft pivots to focusing on embedding its own Copilot offerings into Office 365 and other products, as seen in its recent GitHub Copilot pause.

The revised agreement also quietly eliminates the controversial 'AGI clause', which previously tied revenue sharing and IP ownership to the achievement of artificial general intelligence. This removal removes a major governance overhang for OpenAI, allowing it to operate as a purely commercial entity—paving the way for a potential IPO. Financially, OpenAI will continue paying Microsoft a capped revenue share through 2030, but the cap remains undisclosed. Community reactions highlight Google's TPU infrastructure as a hidden winner, with multiple commenters noting that OpenAI's newfound freedom could lead it to adopt Google's Gen 8 TPUs, intensifying competition with Anthropic, which already uses them. The deal also underscores the hyperscalers' growing role as supply chain providers rather than exclusive partners, a shift that could redefine cloud economics and AI accessibility.

The removal of the 'AGI clause' means OpenAI can now openly pursue commercial partnerships without the threat of a governance trigger, fundamentally reshaping the AI landscape.

Key Highlights
  • Microsoft ends exclusive Azure resale rights for OpenAI models, allowing multi-cloud distribution.
  • Revenue-sharing payments from Microsoft to OpenAI cease; OpenAI continues paying Microsoft through 2030 under undisclosed caps.
  • The 'AGI clause' is removed, freeing OpenAI from governance constraints tied to AGI achievements.
  • Google Cloud may become the only hyperscaler reselling models from all three frontier labs (OpenAI, Anthropic, Gemini).
  • Microsoft refocuses on bundling AI into Office 365 and Copilot, pausing new Copilot signups amid compute reallocation.

#7
Talkie: a 13B vintage language model from 1930
⬆ 372 pts 💬 128 HN comments

Talkie is a 13-billion-parameter language model trained exclusively on 260B tokens of pre-1931 English text, a deliberate experiment in creating a 'vintage' LM that reflects the knowledge, culture, and values of a bygone era. The team—including Alec Radford, a key figure behind GPT—addresses unique challenges: filtering temporal leakage (e.g., knowledge of FDR's New Deal), overcoming poor OCR quality (which reduces learning efficiency to 30% of human-transcribed text), and building a post-training pipeline without modern chat transcripts. They employ an n-gram anachronism classifier, a vintage OCR system, and synthetic instruction data drawn from historical manuals and encyclopedias. The model's outputs are fascinatingly period-appropriate, though imperfect, and the team plans to scale to GPT-3.5-level capability.

Benchmarking reveals that talkie underperforms its 'modern twin' (trained on FineWeb) even after filtering anachronistic questions, but shows comparable core language understanding. The paper explores forecasting evaluation: talkie finds post-1930 events more surprising, especially those from the 1950s-60s. Remarkably, the model can learn simple Python programming from in-context examples despite no exposure to digital computers, successfully implementing inverse functions like a rotation cipher decoder. The project highlights contamination-free evaluation and data diversity research. HN comments range from awe at the model's voice to skepticism about leakage, noting that post-training may reintroduce modern biases. The work raises profound questions about how much LM behavior is shaped by the web versus language itself.

Training a model on pre-1931 text reduces learning efficiency to 30% of human-transcribed versions due to OCR errors, but regex cleaning recovers 70%—a stark reminder of data quality's impact on scaling laws.

Key Highlights
  • Talkie-1930-13b is the largest vintage LM trained solely on pre-1931 text (260B tokens), enabling contamination-free evaluation of LM generalization.
  • A 'modern twin' comparison shows vintage models underperform on knowledge evals but maintain core language and numeracy, with gaps partly due to OCR quality and corpus distribution.
  • Post-training on historical manuals (etiquette, letter-writing) avoids modern influences, but RL with AI feedback (Claude as judge) still introduces anachronistic style.
  • The model can learn Python coding from in-context examples despite no prior exposure to digital computers, achieving simple single-edit solutions like inverse functions.
  • Plans include scaling to GPT-3.5-level with a larger corpus (>1T tokens), improved vintage OCR, and collaboration with historians for era-accurate personas.

GitHub Copilot is moving to usage-based billing
⬆ 670 pts 💬 493 HN comments

GitHub has announced that all Copilot plans will transition to usage-based billing starting June 1, 2026, replacing the current premium request model with GitHub AI Credits. This shift, detailed in a blog post by CPO Mario Rodriguez, is driven by the escalating compute costs of agentic coding sessions—where a single multi-hour autonomous task can consume vastly more inference than a quick chat. Under the new system, credits are consumed based on token usage (input, output, cached) at published API rates per model, with plan prices remaining static: Pro at $10/month, Pro+ at $39/month, Business at $19/user/month, and Enterprise at $39/user/month. Notably, code completions and next-edit suggestions remain free, but fallback experiences to cheaper models are eliminated. The change reflects a broader industry trend, as per-request pricing became untenable once AI coding agents could burn arbitrarily many tokens per request.

The community reaction is overwhelmingly negative, with users decrying the effective price hike—especially via model multipliers that leap from 1x to 6x for GPT-5.4 and from 3x to 27x for Opus 4.6/4.7 on annual plans. One commenter notes they were getting roughly $500 worth of Opus tokens monthly for $10, a subsidy clearly unsustainable. Many plan to defect to direct API providers like OpenRouter or Anthropic's Codex, citing better value and no monthly expiry on credits. Enterprise users worry about lock-in, though pooled credits and admin controls offer some flexibility. However, some acknowledge the necessity: per-request billing was 'broken' for agents, and this move aligns pricing with actual consumption. The shift signals the end of the 'subsidized inference era' as AI companies tighten belts after years of loss-leading to capture market share.

“A quick chat question and a multi-hour autonomous coding session can cost the user the same amount” under old pricing; with new model multipliers, Opus usage becomes up to 27x more expensive.

Key Highlights
  • Copilot transitions to token-based billing (GitHub AI Credits) on June 1, 2026, replacing premium requests.
  • Plan prices unchanged but include monthly credits aligning with subscription cost (e.g., $10 for Pro).
  • Model multipliers increase dramatically, e.g., Opus goes from 3x to 27x, making heavy usage prohibitively expensive.
  • Community heavily criticizes the change, citing better value on direct API providers like OpenRouter and Anthropic.
  • Enterprise customers face stickiness due to data governance and existing contracts, but ROI may now be negative.

4TB of voice samples just stolen from 40k AI contractors at Mercor
⬆ 535 pts 💬 203 HN comments

The breach at Mercor, where 4TB of voice samples paired with government IDs were stolen from 40,000 contractors, represents a watershed moment in biometric security. Unlike previous leaks that separated audio from identity documents, this dump provides attackers with a complete deepfake kit: studio-clean voice recordings (2-5 minutes each) alongside verified ID scans. The technical implication is severe—modern voice cloning requires as little as 15 seconds of clean audio, and pairing it with a valid ID enables a new class of identity fraud, including bank voiceprint bypass, insurance claim fraud, and even multi-person deepfake video calls (as seen in the Arup case). Forensic detection remains an arms race, with artifacts like codec mismatch, breath patterns, and formant trajectories offering clues, but the baseline threat is permanent: voiceprints cannot be rotated like passwords.

The Wall Street Journal reported that high-quality voice cloning is now off-the-shelf, and Pindrop documented a 475% year-over-year increase in synthetic voice attacks against insurance call centers. Mercor's collection pipeline—asking for ID scans, selfies, and voice recordings—was ostensibly for training data but created a toxic liability. The article's practical checklist for victims (auditing public audio footprints, setting up codewords, disabling voiceprints at banks, and using forensic scanners) underscores the asymmetry: individuals bear the burden of protection despite corporate negligence. The lawsuit filings within ten days of the leak suggest legal recourse, but the deeper commentary from HN users highlights the irony of sending voice samples to another AI company (ORAVYS) for analysis, echoing the same pattern that led to the breach. This incident crystallizes the tension between AI's hunger for data and the irreversible risks of biometric collection.

The Mercor leak is the first widely circulated dump where voice, government ID, and selfie all came from the same onboarding session, effectively providing a complete identity package for deepfake-enabled fraud.

Key Highlights
  • Voice samples + ID scans from same source enable full identity cloning for fraud
  • Voice cloning now requires only ~15 seconds of clean audio; Mercor recordings average 2-5 minutes
  • Attack vectors include bank bypass, insurance fraud, and enterprise helpdesk social engineering
  • Remediation is impractical: voiceprints cannot be revoked, only mitigated through behavioral changes
  • Legal liability is unclear; contractors consented via buried terms, but security negligence may be challenged

⬆ 312 pts 💬 122 HN comments

This positions Quarkdown as a potential unified authoring tool, challenging established projects like Typst, Pandoc, and Quarto. Critics also pointed out security concerns with the curl-pipe-bash installation method, and the lack of cross-document references. The author engaged actively, addressing concerns and noting plans for CV templates and improved documentation. The discussion reflects a broader tension in the tech community between the desire for powerful, programmable markup and the demand for minimal, human-readable formats.

One tool to rule them all: Whether you are writing a research paper, a quick report, a company-wide wiki, class notes, or preparing interactive slides for your next talk, there's only one line you need.

#9
FDA approves first gene therapy for treatment of genetic hearing loss
⬆ 244 pts 💬 91 HN comments

The FDA has approved the first-ever gene therapy for genetic hearing loss, targeting mutations in the OTOF gene that disrupt otoferlin protein production. This therapy, developed by Regeneron (via acquisition of Decibel Therapeutics), uses an AAV vector to deliver a functional copy of OTOF directly to cochlear hair cells. OTOF mutations account for 2–8% of genetic hearing loss cases and are the second most common cause of congenital deafness in some populations. The treatment underwent an accelerated review under a new FDA program for rare diseases, highlighting a regulatory shift toward faster approvals for niche therapies with high unmet need. The approval marks a milestone in applying gene therapy to sensory disorders, building on prior success with retinal gene therapies.

The therapy is offered for free under a Trump-era pricing deal tied to tariff exemptions, a move that sidesteps typical high gene therapy costs (e.g., the $2.1 million sickle cell cure). This pragmatic pricing could set a precedent for future rare disease treatments. However, the approval has stirred debate within the deaf community: critics argue it frames deafness as a pathology needing eradication, while proponents see it as a monumental advance for families seeking a cure.
