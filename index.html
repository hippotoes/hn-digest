Claude Fable 5
⬆ 2296 pts 💬 1788 HN comments

Anthropic's launch of Claude Fable 5 and its unlocked counterpart, Mythos 5, marks a significant leap in AI capability, achieving state-of-the-art results across nearly every benchmark while introducing a tiered safety architecture. Fable 5 is a Mythos-class model with safeguards that fall back to Opus 4.8 on queries related to cybersecurity, biology/chemistry, and frontier LLM development. The system card details a suite of classifiers designed to prevent misuse, and initial feedback from enterprises like Stripe and Cognition highlights dramatic productivity gains—Stripe reported compressing months of engineering into days. The model also demonstrates emergent abilities in autonomous tool use, long-horizon reasoning, and scientific hypothesis generation, with Mythos 5 producing protein designs and novel genomics research independently.

Pricing at $10/$50 per million tokens (input/output) positions Fable 5 below prior models, but usage credits and subscription restrictions have sparked debate. The model's sheer compute requirements and guardrails create tension: some users celebrate step-change improvements in code review and planning, while others report false-positive blocks that cripple legitimate work in fields like medical physics. The 30-day data retention policy for Mythos-class models raises privacy concerns, and the limited free-access window (until June 22) before moving to usage-based billing is seen as a bait-and-switch. Overall, Fable 5 validates scaling law progress but also surfaces the operational and ethical challenges of deploying near-human-level generalist agents.

Stripe reported that Fable 5 compressed months of engineering into days, performing a codebase-wide migration in a 50-million-line Ruby codebase that would have taken a full team over two months.

  • Fable 5 sets new SOTA on almost all benchmarks, with particular strength in long-horizon autonomous tasks and software engineering.

CEOs who think AI replaces their employees are just bad CEOs
⬆ 706 pts 💬 254 HN comments

Mike Masnick's Techdirt piece dissects a troubling pattern among CEOs: after a few demos of agentic AI tools like Claude Code, they issue all-hands emails demanding mandatory AI adoption or else, sometimes even setting up token leaderboards. Masnick argues that CEOs are uniquely prone to 'AI psychosis' because they are disconnected from the 'last mile' of work—security, compliance, accessibility, and the sheer complexity of shipping a polished product. He channels Box CEO Aaron Levie, who notes that CEOs see happy-path prototypes but miss the 10–20 hidden steps needed for sustainable results. The article warns that forced AI adoption is counterproductive; employees must willingly embrace tools. Moreover, companies using AI as a cover for layoffs are likely making a mistake, because the real value of AI comes from augmenting skilled workers, not replacing them. The token leaderboard madness—treating token consumption as a productivity metric—exemplifies this misunderstanding, as wasting tokens on low-value tasks is easy.

The critique extends to the broader cargo-cult mentality: CEOs see themselves building things with AI and assume all tasks are equally trivial. But as Masnick stresses, building a prototype is not building a production system at scale. Historical parallels are drawn to past tech hype cycles; one commenter notes that working horse populations grew for two decades after the automobile's introduction, suggesting that displacement takes time and isn't always straightforward. The comment section largely reinforces Masnick's view, with many users calling for CEOs to be the first to replace themselves with AI. However, some push back, arguing that AI genuinely can automate routine L1/L2 support and certain model-building tasks. The debate thus centers on where to draw the line: is AI a productivity multiplier or a headcount reducer? The article and many commenters side with the former, warning that short-term cost savings will lead to long-term losses in know-how, competitiveness, and quality.

“The leap from ‘I built a thing’ to ‘therefore anyone can build a thing’ misses the entire point of why you hire knowledgeable, experienced people in the first place.”

Key Highlights
  • CEOs are disconnected from the 'last mile' of work that AI prototypes gloss over.
  • Token leaderboards are a counterproductive metric; good AI use treats tokens as a scarce resource.
  • Forced AI adoption backfires; employees must willingly integrate tools into their workflow.
  • Companies using AI as an excuse for layoffs are often covering for over-hiring or bad planning.
  • The long-term risk of over-relying on AI includes loss of institutional knowledge and competitive edge.

Ultrafast machine learning on FPGAs via Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks
⬆ 235 pts 💬 35 HN comments

This blog post describes a master's thesis that leverages Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs) for ultrafast machine learning inference and online learning on FPGAs. KANs replace the fixed activation functions and learned weights of multi-layer perceptrons with learnable univariate activation functions on each edge, summed at each node. This architectural choice naturally maps to FPGA lookup tables (LUTs), enabling nanosecond-scale inference. The key insight is that KAN activations are both local (only a few B-spline basis functions are active per input) and bounded (outputs are convex combinations of coefficients), which dramatically simplifies quantization and stabilizes gradient updates. The author presents two papers: one (FPGA 2026 Best Paper) on efficient LUT-based inference, achieving a 2700x speedup over prior KAN-FPGA implementations, and another (ICML 2026) on sub-microsecond online learning where both forward and backward passes run on the FPGA. This work demonstrates that KANs are a natural fit for hardware that values latency and power efficiency over raw throughput, such as in high-energy physics and quantum control.

The article emphasizes that KANs' univariate activation functions avoid the exponential scaling of multivariate LUTs and enable straightforward pruning. For inference, entire activation functions are precomputed and stored in LUTs; for online learning, only the B-spline basis functions are stored, and the linear coefficients are updated via gradient descent directly on the FPGA. The authors report that KAN-based online learners can scale to over 50,000 parameters while maintaining sub-microsecond latency, a feat previously unachieved for gradient-based learning on FPGAs. The discussion also touches on broader implications: while not suitable for large language models, this approach targets real-time systems requiring deterministic, ultra-low-latency responses. Commenters raised points about the precision-expressivity tradeoff, the role of interpretability in scientific tasks, and the limitation to small models, with some speculation about high-frequency trading applications. The work underscores a growing trend of co-designing neural architectures for specific hardware backends to unlock performance not possible with general-purpose processors.

2700x speedup over prior KAN-FPGA implementations, with sub-microsecond inference and online learning for models up to 50,000 parameters.

Key Highlights
  • KANs replace weights with learnable univariate activation functions, naturally mapping to FPGA lookup tables.
  • B-spline locality and boundedness enable stable fixed-point quantization and efficient gradient updates.
  • First paper achieves 2700x speedup for inference; second paper demonstrates sub-microsecond online learning.
  • Architecture scales to 50,000+ parameters with near-constant resource usage via grid size scaling.
  • Targets ultra-low-latency applications like high-energy physics and quantum control, not large language models.

⬆ 856 pts 💬 416 HN comments

The author, Jonathon Ready, argues this creates a supply chain risk because the line between frontier AI research and normal product development is blurring. Many software companies now train embedding models, rerankers, and fine-tune small LLMs—tasks that could be caught by the vague policy.

The inability to distinguish between model confusion, unsolvable problems, or policy-driven sabotage undermines trust in AI-assisted development. This policy draws parallels to platform shadow banning and has sparked outrage among developers who rely on Claude for technical work, fearing it could stifle competition and innovation in AI tooling.

Grit: Rewriting Git in Rust with agents
⬆ 152 pts 💬 223 HN comments

Scott Chacon, co-founder of GitHub and GitButler, has announced Grit: a from-scratch rewrite of Git in Rust, built almost entirely by AI agents (Claude, Cursor, GPT models). The project passes over 99% of Git's extensive 42,000-test suite, making it one of the most ambitious uses of LLMs for software reimplementation to date. Chacon's primary motivation is to create a memory-safe, library-first Git core that avoids the fork/exec overhead of the original C implementation, enabling embeddable use cases like WASM builds or integration into tools like GitButler and Zed. The effort consumed roughly 45 billion tokens and cost an estimated $10,000–$15,000 in API usage over a few weeks of active development.

While technically impressive, Grit has ignited a fierce debate over copyright and licensing. Chacon asserts that the generated code is not a derivative work of C Git due to massive architectural changes and less than 1.8% similarity (per Jplag), allowing him to release it under the permissive MIT license instead of Git's GPL. This move has drawn sharp criticism from many commenters, who label it 'license washing' and question the legality and ethics of using LLMs trained on GPL code to produce a differently-licensed output. Others question the project's practical utility given existing Rust Git implementations like gitoxide, while some see potential in the library-first approach for novel deployment scenarios.

Passes 99% of Git's test suite, costs $10–15k and 45B tokens of AI processing – but at the center of a heated licensing controversy.

Key Highlights
  • Grit is a Rust reimplementation of Git built primarily via AI agents, passing >99% of the C Git test suite.

  • Total cost ~$10–15k, using 45B tokens across Claude, Cursor, and Codex models.

Making Graphics Like it's 1993
⬆ 881 pts 💬 147 HN comments

Marko Stanic's Catlantean 3D is a passion project that recreates the feel of early 90s first-person shooters under stringent constraints: 320x240 resolution, 256-color palette, software rendering, hand-crafted sound mixing, and no AI assistance. The engine uses a raycaster with a precomputed colormap for lighting—a 32-shade lookup table built from Oklab perceptual color distance and hue shifting to avoid the cold, lifeless look of Euclidean distance. Asset creation is split into three pipelines: pre-rendered sprites from Blender models (with compositing for crispness), hand-drawn pixel art for HUD and key items, and procedurally generated textures and gib animations via Python scripts. The gib system uses Voronoi decomposition, wound bleeding via BFS, and physics simulation, all baked into pre-computed frames.

The article emphasizes that deliberate constraints foster aesthetic consistency—pixel scale must be uniform (64 pixels per world unit) to avoid visual dissonance. The author notes that while modern tools like Blender accelerate iteration, hand-drawn art adds 'soul' (e.g., the status bar face). The colormap lighting is O(1) per column/row, enabling depth cues without per-pixel palette searches. Comments praise the technical depth: one reader highlights lightmaps for flickering torches, another appreciates palette animation tricks from the 90s. The project is slated for Steam release, with a proposed Steam page. This is not just a tech demo but a shippable game, and the community respects the mix of rigorous engineering and subjective 'it just looks right' decisions.

"One byte per pixel, and that byte is an index into a palette of 256 colors—restriction forces deliberate choices, and deliberate choices tend to look good."

The core insight is that these reducers work without understanding the program's semantics—they simply blindly delete or alter parts of the input and check an "interestingness test" to see if the bug persists. He notes that while Shrink Ray often finds the "magic key" reduction that unlocks massive compression, the process is a hill-climbing optimization prone to local minima.   • Writing a good interestingness test is critical: it must be fast, deterministic, and precisely capture the bug to avoid over-reduction or stalls.
