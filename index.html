Perplexity and Intelligence
⬆ 676 pts 💬 437 HN comments

OpenAI Chief Scientist Jakub Pachocki's post argues that frontier AI is best understood as one substrate — a compressor (equivalently, a next-token predictor) trained on a vast corpus — with prompting, finetuning and RL merely eliciting latent capabilities from it. On that view, log-perplexity (cross-entropy loss) on a held-out set is the single most durable measure of the substrate itself: it has tracked GPT-3.5 at ~0.99, GPT-4 at ~0.91, and current models below 0.8, where he claims 'transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics' emerged. Crucially, OpenAI is publishing its internal numbers, drawn from a held-out snapshot of its own codebase, accepting a competitive cost in exchange for what Pachocki frames as an urgent coordination good: a shared, transferable yardstick for 'pacing' frontier development.

The policy payload is a proposed industry norm — shared transparency standards plus explicit 'common limits' on perplexity that labs commit not to cross until a concrete monitoring milestone (a successor to chain-of-thought monitors) exists. Pachocki concedes four weaknesses: cross-lab dataset and tokenizer mismatch, detachment from actionable evals, the fact that CoT/RL gains do not lower perplexity at all, and an unknown entropy floor where smaller perplexity deltas imply larger capability jumps. He extrapolates a ~0.68–0.7 window for the next discontinuity and floats 2027 for AI-accelerated pretraining research, citing math results at ~0.76. Microsoft's MAI-2 Base reportedly submitted at 0.819 on the same eval set — a first sign of uptake, and a first test of whether the metric survives contact with rivals.

GPT-3.5 at ~0.99 log-perplexity, GPT-4 at ~0.91, today below 0.8 — and Pachocki expects the next 'avalanche' of capabilities in the 0.68–0.7 window, a regime where OpenAI openly admits it does not know what to test for.

OpenAI claims that sub-0.8 log-perplexity coincided with 'transformative capabilities in fields like cybersecurity and mathematics,' and that math breakthroughs appeared at ~0.76 — while proposing that rival labs voluntarily stop short of the 0.68–0.7 window until monitoring catches up.

Key Highlights
  • Log-perplexity/cross-entropy on held-out data is proposed as the one metric that survives benchmark churn, tracking GPT-3.5 (~0.99) → GPT-4 (~0.91) → sub-0.8 models
  • OpenAI publishes internal eval numbers on a held-out snapshot of its own codebase, conceding competitive exposure for coordination gain
  • The proposal asks labs to adopt 'common limits' on perplexity that they will not cross until a concrete monitoring milestone such as a CoT-monitor successor exists
  • Pachocki flags the metric's holes: dataset/tokenizer mismatch, no direct task mapping, RL and CoT gains invisible to perplexity, and an unknown entropy floor
  • Microsoft's MAI-2 Base reportedly reports 0.819 on the same eval set using OpenAI's tokenizer — the first external test of the scheme's viability

#4
Derivative Flipping
⬆ 5 pts 💬 0 HN comments

Dan Piponi's blog A Neighborhood of Infinity (sigfpe) publishes a pedagogical Haskell construction that reframes automatic differentiation as a bilinear pairing between polynomials. Defining ⟨p,q⟩ = p(∂₁,∂₂,…)q evaluated at the origin, he first motivates the idea with Brzozowski derivatives for regular expressions — where ⟨R,aS⟩ = ⟨∂_a R,S⟩ lets you peel characters off the input and walk a finite automaton — then transposes that trick into ordinary calculus. An `Expr v a` datatype with a `Num` instance, an `apply` function encoding ∂ᵢxⱼ = δᵢⱼ, linearity and Leibniz, and an `atZero` evaluator together implement the pairing in a dozen lines. Two theorems do the work: ⟨p,xᵢq⟩ = ⟨∂ᵢp,q⟩, and, remarkably, symmetry ⟨p,q⟩ = ⟨q,p⟩, since polynomials on the left compose as differential operators while on the right they multiply ordinarily.

The payoff is the flip. Instead of applying a simple operator (say the Laplacian x²+y²) to a complicated product, you apply the complicated expression *as* an operator to the simple one — and the intermediate terms stay bounded: only x²+y², 2x, 2y and 2 ever appear, so a 2D Laplacian needs four tracked coefficients rather than a full Hessian. The construction generalises to any constant-coefficient operator, e.g. the biharmonic (Σxᵢ²)², and Piponi notes this is a rediscovery of his own 'square roots of infinitesimals' method, later reimplemented in the optimised `folx` library used in PINNs and variational Monte Carlo. He links the 'Collapsing Taylor Mode' paper, notes apolar algebra as the classical name (but complains quotients are hard to work with), and points to umbral calculus and quantum mechanics as places where flipping differentiator and differentiated object is central.

Because ⟨p,q⟩ = ⟨q,p⟩, you can swap the roles: a complicated expression becomes the *operator*, and the intermediate results are provably bounded — a 2D Laplacian collapses to 4 coefficients instead of a full Hessian.

Key Highlights
  • Defines a pairing ⟨p,q⟩ = p(∂₁,∂₂,…)q evaluated at x=0, implemented in ~30 lines of Haskell
  • Brzozowski regex derivatives (⟨R,aS⟩ = ⟨∂_a R,S⟩) serve as the intuition pump, including the finite-automaton state argument
  • Two key theorems: ⟨p,xᵢq⟩ = ⟨∂ᵢp,q⟩ and the symmetry ⟨p,q⟩ = ⟨q,p⟩
  • Flipping lets a complex operator act on a simple polynomial, bounding intermediate complexity — 4 coefficients for the 2D Laplacian
  • Generalises to any constant-coefficient operator; ties into apolar algebra, umbral calculus, quantum mechanics, and the `folx` library used in PINNs and VMC

A Beginner's Guide to Local AI with Inkling, Qwen, and Unsloth
⬆ 709 pts 💬 286 HN comments

The guide, published by GPU vendor GPUPartner, is a packaged on-ramp to self-hosted inference rather than a research artifact: install Unsloth Desktop, pick a GGUF quantization sized to your VRAM, set the context window, and toggle LAN sharing so the whole household gets a private chat UI behind a single "unsloth" login. The technical core is the sizing calculus. A 16-bit Qwen3.8-27B is ~56GB of weights and simply won't fit consumer cards, so quantization (compressing weights to 4-bit or lower) trades accuracy for fit and throughput. The author chooses UD-Q4_K_XL (~18GB) on an RTX 5090's 32GB and spends the remaining headroom on a 128K context rather than a bigger model — an explicit admission that on-device inference is now a memory-budgeting discipline, not a model-selection one. The article's tiering table (8–12GB for 9–12B, 16GB for 14B and MoE models, 24GB for 27–31B, 32GB+ for 128K contexts) is the piece's real utility, as is its treatment of mixture-of-experts models that hold inactive parameters in system RAM and still hit ~47 tok/s on a 16GB card.

The numbers sharpen the picture. Inkling 2 Mini — released November 17, 2026 by Thinking Machines Lab, 26.4B parameters, Apache 2.0, vision support, 1M-token context — ships as a 17.6GB UD-Q4_K_XL file that fits a 32GB card with ~14GB left for context; its sibling, Inkling 2 Small, is 594B parameters and 171.7GB at minimum, i.e. unified-memory territory (Mac Studio, DGX Spark), not a graphics card. Commenters immediately re-litigated the hardware premise: a $300 used Tesla P40 gives 24GB of GDDR5 and 14 tok/s on Qwen3.8-27B; a $600 Dell T5820 plus a $250 P100 reportedly clears 30 tok/s for under $1k; an M2 Max running a forked "splash" runtime hits 90–112 tok/s at 24–32GB RAM. The counterweight is equally concrete — a 5070 Ti owner reports 60 tok/s at 128K context and three weeks of unblocked development, while others insist a $20 Claude subscription still wins on utility for everyone who isn't optimizing for privacy.

"Inkling 2 Small is small in name only. It is a 594B model, and the smallest file Unsloth publishes is 171.7 GB" — the article's own yardstick for where consumer GPUs stop and unified-memory workstations begin, while commenters point out a $300 used Tesla P40 still gives you 24GB of VRAM at 14 tok/s.

Key Highlights
  • Local inference has become a memory-budgeting problem: the author deliberately picks a 4-bit 27B model (~18GB) over a larger one so he can spend 32GB of VRAM on a 128K context instead of weights.
  • Mixture-of-experts architectures change the affordance curve — Inkling 2 Mini keeps only 5.4B parameters active, so a 17.6GB 4-bit file still runs on a 16GB card with the remainder paged into system RAM.
  • The article's Windows-first framing drew the sharpest pushback ("this is not true"), with the Unsloth maintainer himself clarifying in-thread that Desktop ships for Linux (AppImage/.deb) and macOS with MLX.
  • HN's counter-proposals are aggressively frugal: a $300 Tesla P40 (24GB GDDR5, 14 tok/s), a sub-$1k T5820 + P100 build at ~30 tok/s, and a $0.30/hr rented 3090/5090 on RunPod or Vast.
  • Capability is the live variable, not throughput: users report Qwen3.8-27B being factually wrong on recall tasks unless forced to consult docs, and Inkling 2 Small failing a simple web-app task that Qwen finished in 17 minutes.
