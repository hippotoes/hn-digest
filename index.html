#2
More questions about whether researchers can trust OpenAI with unpublished math
⬆ 828 pts 💬 763 HN comments

The Hacker News discussion centers on Andreas Thom's Mathstodon thread questioning whether researchers can trust OpenAI with unpublished mathematics, after allegations that OpenAI models may have been trained on or informed by private conversations with mathematicians while simultaneously racing to publish results on the same open problems. The technical backdrop is provenance: frontier models are trained on massive corpora, RL on verifiable math, and internal evaluation sets, making it nearly impossible for outsiders to verify claims that user data was excluded. Commenters draw an analogy to human collaboration, noting that if a human collaborator took ideas from a researcher and published without attribution, it would be clearly unethical. OpenAI's response that no specific user data was accessed doesn't resolve whether model weights encoded the conversations. The thread exploded because it crystallizes a recurring HN theme: labs present capability gains as autonomous reasoning, but the training pipeline remains a black box.

Specific evidence cited includes Navier-Stokes and non-sofic group results, with claims that OpenAI learned Alpöge and Buckmaster were close and then ran a large agent effort; one commenter highlights '300 billion output tokens from a model still in training' right after learning a proof was in training data, calling it 'parallel construction.' Others cite Anthropic research showing 250 poisoned documents can influence arbitrarily large models, undermining the idea that a single chat can't matter. The debate splits between those demanding proof before accusations and those arguing that broken opt-out promises, benchmark contamination, and strategic 'scooping' are enough to establish bad faith. Broader stakes include academic credit, reviewer trust, data rights, and whether researchers should use cloud LLMs for novel work at all. The community also notes that OpenAI's invitation to 100,000 researchers may have been strategically useful as a source of fresh, high-quality math data.

One commenter flags OpenAI generating '300 billion output tokens from a model still in training' immediately after learning a major proof might be in that model's training data, calling it 'parallel construction.'

Key Highlights
  • Allegations center on whether OpenAI's math models were trained on or influenced by private conversations with researchers working on the same open problems.
  • OpenAI denies accessing specific user data, but commenters note that model provenance and training-data influence are practically impossible to verify externally.
  • A key technical worry is contamination: researchers using Codex on open problems may feed fresh data into the exact evaluation targets, inflating apparent AI capability.
  • The Navier-Stokes and non-sofic group episodes raise attribution and scooping concerns, including an alleged offer to co-author if a co-researcher was dropped.
  • Broader implications include academic credit, broken opt-outs, benchmark saturation, and the risk of cloud LLMs absorbing proprietary or unpublished expertise.

#5
Cognition launches new SWE-2 model, Rivaling Fable 5.1 and GPT-Astra
⬆ 426 pts 💬 176 HN comments

Cognition launched SWE-2, a coding agent model post-trained from Moonshot's Kimi K3 — a 2.8T-parameter mixture-of-experts base that had already absorbed extensive agentic RL — rather than from a fresh pretrain. The headline claim is 50.0% on Cognition's own FrontierCode 1.1 Main, within a point of Fable 5.1 at 64% lower cost, and 73.0% on DeepSWE 1.1 versus 37.7% for SWE-1.7. The methodological contribution is an RL scheme that optimizes every reasoning-effort level in one run: a linear cost-penalized reward R = S − λeC, with λe set to the local slope of the base model's cost–performance Pareto frontier, so iso-reward lines stay tangent to the frontier and reward increases cannot be won by merely degrading to a cheaper behavior. They pair this with a length-weighted reward baseline that proxies the Greensmith/Bartlett/Baxter optimal gradient baseline via rollout token counts, plus NVFP4/FP8 kernels, quantization-aware training on MLA layers, DSpark speculative decoding with an online-refreshed draft model, and prefill batching.

The efficiency story is as notable as the accuracy: SWE-2 medium reaches higher FrontierCode scores than SWE-1.7 while taking 53 steps instead of 127 and costing 81% less, making its first real edit after a median of 18 steps versus 48. But Hacker News latched onto the benchmark delta — 92.8% on the saturated Terminal-Bench 2.1 collapsing to 27.3% on the weeks-old Terminal-Bench 4 — as a generalization test, contrasted against Opus 5's 51.8% and GLM 5.3's 41.8% on TB4. Commenters also note the model is closed-weight, Devin-gated (though there is a CLI and a free month), distilled from another lab's distilled model, and that Cognition's submission came from a two-day-old account while FrontierCode itself is a Cognition-authored leaderboard. Trust evaluations — 98.0% propaganda/censorship pass rate, framing-invariant vulnerability scores — went largely unremarked.

SWE-2 scores 92.8% on Terminal-Bench 2.1 but only 27.3% on the freshly released Terminal-Bench 4 — a 65-point collapse that commenters call "the only number that matters."

Key Highlights
  • SWE-2 is post-trained from Kimi K3 (2.8T params) with RL that optimizes all effort levels in a single run via a linear cost penalty λe fitted to the base model's Pareto frontier slope.
  • Cognition claims 50.0% on FrontierCode 1.1 Main at 64% lower cost than Fable 5.1, with 73.0% on DeepSWE 1.1 and 92.8% on Terminal-Bench 2.1.
  • Efficiency gains are behavioral: medium effort uses 53 steps vs SWE-1.7's 127 and costs 81% less, driven by 'focused exploration' and earlier first edits.
  • The 92.8% → 27.3% drop from Terminal-Bench 2.1 to the newer Terminal-Bench 4 became the community's central test of whether the model generalizes or is benchmaxxed.
  • Availability is Devin-only (Desktop, CLI, Web, Fusion, free for a month), and FrontierCode is a Cognition-authored leaderboard, raising self-evaluation and lock-in concerns.

#7
OpenAI Agents API
⬆ 282 pts 💬 157 HN comments

OpenAI has shipped an Agents API that exposes the Codex harness as a managed service, collapsing the agent loop — session orchestration, context compaction, sandbox lifecycle and recovery — into hosted infrastructure. The API is built on four primitives: Agent (model, instructions, tools, MCP servers), Environment (an OpenAI-hosted or self-hosted sandbox with a workspace_directory and capability_directories), Session (a durable instance that accepts input and emits events) and Events/Items. Sessions are created via `client.beta.agents.sessions.create`, configured with tools like `programmatic_tool_calling`, `mcp` (HTTP transport to developers.openai.com/mcp) and `web_search`, plus a `multi_agent` block with `max_concurrent_subagents`. The managed harness handles command execution, skill application, mid-turn steering, transcript summarisation and subagent delegation. Crucially, OpenAI's harness runs outside the sandbox, interacting with VMs as needed — decoupling agent scale from compute scale. Pricing is deliberately modular: model token rates, standard OpenAI tool rates, and container rates for hosted sandboxes.

The Hacker News thread (282 points, 157 comments) reads as a referendum on where the agent loop should live, and the answer is overwhelmingly 'not on your servers.' neilellis asks the load-bearing question — 'Why on earth would we want such a lock-in at this stage when there is no clear winner' — while notatoad argues the providers are 'deeply untrustworthy' and should not get further into the stack than necessary. Counter-evidence arrives from self-hosters: andrewchambers runs Codex in a QEMU VM controlled from his phone, mrieck wires a Slack bot to a Mac mini dispatcher with a Herdr scheduler, and lukebuehler ships an open-source equivalent at scale. Even pragmatists hedge: baalimago uses the open Agents SDK to keep tight control over what is exposed to OpenAI, and druscacik has a genuine scaling need — hundreds of parallel Codex crawler sessions against a VPS that handles ten. Aperocky's framing that a harness is 'essentially a more complicated .vimrc or .zshrc' captures the commoditisation anxiety, while varenc notes showcase repos 404 under an 'OpenAI-Early-Access' GitHub org with no public repos.

"I think the pain of selling a harness is if your target market understands what a harness is, then they can build it to exactly how they'd like it without much effort." — Aperocky, on why managed harnesses may be a hard sell to sophisticated teams

Key Highlights
  • Agents API exposes the Codex harness (session orchestration, context compaction, recovery, subagents) as a managed OpenAI service built on Agent/Environment/Session/Events primitives.
  • Environments can be OpenAI-hosted sandboxes or self-hosted, with configurable workspace_directory and capability_directories — the most-cited escape hatch from lock-in.
  • Billing stacks model token rates, standard tool rates, and container rates for hosted sandboxes, making cost proportionality a live concern for high-parallelism workloads.
  • Multi-agent is first-class: `multi_agent.enabled` with `max_concurrent_subagents`, plus MCP connections, web search, programmatic tool calling and mid-turn steering.
  • Showcase example repos are already 404ing under an 'OpenAI-Early-Access' GitHub org with no public repos, signalling an early, unfinished release.

Nine coding harnesses vs. your laptop
⬆ 125 pts 💬 41 HN comments

The Hacker News submission “Nine coding harnesses vs. your laptop” points to a Notion-hosted benchmark comparing nine coding-agent harnesses under laptop resource constraints. Although the article body is inaccessible without JavaScript, the discussion reveals the core focus: how different LLM orchestration layers—prompting, tool-calling, context management, and prefix caching—perform on commodity hardware, especially with local models. This matters because coding agents are no longer just cloud APIs; they are becoming local-first systems where RAM, CPU, latency, and dependency weight directly shape usability. Commenters highlight minimalist native-code alternatives like hax (a 0.7 MB dynamically linked C binary using only a few MBs of RAM) and clm (no JavaScript runtime dependency, works on Solaris and ESP32), suggesting the benchmark implicitly critiques the bloat of mainstream harnesses such as Claude Code, opencode, and oh-my-pi.

Specific data points sharpen the analysis. On a 32 GB laptop with no extra GPU, llama.cpp answered “what is ls” almost immediately at roughly one word per second, while opencode with the same small model took 20 minutes to inspect its working directory—a stark demonstration of harness overhead. Local models like Qwen 3.8 27B and 3.6 27B are repeatedly praised, with one commenter claiming the Ornith 1.5 finetune is “competitive at about Opus 4.5” on some benchmarks. But methodology worries surface: humbleferret asks for a reproducible repo measuring per-turn prefix token count, time to first token, tokens/sec, cache reuse, and pass rate; another commenter questions the phrase “spreads up to 50% between nights, so nothing between the lean arms is a finding.” Meanwhile, Notion’s janky rendering and AI-generated documentation provoke visible irritation, reflecting broader fatigue with benchmark churn and self-promotional harness launches.

Shopify is moving from React Native back to Swift and Kotlin
⬆ 1120 pts 💬 816 HN comments

Shopify's engineering blog announces that the company is moving its mobile apps from React Native back to native Swift and Kotlin. The 2020 bet on React Native was successful: it eliminated duplicate feature work, let non-mobile developers contribute, and reduced parity chasing. But coding agents changed one core assumption. By late 2025, LLMs could implement Android from an iOS reference, ramp developers across stacks, and maintain parity through shared specs, tests, and review checkpoints. Shopify rebuilt core parts in Swift and Kotlin as prototypes, then chose greenfield over brownfield. The Shop app went from proof of concept to fully rebuilt native app in 12 weeks; the 300-plus-screen Shopify app is underway. Native brings Shopify closer to platform capabilities, first-party tooling, and fewer dependency layers. To prevent 'slop', Shopify built Helix: agents propose ordered checkpoints per screen; each must pass tests, visual review, two adversarial code reviewers, and human approval before commit. Feedback is remembered, making the loop more autonomous.

The migration also required infrastructure for fast feedback. Agents struggled with slow, brittle simulator control, so Shopify decoupled business logic from UI and made it runnable headlessly on desktop. A CLI lets agents inspect state, navigate, and act without touching the UI, iterating in milliseconds instead of minutes; remote mode drives simulators via commands rather than accessibility trees or screenshots. Shopify is transitioning its React Native open-source portfolio: React Native Skia sponsorship through 2026, FlashList seeking long-term stewardship, and Restyle archived. The post drew 1,120 points and 816 comments, exposing a broader debate. Shopify insists 'React Native apps can be fast. Ours are,' but argues native now wins because agents reduced the cost of building twice. The company frames the move as 'not just the same apps rewritten in different languages' but rebuilt for humans and agents to understand, test, and change quickly. Success will be measured by product velocity, app quality, and autonomous agent work. Critics warn about complexity, drift, and unmaintainable AI-generated code.

Shopify rebuilt its Shop app from proof of concept to a fully native app in the app stores in just 12 weeks—after years of championing React Native. The trigger: coding agents made building iOS and Android versions no longer cost twice the work.

Key Highlights
  • Shopify is migrating major mobile apps from React Native to native Swift/Kotlin, citing LLM coding agents as the changed assumption behind its 2020 decision.
  • The Shop app was rebuilt natively and shipped in 12 weeks; the 300-plus-screen Shopify app is underway for later this year.
  • Helix enforces checkpoint-based migration with tests, visual review, adversarial reviewers, and human approval to avoid unmaintainable LLM-generated code.
  • Shopify is decoupling business logic from UI for headless agent testing via CLI, enabling millisecond iteration and autonomous simulator control.
  • Open-source fallout: React Native Skia transitioning, FlashList seeking stewardship, Restyle archived; Shopify will continue critical FlashList fixes.

#3
Rust is tier-1 language at Microsoft
⬆ 695 pts 💬 451 HN comments

Microsoft has formalized Rust as a Tier-1 language for internal development, placing it alongside C++, C#, and TypeScript in the company's 'paved path' from local development to production. The announcement's technical core is rustc_codegen_utc, an alternative code generation backend that plugs rustc's shared compiler machinery into the MSVC backend (internally 'UTC'), sitting in the same architectural family as rustc_codegen_llvm and rustc_codegen_gcc. Rather than reimplementing every Windows-specific capability, Rust now inherits decades of MSVC investment: ABI fidelity, binary hardening, post-link compliance analysis, Hotpatch servicing, cross-language inlining, Sample Profile Guided Optimization, debugging and crash-dump analysis, and profiling/coverage tooling. That matters enormously for the hybrid Rust/C++ codebases Microsoft expects to maintain for years to come, and it mirrors the shared-LLVM story that Clang-based C++ projects already enjoy on other platforms.

Victor Ciura, a Principal Engineer in Microsoft DevDiv, reports the backend has been production-ready since early 2026, self-hosted since Rust 1.90, and is already used by more than 100 Microsoft repositories, with adoption widening weekly. The strategic framing echoes Azure CTO Mark Russinovich's claim that roughly 70% of Microsoft CVEs are memory-safety issues, alongside pressure from NSA/CISA memory-safety guidance, DARPA's TRACTOR C-to-Rust program, and talk (later corrected online as one employee's personal LinkedIn aspiration) of converting a billion lines by 2030. The 451-comment HN thread shows the community split: a Zulip participant notes runtime performance 'is on par with llvm. Not what I would've expected!' while others warn MSVC's backend 'has been falling far behind LLVM' and ask whether rustc_codegen_utc will ever be open-sourced outside Microsoft.

More than 100 Microsoft repositories already build with rustc_codegen_utc, production-ready since early 2026 and self-hosted since Rust 1.90 — with early benchmarks reportedly 'on par with llvm.'

Key Highlights
  • Microsoft ranks Rust alongside C++, C#, and TypeScript as a Tier-1 internal language with a supported 'paved path' to production.
  • rustc_codegen_utc connects rustc to the MSVC backend, unifying codegen for Rust and C++ on Windows instead of duplicating platform features.
  • The backend is production-ready since early 2026, self-hosted since Rust 1.90, and adopted by 100+ Microsoft repositories.
  • Microsoft frames Rust adoption around memory-safety CVE reduction (Russinovich: ~70% of CVEs) and compliance/security workflows like SDL and Hotpatch.
  • Interop remains the open problem: FFI contracts, bindings, build systems, and gradual C++ migration dominate both the article and HN discussion.

The story traces an unusually clean arc from orbital remote sensing to archaeology. Decorrelation stretch originates in a 1978 JPL paper by Jim Soha and colleagues proposing the Karhunen–Loève Transform for digital color enhancement — the same KLT used in PCA and statistics, coinvented by Michel Loève. The core idea is that color channels are statistically correlated; diagonalizing the covariance matrix decorrelates them, then each axis is stretched independently across the full displayable gamut, exposing variance that the eye discards.

The applied payoff is concrete. Rock-art enthusiast and Berkeley math PhD Jon Harman, working in medical imaging, saw ASTER-era Mars rover imagery with and without the transform, found Alley's paper via Google, and built Dstretch as an ImageJ plug-in — later $20 iOS/Android apps that mimic the transform via a shortcut. Between 2010 and 2012 Singaporean archaeologist Noel Hidalgo Tan used it to find over 200 nearly invisible paintings at Angkor Wat, including a pinpeat musical ensemble. Similar recoveries came from Norway's Årsand 1 site (~15 new figures, new detail in 28), Egyptian Beni Hassan, Alberta's Writing-on-Stone, aerial imagery at Vlochos in Thessaly, and a 2023 Tübingen/Tennessee protocol for enhancing tattoos on mummified remains.
