Plasticity and language in the anaesthetized human hippocampus
⬆ 106 pts 💬 39 HN comments

A recent study from Baylor College of Medicine, published in Nature (2026), reveals that the human hippocampus performs predictive language processing even under general anesthesia, challenging long-held assumptions about the necessity of conscious awareness for complex linguistic functions. Using intracranial recordings from epilepsy patients, researchers found that hippocampal neural activity during anesthesia exhibited patterns of predictive coding—neural signals that anticipated upcoming words in sentences—similar to those observed in awake, attentive states. This suggests that the brain's language network can operate autonomously, without conscious oversight, and that predictive processing may be a fundamental property of neural circuits rather than a product of attention or awareness. The findings align with psycholinguistic theories that treat language comprehension as largely unconscious, but provide direct neurophysiological evidence from a key memory and language hub.

Specifically, the study recorded from the hippocampus while participants listened to sentences under anesthesia. The neural signals could predict upcoming words with significant accuracy, indicating that the brain continues to build linguistic predictions and update expectations even when the person is unconscious. This has profound implications: it suggests that learning and memory consolidation may occur during sleep or anesthesia, potentially compressing the time required for education. However, commenters on Hacker News caution that this does not imply consciousness or qualia in artificial systems like LLMs, as the brain could be processing without recording experiences. The research also reignites interest in sleep-learning and hypnagogic states, with users sharing personal anecdotes of complex problem-solving during dreams. The study provides a neural basis for these phenomena, though the extent of unconscious learning remains debated.

"Neural signals could predict upcoming words in a sentence... happening in an unconscious state" — a direct demonstration of predictive linguistic processing without awareness.

Key Highlights
  • The hippocampus performs predictive language processing even under general anesthesia.
  • This provides direct neural evidence for unconscious linguistic predictive coding.
  • Implications for education: potential for learning during sleep or unconscious states.
  • Sparks debate about consciousness and qualia in AI, particularly LLMs.
  • Aligns with psycholinguistic models but offers new neurophysiological grounding.

⬆ 92 pts 💬 19 HN comments

Two senior South African Home Affairs officials were suspended after an internal review discovered AI-generated fictitious references in the revised white paper on citizenship, immigration, and refugee protection. The department found that these references, typical LLM ‘hallucinations’, were appended without being cited in the document's body. This incident highlights a critical challenge: generative AI can produce plausible but false information that slips past human review, especially in high‑stakes government documents. The department admitted embarrassment and announced plans to embed AI checks and declarations into approval processes. The timeline for the review—retroactive to November 2022, when ChatGPT launched—signals awareness that AI misuse predates this event. The case reflects broader risks: as government agencies adopt AI for efficiency, the gap between automation and accountability widens, threatening the integrity of policy formation.

The fallout extends beyond internal discipline; the government must now audit all policy documents produced since ChatGPT’s release, a massive undertaking with resource implications. The irony is palpable: South Africa’s own draft National AI Policy, from the Department of Communications and Digital Technologies, was also withdrawn days earlier for containing AI‑generated citations. This pattern suggests systemic shortcomings in AI literacy and verification protocols across public institutions. While the department insists the white paper’s substance remains unaffected, the reputational damage is real. Observers note that these incidents undermine public trust and expose the difficulty of integrating AI into governance without robust guardrails. The suspensions—two officials, one chief director—send a message that accountability starts at the top, but critics argue this is merely performative justice in a system plagued by inefficiency.

Maybe you shouldn't install new software for a bit
⬆ 511 pts 💬 267 HN comments

The Hacker News story highlights a blog post urging a temporary halt to software installations due to a cluster of unpatched Linux kernel vulnerabilities, notably copy.fail and dirty frag. These local privilege escalation flaws have public proof-of-concept exploits, yet patches are not yet distributed across major distributions. The post's core argument is that supply chain attacks—via package managers like npm, PyPI, or Cargo—could weaponize these exploits to gain root access, creating widespread risk. The comment section reveals deep division: some see the advice as naive, pointing out that exploits can be dormant (referencing Stuxnet) and that timed attacks would simply wait out any delay. Others argue that the old 'wait for patches' model is broken in the LLM era, where patches are rapidly reverse-engineered into exploits.

A recurring theme is the tension between convenience and security in modern software supply chains. Many commenters advocate for proactive measures: pinning dependency versions, using internal repositories, or adopting tools like pnpm's `minimumReleaseAge` setting, which blocks packages newer than a configurable number of days. Others point to organizational policies like mandatory PR guards and vetted repos. The debate extends to OS-level security, with some recommending FreeBSD or OpenBSD for more coordinated patching, while critics note FreeBSD's lack of ASLR. A particularly cited insight is that 'the proof of concept code is out before patches are available for any distro,' underscoring the urgency. The community broadly agrees that supply chain attacks are an escalating threat, but opinions diverge on whether abstinence or rapid, isolated patching is the better short-term tactic.

The proof of concept code is out before patches are available for any distro.

Key Highlights
  • Unpatched Linux kernel vulnerabilities (copy.fail, dirty frag) with public exploits.
  • Supply chain attack vectors via npm, PyPI, Cargo can escalate to root.
  • Debate: wait a week vs. exploit dormancy makes waiting ineffective.
  • Tools like pnpm's minimumReleaseAge and cooldowns.dev for delayed installs.
  • OS-level security comparison: Linux vs. FreeBSD patching coordination.

Gambling ads on social media reach more than twice as many men as women: study
⬆ 67 pts 💬 55 HN comments

A University of Cambridge study harnessed the Meta Ad Library, a transparency tool mandated by the EU Digital Services Act, to analyze 411 gambling advertisements from 88 licensed operators in Ireland. The research revealed that young men aged 25–34 were reached 2.3 times more than women across Facebook and Instagram, despite most ads not explicitly targeting men. This demographic, which accounts for over a third of all unique accounts reached (6.2 million impressions), also exhibits the highest rates of problem gambling. The study exploited Meta's detailed demographic data per ad—a technical capability now available under EU regulation—to quantify the actual reach of ads, bypassing self-reported or survey-based metrics.

The data show stark imbalances: a single Betfair ad reached 1.32 million unique accounts, equivalent to 26% of Ireland's population. Across all 411 ads, 12.6 million men were reached versus 5.4 million women, while 22% of ads targeted only men and none exclusively women. The study arrives as Ireland's Gambling Regulation Act comes into force, which will soon require users to opt in to view gambling ads on social media. Lead author Dr. Elena Petrovskaya noted that even gender-neutral targeting still disproportionately reaches vulnerable young men, consistent with a 'dose response' where increased ad exposure amplifies gambling participation and harm. The methodology offers a replicable framework for auditing harmful advertising in jurisdictions lacking similar transparency laws.

A single Betfair ad reached 1.32 million accounts—26% of Ireland's population—while men overall were reached 2.3 times more than women, even without explicit targeting.

The surprisingly complex journey to text-selectable client-side generated PDFs
⬆ 7 pts 💬 1 HN comments

The article chronicles the arduous journey of building a client-side PDF generator that produces text-selectable documents directly in the browser. The author, developing for SDocs, initially assumed PDF generation would be straightforward given the format's ubiquity. However, they quickly uncovered a Pandora's box of complexities: the PDF specification is vast and quirky, with issues ranging from font embedding and Unicode handling to precise layout calculations. Unlike server-side libraries (e.g., wkhtmltopdf, Puppeteer), a browser-only solution faces constraints like limited memory, no access to system fonts, and the need to reproduce exact print rendering without a headless engine. The piece details how they tackled selectable text by ensuring each character is positioned as a PDF text object, rather than rendering text as paths or images—a common pitfall in simpler generators. This requires meticulous mapping of DOM text nodes to PDF operators, handling kerning, ligatures, and bidirectional text. The author also discusses trade-offs between file size and quality, and the challenge of maintaining cross-browser consistency.

The article highlights that even after achieving text selection, edge cases like embedded SVGs, math formulas, and complex tables introduced new hurdles. A memorable insight is that 'the PDF format was designed for print, not for the web,' so translating CSS layout to PDF coordinate systems is inherently fragile. The author’s solution leverages a custom layering approach: first rendering content on a hidden canvas to extract text metrics, then building the PDF stream with precise glyph positioning. Performance was a concern—generating a 10-page document could take several seconds in JavaScript. The community reaction, though sparse, underscores the theme: one commenter notes it's a ‘well-known Pandora’s box of web development,’ while the author humbly admits they underestimated the task. This story serves as a cautionary tale for developers venturing into client-side PDF generation, emphasizing that even simple-seeming features like text selection require deep engagement with legacy file formats.

Automating Hermitage to see how transactions differ in MySQL and MariaDB
⬆ 4 pts 💬 0 HN comments

The SQL standard's definition of transaction isolation levels is notoriously ambiguous, allowing behaviors like dirty writes that no major database actually permits. Martin Kleppmann's Hermitage project cataloged anomalies across databases a decade ago, but it was manual and hasn't been updated. The author introduces Monastery, an automated concurrent transaction runner, to re-evaluate MySQL and MariaDB against Hermitage tests. The key difference: MariaDB migrated from Two-Phase Locking to Snapshot Isolation (similar to PostgreSQL's SSI) in 2024, causing it to handle anomalies like Lost Updates at Repeatable Read, whereas MySQL still fails those tests. This reveals that isolation level semantics are not portable between databases, even when they share lineage.

Monastery's results show MySQL at Repeatable Read still fails P4 Lost Update and G-Single Write Predicate, while MariaDB passes all academic-required tests at that level. At Serializable, MySQL uses locking (blocking conflicting transactions) whereas MariaDB uses optimistic concurrency with aborts (e.g., 'Record has changed since last read' errors). The article includes detailed timestamped logs illustrating these behavioral differences. The author emphasizes that these tests are non-adversarial and don't prove absence of bugs, but they provide a useful characterization. Percona's report on MariaDB's lingering Lost Update susceptibility under contention is noted, showing that even snapshot isolation isn't a panacea. The tool promises to help engineers choose appropriate isolation levels across databases.

MariaDB's switch to Snapshot Isolation in 2024 enables it to pass anomaly tests like Lost Updates at Repeatable Read that MySQL still fails—a decade after Hermitage first recorded them.

Key Highlights
  • The SQL standard's ambiguity allows dirty writes, but databases enforce stricter behavior in practice.
  • Monastery automates Hermitage tests, enabling reproducible cross-database isolation characterization.
  • MariaDB (2024+) uses Snapshot Isolation, eliminating lost updates and write skew at Repeatable Read unlike MySQL.
  • MySQL at Serializable uses locking (blocking), while MariaDB uses optimistic concurrency (aborting conflicting transactions).
  • Non-adversarial tests cannot prove absence of bugs but are useful for understanding anomaly handling in typical scenarios.
