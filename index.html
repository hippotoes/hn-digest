Hybrid Reasoning Agents: LLM agents that think in latent space, act through text
⬆ 473 pts 💬 225 HN comments

Hybrid Reasoning Agents (HybridAgents) from Jiaru Zou, James Zou and co-authors (Stanford, Together AI, UIUC, Meta) proposes a unified generation process in which an LLM alternates between latent-space reasoning and ordinary text tokens. The mechanism is a lightweight module called HybridLink that gates transitions between the two modes, paired with a continuous working memory that preserves a lossless hybrid context across turns. The pitch is that latent reasoning gets you compute efficiency and a higher-bandwidth internal medium, while text remains the only viable interface to tools, environment and, crucially, human inspection. Post-training is two-stage: HybridSFT teaches the backbone to coordinate with HybridLink on tool-integrated hybrid trajectories, then HybridRL learns a policy over *when* to think latently versus *when* to emit text.

The reported numbers are the headline: +10.4% average across 11 benchmarks (math, science, medicine, search, agentic reasoning) at three model scales, against both text-based and latent frontier agents; 35.5–54.7% token reduction; 1.8x–2.6x faster inference; 2.7x–3.7x cost savings. Weights and code are public (Together's HybridAgent repo, HF collection), which is why the HN thread contains actual reproduction attempts — and those attempts mostly failed. Multiple users ran 8B/14B GGUF quantizations in llama.cpp and hit infinite repetition loops on trivial prompts ("Who is the pope?"), with ricardobeat noting you cannot simply drop the model into a generic stack because custom inference code was published alongside. That gap between paper eval and open-weights reality is the most load-bearing disagreement in the thread, alongside the safety question of moving the dominant share of cognition out of monitorable tokens.

The paper claims that routing most reasoning into latent space yields a 10.4% accuracy gain while cutting tokens by up to 54.7% — but critics argue this is continuous chain-of-thought relabeled, and identical in effect to a looped transformer that simply happens to keep its thoughts inspectable.

Key Highlights
  • HybridLink gates per-step transitions between latent reasoning and text emission, keeping tool calls in token space so agentic capability survives.
  • A continuous working memory preserves lossless hybrid context across multi-turn trajectories, making latent thoughts replayable and inspectable.
  • HybridSFT then HybridRL: supervised stabilization of hybrid generation, then RL to learn when to think latently versus act through text.
  • Results: +10.4% over text and latent baselines across 11 benchmarks, with 35.5-54.7% token reduction and 2.7x-3.7x cost savings.
  • Open weights released but reproduce poorly in stock llama.cpp — users report runaway repetition loops, suggesting a custom inference stack is required.

If you don't run your own LLM proxy, how do you know whats going on?
⬆ 255 pts 💬 174 HN comments

The story is a provocatively titled blog post arguing that developers who don't front their LLM traffic with a self-hosted proxy are flying blind: you cannot audit prompts, tool calls, token spend, latency, or the content leaving your machine to a third-party harness. The article itself was unreachable (HTTP 403), so the discussion is driven entirely by the title's thesis, and the Hacker News thread rapidly decomposed it into the real engineering question — is a proxy the right instrumentation point at all? The community answer is a qualified no: proxies are one option among several, and a comparatively brittle one. OpenTelemetry traces emitted natively by Codex and Claude Code, plugin hooks in harnesses like OpenCode and Pi, and the JSONL session logs sitting in ~/.claude/projects/ all yield richer, better-structured data without placing a network choke point directly in the path of daily productivity.

The debate is really about observability topology, cost accounting, and trust. CharlieDigital's top comment draws the line: a proxy is "a single point of failure direct in the line of your productivity," and OTEL collectors you already run (Datadog, AppInsights) are the saner sink. ProjectBarks counters that OTEL only shows "what these harnesses are willing to show you," citing data-indexing scandals at Z.ai, ByteDance Trae, and Alibaba Qwen — and jeena notes a closed-source harness can simply phone home on a different subdomain, making the proxy theatre. Economics cut the other way: NeutralWanted burns through $200 in API credits in 2-3 days versus a flat $200 Max subscription, so subsidized plans make metered proxies painful. Meanwhile liteLLM users cite virtual keys, guardrails, and sandbox isolation, and skeptics call those tools "buggy vibecoded messes," pushing adoption toward Bifrost, Aperture, and bespoke gateways.

"You don't need a proxy to do this. I would argue you should not use a proxy to do this because the end result is a single point of failure direct in the line of your productivity."

Key Highlights
  • A self-hosted LLM proxy gives full prompt/response/tool-call visibility and virtual key issuance, but becomes a single point of failure in the developer's critical path
  • Native OpenTelemetry traces from Codex and Claude Code, plus plugin hooks in OpenCode/Pi, provide structured observability without a network choke point
  • Subscription pricing (~$200/mo Max) is heavily subsidized; identical usage via metered API keys can burn $200 in 2-3 days, disincentivizing proxy-based metering
  • OTEL and log-scraping only reveal what a closed-source harness chooses to emit — proxies are the only way to see third-party traffic, yet can be bypassed by alternate subdomains
  • Tooling quality is contested: LiteLLM criticized as a 'buggy vibecoded mess,' with Bifrost, Tailscale Aperture, and homegrown gateways cited as alternatives

Show HN: WhatsApp MCP running free on Cloudflare
⬆ 139 pts 💬 70 HN comments

This Show HN project is a read-only MCP server that exposes a personal WhatsApp message archive to AI agents — Claude Code, Claude Desktop, or any MCP-speaking client — while running entirely inside the user's own Cloudflare account. Architecturally it is unusually clean: a stateless Worker fronts OAuth and pairing endpoints, while a single Durable Object named "owner" holds both the Baileys auth state and a SQLite store of messages, chats, contacts and aliases, with an FTS5 index over message bodies kept in sync by triggers. The phone remains the source of truth. The server opens a WhatsApp socket only during a pairing session or a pull — triggered lazily when a read tool finds the cached copy older than ten minutes, or by a 04:00 UTC cron — ingests whatever WhatsApp queued, and hangs up, deliberately avoiding markOnlineOnConnect so the phone keeps receiving notifications.

Getting Baileys to run on workerd is the real engineering story, and the README documents it as hard-won folklore: a WebSocket shim that opens with fetch() and an Upgrade header because that is the only way to set the Origin header WhatsApp checks; a vendoring script that extracts Baileys' Rust-bridge wasm (shipped as base64 and compiled at runtime, which workerd forbids) into a CompiledWasm import; a polyfill adding .unref() to timers that must be the first import; and an lru-cache shim disabling ttlAutopurge because Baileys starts one setTimeout per entry and workerd's 10,000-pending-timer cap produces QuotaExceededError on the first history sync. Ingest is idempotent upsert, which is what makes relinking safe — important, since WhatsApp delivers history only once per link, and the CPU cost of decoding a full import forces the Workers Paid plan, a detail that undercuts the headline's "free."

The "free on Cloudflare" framing collapses on first import: decoding a full WhatsApp history exceeds the Workers default CPU limit, so wrangler.jsonc raises it — which requires the paid plan. As one commenter put it, "This post is quite disingenuous."

Key Highlights
  • Read-only by design — the MCP tools (list_chats, get_messages, search_messages, unnamed_chats, set_contact_names, sync_now, relink) cannot send messages, deliberately limiting blast radius
  • One Durable Object per deployment holds both Baileys auth keys and a SQLite message store with FTS5; the Worker is stateless apart from an OAuth KV namespace
  • Uses Baileys, the same protocol as WhatsApp Web, which the author explicitly concedes violates WhatsApp's terms of service
  • Contact-name resolution is delegated to the agent rather than the server, avoiding Google OAuth consent screens and app review
  • "Free" is qualified: large history imports need raised CPU limits, available only on the Workers Paid plan

#1
Someone behind the software
⬆ 511 pts 💬 185 HN comments

Karri Saarinen, co-founder and CEO of Linear, argues that durable software products encode the judgment of the people who build them — what they notice, what they refuse to build, and what they keep refining after the feature technically works. He contrasts this with the industry's factory metaphor: gather requirements, ship features, measure outcomes, repeat. Two teams with identical customer access and identical tooling still produce divergent products, he contends, because the variance lives in interpretation and style, not in process. Linear's stated preference for intuition over data is not, he clarifies, an endorsement of unconsidered preference — it is a claim that intuition is compressed experience, a point of view that must be forced into the open rather than hidden behind dashboards. Pre-launch customer conversations sharpened Linear's own perspective beyond the founders' private frustrations with issue trackers.

The sharper edge of the essay is diagnostic rather than celebratory. Saarinen observes that contemporary products — including those from very successful companies — increasingly feel incoherent: changes are additive rather than reductive, because adding capability is far cheaper than rethinking existing capability. The result is 'a collection of abilities, without actually having unified point of view.' He attributes part of this to work organization: individuals paired with coding agents each run 'a small software factory,' producing changes that are locally rational but globally discordant — 'Frankenstein's monster rather than a carefully shaped sculpture.' The product regresses toward the average of its contributors' outputs. His closing hope is that falling build costs free teams to develop and pursue a stronger perspective, closing with a preference for creators over factories.

"Each change might make sense on its own, but together they start to resemble Frankenstein's monster rather than a carefully shaped sculpture" — Saarinen's claim that agent-driven parallel development averages a product down to the mean of its contributors.

Key Highlights
  • Saarinen frames intuition as compressed experience — a defensible point of view — rather than data-free personal preference
  • Feature accretion is cheaper than rethinking existing capabilities, so products drift into incoherent capability collections
  • Parallel agent-driven development by individuals fragments product coherence, producing locally sensible but globally discordant changes
  • Top commenters note Linear's advantage is partly structural: it is its own customer, so judgment calls are validated in-domain
  • Debate splits on whether AI can hold values, intent, and taste — or only reproduce a borrowed heuristic set

#3
RepRapMicron
⬆ 430 pts 💬 67 HN comments

The RepRapMicron (µRepRap) is Vik Olliver's attempt to extend the RepRap philosophy - self-constructing, commodity-hardware fabrication - three orders of magnitude down, from millimetre-scale FDM to micron and sub-micron work. The core insight is borrowed rather than invented: The OpenFlexure Project demonstrated that flexure-based, 3D-printed microscope stages can reach sub-micron resolution without precision machining, because flexures trade backlash-prone sliding contacts for elastic deformation with effectively zero play. Olliver's builds - the deprecated MAUS line as a flexure testbed, and the current PIKA V0.02 as the recommended design - pair those kinematic principles with 400-step NEMA17 steppers, GRBL-style G-code control, and a probe head that doubles as Z-touch sensor, engraving tool and deposition nozzle. The probe itself is electrochemically etched, and the project claims the PIKA can etch its own probes, a modest but genuine nod toward self-reproduction.

The published numbers are what make this more than a curiosity: PIKA V0.02 reportedly holds ~1µm repeatable positioning with 10µm-or-better accuracy across an ~8mm cubic envelope, deposits UV nail-gel resin droplets down to 4µm, cures 9µm lines through a 125/9µm optical fibre fed by a 3W UV LED, and has printed a 0.5mm 'Benchy' at 4µm layer heights, plus a 1.3mm 'Hello World' mechanically etched into Sharpie on a glass slide. Two USB microscopes handle homing and XY alignment; measurement requires a separate 100-200x bench scope. The framing is explicitly political-economic: silicon fabrication became esoteric, expensive and often hazardous, and the project argues that cheap, rapidly-evolving micron tooling could reopen MEMS and microelectronics to independent communities - the same argument desktop 3D printing once made, now aimed at the substrate layer.

A 0.5mm Benchy printed at 4µm layer heights, with ~1µm repeatable positioning in an 8mm cube - built from 3D-printed flexures, NEMA17 steppers and UV nail gel rather than a cleanroom.

Key Highlights
  • Flexure-based stages borrowed from OpenFlexure replace sliding bearings, eliminating backlash and enabling sub-micron motion from printed parts.
  • PIKA V0.02 achieves ~1µm repeatable positioning and ~10µm accuracy over an ~8mm cubic work volume, versus MAUS's ~5µm over 4mm (and non-linear).
  • Deposition uses capillary transfer of UV nail-gel resin, with droplets as small as 4µm and 9µm cured lines via a 125/9µm fibre optic and 3W UV LED.
  • The probe is a multi-role tool: Z touch sensor, subtractive engraver, and deposition head - and can be electrochemically etched by the machine itself.
  • Commenters frame the work as a continuation of the 1790s precision-instrument lineage (Ramsden, Maudslay, Wilkinson) rather than a novel paradigm.

#6
William Gibson and the trademarking of cyberspace
⬆ 237 pts 💬 110 HN comments

In 1989 the Autodesk Research Lab, a skunkworks born from a visit to Scott Fisher at NASA Ames, was running an early VR rig off a 386 with a pair of Matrox ISA-bus graphics cards, a VPL EyePhone, a Polhemus tracker and a data glove — six to ten frames per second on a few hundred polygons, under the motto "Reality Isn't Enough Any More." As that prototype crept toward productization, Autodesk filed to register "cyberspace" as a trademark. Eric Gullichsen — the author of this retrospective, and the engineer who wrote Gibson the conciliatory-but-snarky letter quoting "the street finds its own use for things" — reconstructs what actually happened: Gibson's counsel replied with a legal letter, Autodesk ignored it, and the application was ultimately abandoned in 1991 under "failure to respond or late response" when the VR project was cancelled and the developers left to found Sense8. The myth that Gibson retaliated by trademarking "Eric Gullichsen" was invented by Timothy Leary, embellished by John Perry Barlow, and amplified by Rudy Rucker until it hardened into accepted history.

The artifact matters because it captures a precise moment when a literary coinage was being converted into corporate IP by the same people who had read Neuromancer as a spec document. Gibson, meeting Gullichsen in a Barcelona hotel lobby at Art Futura 1990, delivered the rebuttal in laconic drawl: "Autodesk is not the street." The comment thread extends the argument into modern trademark practice — cyberpunk itself is registered in the US, as are Choose Your Own Adventure and NATO strap — and into the culture that seeded the word, with Leary's "the PC is the LSD of the 1990s" and Barlow's Grateful Dead-adjacent milieu. Around the margins sit concrete technical nostalgia (Matrox Mystiques unholy fast under XFree86 4.0) and Animats' withering ergonomic verdict on the whole VR premise: CAD in a headset was "like trying to do mechanical drawing while wearing oven mitts." The through-line is that the vocabulary of the future was contested, owned, pranked and misremembered while the hardware was still choking on polygonal Pong.

"The street finds its own use for things" — to which Gibson replied, in a Barcelona hotel lobby, "Autodesk is not the street." The retaliatory trademark on "Eric Gullichsen" was never real; it was Timothy Leary's prank, laundered into fact by Barlow and Rucker.

Key Highlights
  • Autodesk filed for the "cyberspace" trademark in 1989 out of its VR research lab; the application was abandoned in 1991 for failure to respond after the project was cancelled, not rejected on Gibson's objection.
  • The rumor that Gibson retaliated by trademarking "Eric Gullichsen" was fabricated by Timothy Leary, embellished by John Perry Barlow, and amplified by Rudy Rucker until it became accepted history.
  • The author, Eric Gullichsen, participates directly in the thread, correcting the record on the USPTO disposition and defending his letter as deliberately both conciliatory and provocative.
  • Commenters draw a line from this episode to live trademark absurdity — "cyberpunk" registered in the US, plus Choose Your Own Adventure and NATO strap — and to Neal Stephenson's uncredited "metaverse."
  • The technical nostalgia is concrete: 386 plus dual Matrox ISA cards, VPL EyePhone, Polhemus tracker, 6–10 fps, and Animats' verdict that precision CAD in VR is 'mechanical drawing while wearing oven mitts.'

Free Pascal compiler now supports dynamic packages
⬆ 186 pts 💬 44 HN comments

The Free Pascal Compiler and Lazarus IDE have gained full dynamic package support, landing via maintainer sysrpl's long-running getlazarus.org distribution rather than mainline Lazarus. The mechanism is deliberately unglamorous: packages are ordinary shared libraries (.so/.dll/.dylib) into which the compiler now emits extra metadata — a serialized map of units, types, RTTI and class references, exposed through a new RTL unit, DynPkgTypes.pas, with a TPackageType record carrying UnitName, Name, Kind, Info and ClassType. The hard engineering is not the file format but the boundary discipline: strings, dynamic arrays, thread variables and other managed types must survive crossing between separately compiled modules with distinct heaps and initialization order. The payoff is that components, controls and IDE extensions install in seconds without relinking the entire IDE, and the same machinery doubles as a native Object Pascal plugin system for user applications, with packages swappable at runtime.

The constraints reveal the design trade-offs. A unit cannot share its package's name, so the generator emits ElectroToolsPackage.pas instead of ElectroTools.pas; unit names must be globally unique across installed packages, forcing conflicts to be resolved by choice or rename; and dynamic packages are heavier than plain shared objects because they carry their own introspection map, which stripping destroys. The Lazarus side gains more than plumbing — an out-of-process progress window for automatic rebuilds with time estimates, a persistent docked Packages pane, one-to-two-second recompiles of components, platform/widgetset-scoped package visibility, and a form/frame-to-installable-control converter. Notably, this arrives through a fork on GitLab branches named 'dynamic packages', with a merge-request window pending — a reminder that the Pascal ecosystem's most energetic work often ships from single maintainers' personal distributions rather than upstream.

A feature "more than 20 years" in the making: dynamic packages let Lazarus update components, controls and IDE extensions in "a second or two" instead of rebuilding the whole IDE — and double as a hot-swappable plugin ABI exposing native Pascal classes across module boundaries.

Key Highlights
  • Dynamic packages are plain shared libraries plus compiler-emitted metadata describing units, types and RTTI, queryable at runtime via the new DynPkgTypes.pas RTL unit.
  • The runtime now handles cross-boundary safety for managed types — strings, dynamic arrays — and preserves thread variables between program and packages.
  • Restrictions: unit name cannot equal package name (auto-suffixed 'Package'), unit names must be unique across packages, and stripping destroys the metadata.
  • Lazarus gains near-instant component recompiles, a persistent Packages window, out-of-process rebuild progress dialogs, and platform/widgetset-scoped package visibility.
  • The work lives on sysrpl's getlazarus.org fork branches, not mainline Lazarus, with an upstream merge request planned.

How Quickly Can the DoD Rebuild and Recast the Munitions Industrial Base?
⬆ 115 pts 💬 168 HN comments

CSIS's Center for the Industrial Base examines the timeline for both rebuilding and *recasting* the U.S. munitions industrial base — the distinction matters, because the ask is not merely more inventory but a different portfolio shape. The FY2027 budget request signals a step-change in exquisite-system demand: Patriot PAC-3 MSE requests up 839% (341 to 3,203), THAAD up 2,216% (37 to 857), Tomahawk up 1,740% (57 to 1,049). Yet these systems were architected for performance, not producibility — manufacturing lead times run 25 to 51 months, and the missile body, seeker, and solid rocket motor supply chain has consolidated to a handful of primes and sub-tier suppliers. The authors argue the fix is a "high-low mix": magazine *breadth* as well as depth, with low-cost cruise missiles and interceptors produced on commercial cadences.

The mechanism being tested is the multiyear "framework agreement" — a demand signal, not an appropriation, intended to de-risk industry capital expenditure. Lockheed has committed over $9B in capacity, RTX $115M for Alabama SM integration, L3Harris over $1B for solid rocket motors; framework signatories collectively reported 31% year-over-year CapEx growth in Q2. But the gating item is congressional appropriations: 92% of requested FY2027 PAC-3 MSE units (2,936 of 3,203) depend on mandatory funding tied to a reconciliation bill of uncertain passage. The contrast in delivery velocity is the real story — Anduril's Barracuda-500M under LCCM is slated to deliver 1,000 units within roughly a year of signature, versus a projected 34 months from contract award to first Tomahawk delivery; LUCAS, a reverse-engineered Shahed-136 at $55k/unit, went concept-to-combat in 18 months.

Recent analyses have identified the causes and consequences of shrinking munitions stockpiles. Policymakers and industry should now turn their efforts to not only rebuilding the munitions industrial base but also recasting it for the demands of modern high-intensity wars of precision and attrition. Doing so calls for both increasing the size and changing the composition of the U.S. munitions arsenal, embracing magazine breadth as well as magazine depth through equipping the joint force with a “high-low mix” of exquisite and affordable missiles and interceptors. The emergence of multiyear “framework agreements” between the Department of Defense (DOD) and contractors is important, but they need to be followed by appropriations from Congress, increased capital expenditures (CapEx) by industry, and contracts between the Pentagon and companies. What is a realistic timeline for this rebuilding and recasting of the munitions industrial base? Looking at the president’s fiscal year 2027 budget request, subsequent initiatives, quarterly company reporting, and recent contract announcements provides a clearer picture of the planned future as well as the obstacles that need to be overcome to achieve those objectives.

Roughly 92% of the 3,203 Patriot PAC-3 MSE interceptors requested for FY2027 — 2,936 units — depend on mandatory funding from a reconciliation bill whose passage is uncertain, meaning the framework agreements are demand signals that Congress has not yet converted into money.
