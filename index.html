Show HN: Uncracked City, detective game where you question LLM suspects that lie
⬆ 103 pts 💬 52 HN comments

Uncracked City is a mobile detective game from solo developer BNT Technologies in which the suspects are language-model agents that can lie. The loop is deliberately narrow and classic: search a real-world neighbourhood for clues, interrogate four suspects via typed or spoken free-text questions in six languages, then confront a specific statement with a specific piece of evidence. When the contradiction holds, you get three attempts to file a written report. What makes it technically interesting is the guardrail architecture the founder describes in the thread: the case itself — timeline, clue graph, each suspect's knowledge state — is fixed and authored, and only the conversational surface is free-form. Suspects can lie, but they cannot mutate the underlying story, which keeps every case provably solvable. Five training cities (Paris, Rio, Marrakech, Tokyo, Sydney) precede the harder 'real' cases, each of which displays a global solve counter that for most cases reads: nobody, not yet.

The HN reception split along a now-familiar seam: enthusiasm for the interaction model, scepticism about the craft around it. Several commenters independently invoked Where in the World Is Carmen Sandiego and 1990s Sherlock Holmes Consulting Detective as the ancestral DNA — one called the map-with-airplane-line 'a good memory.' But the sharpest criticism targeted the non-core assets. A native Italian speaker flagged the localisation as machine output using inappropriate courtesy third person ('Perquisisca i luoghi, Smascheri la bugia'), and the founder conceded: 'I don't speak Italian natively. So I have to base on the translation by AI completely.' He further confirmed the cases themselves are AI-written through a Claude-assisted pipeline, that artwork is generated, and that inference runs a few thousand dollars a year out of pocket. One detractor summarised the risk bluntly: it's not enough to avoid looking like slop — you must avoid it being slop.

The founder's most revealing admission: the case narrative, the localisation, and the artwork are all AI-generated, and only the dialogue is dynamically modelled — meaning the 'LLM suspects' are the thinnest layer of the product, not the thickest.

Key Highlights
  • Suspects are LLM agents constrained by a fixed authored timeline and knowledge graph, so they can lie about motive but cannot contradict the case facts or break solvability
  • Conversation is free-text (typed or spoken) in six languages; suggested questions carry the tutorial, while real cases require unscripted interrogation
  • The developer is solo, self-funding inference costs ('a few thousand a year'), with an MCP planned so users can author private cases
  • Localisation, artwork, and case writing are all AI-generated through a pipeline — the single biggest source of criticism in the thread
  • The landing page is marketing-only; a web build is in progress, and the Android app collects only a random player ID plus optional push token, no hardware or ad ID

Stringman, an $800 open-source room scale gripper robot
⬆ 709 pts 💬 218 HN comments

Stringman, from Neufangled Robotics, is a suspended cable-driven parallel robot (CDPR) that trades actuator count for reach: four DM-H6215 1 N·m direct-drive motors on ceiling-corner anchors spool a single twisted-pair FEP line per corner, positioning a 638 g two-finger gripper anywhere in a 7 × 7 × 3 m volume. Payload is a shared-tension property rather than a single-actuator limit — 28 N per line yields ~1.9 kg average and up to 2.2 kg near the floor, peaking mid-room where the lines divide the load most evenly. The published comparison is stark: 4 actuators and 1.9 kg against a hypothetical 7-actuator 0.75 m serial arm at 0.32 kg, or 147 m³ versus 0.88 m³ of work volume for $795 versus $2,500 — roughly 500× the reachable volume per dollar.

The implementation is deliberately commodity and deliberately open: PET-GF structural prints, Raspberry Pi Zero 2W in the gripper and Pi 3A+ at each anchor, 1080p30 anchor cameras plus a 684x384 60 fps wrist camera, 5 GHz Wi-Fi with QR pairing, and a host PC doing the vision and control — so no NPU or GPU ships in the box. CAD, PCBs, and firmware are OSHWA-certified and public; the UI is served locally and models run on owner hardware, with an optional opt-in cloud relay. Underslung geometry adds a pendulum mode, handled by active swing cancellation rather than a fifth motor, and the software stack exposes a Python API plus an MCP server, LeRobot compatibility, and a controller-in-the-loop MuJoCo sim.

"Tension is all you need" — four ceiling-anchored winches give Stringman ~185 m³ of work volume per $1,000 versus 0.35 m³ for a comparable desktop arm, a ~500× advantage at equal payload.

Key Highlights
  • Suspended CDPR architecture: four ceiling-corner winches, 28 N line tension limit, ~1.9 kg average payload in a 147 m³ room
  • Open source end to end — OSHWA certified CAD, PCBs, firmware; local UI, local inference, optional cloud relay
  • Commodity BOM: 4× DM-H6215 ($66), 2× STS3215 ($21), Pi 3A+ anchors, Pi Zero 2W gripper, 3D-printed PET-GF structure
  • Agent-ready software: Python API, MCP server, LeRobot support (Pi.05, JEPA, Multitask DiT), MuJoCo controller-in-the-loop sim
  • Active swing cancellation compensates for the underslung pendulum without adding a fifth actuator

Show HN: HackerTV – HN's most-recommended Youtube videos as 24/7 TV channels
⬆ 410 pts 💬 76 HN comments

HackerTV, a Show HN project by Danny Salzman, converts Hacker News' collective recommendation habit into a linear broadcast. The catalog is seeded from HackerTube (ODC-By) and the open-index/hacker-news archive, then kept current weekly by mining new HN comments through the HN Search API, filtering in code, and labeling with Claude. The interface is the argument: a skeuomorphic HNTV MODEL HN-1986 chassis with a program guide, tune-by-number dial (0–9), and hotkeys for mute, captions, theater, and picture mode — deliberately constraining interaction to a remote control rather than a recommendation feed. Because durations are estimated until YouTube metadata is fetched, programs overrun into 'PLEASE STAND BY' slates or get cut off mid-episode, an accidental authenticity several commenters explicitly praised as feeling 'organic and live.' Underneath it is a thin scheduler over YouTube's embedded player, with no visible ads and no affiliation with YouTube, Google, HN, or YC.

The 410-point, 76-comment thread is less about the site than about what it exposed. Commenters described behavior change immediately: 'skimming through channels is more enjoyable than clicking a video I'd probably wouldn't click on,' and 'This is cooler than Karpathy's!' Port demand followed fast — Roku sideload (itself one-shotted from a prompt), webOS, the LG App Store, a Jellyfin plugin at teilifíse.ie for surfing one's own library, plus spelunkr.tv and ytch.tv as prior art using Internet Archive footage. Two substantive critiques landed: 'Does YouTube try to block third-party front-ends?' and 'how come there is no ads? Why would YouTube allow this type of embedded player?' — answered with 'Google runs on ads. Ads need data. Embeds give data.' oever argued the filter should be by content type rather than host, citing media.ccc.de and PeerTube. The deeper insight is architectural: reintroducing simultaneity and scarcity — 'Everyone sees the same thing' — restores the serendipity that personalized feeds optimized away.

'Everyone sees the same thing' is the whole design thesis: replacing an infinite recommendation feed with a shared, scheduled broadcast — where even the bugs are period-correct, since estimated durations mean programs 'will run short ("please stand by") or get cut off.'

Key Highlights
  • Recommender inverted into broadcaster: HN's most-cited YouTube links become synchronized 24/7 channels, with a deliberately limited remote-control interface (0–9 tune, G guide, F picture, M mute, C captions).
  • Pipeline is fully documented and reproducible: HackerTube seed + open-index/hacker-news archive, weekly refresh via the HN Search API, code-level filtering, Claude labeling, source and pipeline published.
  • Estimated durations until YouTube metadata resolves cause overruns and 'PLEASE STAND BY' padding — an unplanned artifact users read as authentic liveness.
  • Immediate demand for TV-form-factor clients: Roku sideload, webOS, LG App Store, plus analogous projects like the teilifíse.ie Jellyfin channel-surfing plugin and Internet Archive-based spelunkr.tv.
  • Open questions the thread could not settle: YouTube's tolerance of front-ends and embedded players ('Ads need data. Embeds give data.'), and whether curation should be by content type (video) rather than by hosting site.

The Internals of PostgreSQL
⬆ 331 pts 💬 24 HN comments

The HN post is not a new release but a rediscovery of Hironobu Suzuki's long-running online book, The Internals of PostgreSQL, first published on 26 September 2015 and now updated through PostgreSQL 19-beta, excluding the new buffer page-lock mechanism. Its value is architectural: it treats Postgres as a set of cooperating subsystems—process and memory architecture, query planning and execution, foreign data wrappers, concurrency control, VACUUM, HOT and index-only scans, buffer management, WAL, online backup and PITR, streaming replication, and beta logical replication—rather than a black box. For engineers who administer or integrate Postgres, that decomposition explains why tuning one layer often collides with another. The material is actively maintained: recent additions include asynchronous I/O, incremental backup, TOAST, parallel query, and logical replication; the author notes a 29% word-count increase and 49 new figures. Its longevity and academic citations make it a de facto reference.

The discussion is sparse—331 points and 24 comments—but unusually technical. Commenters skip the announcement and head straight into byte-level behavior: one shows that column order changes row width, with bigint,bigint,bool,bool taking 18 bytes and aligning to 24, versus bool,bigint,bool,bigint taking 32 bytes because of padding, a reminder of Postgres alignment rules and per-row overhead near 30 bytes. Another points to the HOT chapter as “the one with the most direct practical payoff,” noting that an index on a frequently updated column can quietly disable HOT updates and that fillfactor tuning plus pg_stat_user_tables.n_tup_hot_upd can reveal whether HOT is working. Others debate MVCC evolution—OrioleDB's newer approach versus Tom Lane's reported skepticism about abandoning dead tuples and vacuuming—and cite Jepsen's SSI findings. The thread also captures a shift in learning: a former Greenplum engineer says his team studied the book alongside CMU database courses, while beginners now converse with AI.

HOT only fires when no indexed column changes and the new tuple fits on the same page; an index on a frequently updated column such as status or updated_at can silently disable it for every update to that table.

Key Highlights
  • The site is a long-maintained canonical reference, first published in 2015 and updated through PostgreSQL 19-beta across 12 chapters.
  • It covers subsystem internals: query processing, concurrency control, VACUUM, buffer manager, WAL, backup/PITR, and replication.
  • Commenters highlight HOT update mechanics and fillfactor/indexing pitfalls as the most actionable material.
  • Tuple alignment padding means column order materially affects row size, with an 18-byte example aligning to 24 versus 32 bytes.
  • The thread touches MVCC alternatives such as OrioleDB and zHeap, SSI/Jepsen reliability, and AI-assisted learning shifts.
