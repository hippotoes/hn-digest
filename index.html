Clef: Open-weight decision models, and new RL fine-tuning platform
⬆ 561 pts 💬 203 HN comments

Cloudflare has released Clef and Clef-flash, two open-weight "decision models" hosted on Workers AI, published on Hugging Face under Apache 2.0, and paired with a new reinforcement-learning fine-tuning platform. The models target the niche popularized by Typesafe AI's Jev: bounded, schema-typed outputs — noul, choice, score — returned with calibrated probabilities rather than free text. Architecturally, Clef freezes a Qwen3.8-27B backbone (Clef-flash uses Qwen3.5-9B), performs a prefill-only pass, then scores every valid schema choice in parallel, avoiding autoregressive decoding entirely. A two-stage attention routing scheme lets each option extract prompt-relevant evidence and cross-attend across fields and back to the payload; training jointly optimizes a rank-256 LoRA head with label-smoothed cross-entropy plus a Brier loss for probability calibration, with a bespoke RLCD objective as a secondary target.

Cloudflare claims leadership on the Jev Decision Index — 98.47 case-exact on BFCL, 209ms median latency versus Jev's 524ms, with Clef-flash at 38.8ms — and cites an internal threat-intelligence workflow classifying a domain in 2.2s versus 4.7s for gpt-oss-120b. Clef adds a vision encoder and a 64k context window, exceeding Jev's text-only 32k, while remaining API-compatible for drop-in swaps. The catch is price: $0.24 per million input tokens versus Jev's $0.042, roughly 6x, with Clef-flash at $0.09 — an economics curve conspicuously absent from the published pareto chart. Commenters also note Cloudflare prototyped on DiffusionGemma before silently switching backbones to Qwen, and that a community Solana fine-tune already exists on Hugging Face.

Cloudflare's Clef claims ~2.5x lower median latency than Jev (209ms vs 524ms) while costing ~6x more per input token ($0.24/M vs $0.042/M) — a cost/latency frontièr the launch's own pareto chart omits.

Key Highlights
  • Clef (Qwen3.8-27B) and Clef-flash (Qwen3.5-9B) are non-autoregressive decision models: a prefill-only pass scores all valid schema choices in parallel with calibrated probabilities, enabling typed noul/choice/score outputs.
  • Cloudflare claims Jev Decision Index leadership — 98.47 BFCL case-exact, 209ms median vs Jev's 524ms, Clef-flash at 38.8ms — plus a vision encoder and 64k context window (vs Jev's text-only 32k).
  • Pricing is $0.24/M input tokens (~6x Jev's $0.042), with Clef-flash at $0.09; critics note the pareto frontier excludes cost entirely.
  • Training pipeline: frozen backbone + rank-256 LoRA + routing head, label-smoothed cross-entropy, Brier loss for calibration, and an RLCD post-training objective; HF weights are Apache 2.0.
  • Independent community evals diverge sharply: 41% accuracy on coin classification vs 53% for local gemma, 2-3x slower than Jev on moderation, and ~850ms p50 vs Jev's ~110ms on a KB-review task.

Vote on which of Hacker News' challenges for AI have been met
⬆ 175 pts 💬 211 HN comments

stoppels.ch/goalposts is a crowd-sourced audit of a decade of Hacker News AI predictions. The author scraped HN comments from 2016–2026 in which users set explicit or implicit bars for machine intelligence — 'write software,' 'pass the Turing test,' 'solve an open math problem' — and turned each into a yes / not sure / no poll with the original comment embedded. The intellectual payoff is not any single verdict but the aggregate shape: a longitudinal record of how a technically sophisticated community calibrates expectations, and how those expectations drift as capability arrives. As commenter happytoexplain notes, HN is 'generally reasonable about objective things' — the modal historical claim is 'AI does not currently,' not 'AI will never' — yet those two are routinely conflated after the fact, which is exactly the failure mode a page like this is designed to expose. The site itself, being JavaScript-gated and AI-summarised, also becomes an unwitting test subject.

Two hard numbers anchor the debate. jefftk tabulated the vote share answering 'yes' by comment year: 56.89% (2016), 51.21% (2018), 37.32% (2020), 43.93% (2023), 42.11% (2024), 38.57% (2025), and 28.84% (2026) — a monotonic-ish decline in perceived met-ness precisely as frontier capability rose, prompting zeroonetwothree's rejoinder, 'Moving or just different goalposts?' ErrantX supplies the mechanism: 2016 asks were Turing tests and coffee orders; 2026 asks are 'can open a physical door, emulates human pettiness convincingly, makes novel scientific breakthroughs.' Meanwhile Retr0id's ASCII-art-foot challenge (64% yes) failed on ChatGPT's free tier — it saw a 'train/locomotive' — but passed instantly on a frontier model, exposing how much perceived capability is model-tier, not model-class. bmenrigh estimates a third of challenges are too ambiguous to adjudicate at all.

Context Language Models
⬆ 160 pts 💬 43 HN comments

Context Language Models (CLMs), from a 13-author group spanning UW, AI2, and Meta, propose that context management should stop being an external harness concern and become an intrinsic model capability. The mechanism is deliberately blunt: treat the context window as a file, and let the model make unrestricted edits to that file. Because the file abstraction is uniform, multi-agent systems fall out naturally — each agent simply owns its own context file. Crucially, the authors claim CLMs can be constructed zero-shot on top of existing models, meaning the behavioral change does not require pretraining from scratch, only an interface and prompting/training regime that treats memory as an addressable artifact rather than set of append-only tokens.

The reported numbers are aggressive and consistent across benchmarks: 11.4% higher accuracy with 21.5% fewer FLOPs on BrowseComp-Plus, 5% higher scores with 59% fewer FLOPs on a 12-hour EdgeBench run, and 65% greater improvement at matched compute on a 24-hour multi-repository agent swarm. Natural-language steering evolved through a skill-optimization loop adds up to 35.9 points on a context-management task, and online RL lifts Qwen3.5-9B by 47.6% on BrowseComp-Plus with 12% fewer FLOPs. The serving contribution — Suffix Cache Reuse co-designed with the model — cuts server-side compute another 35% versus standard SGLang. The implication is architectural: if the model decides what stays resident, cache invalidation, KV management, and attention budgeting all become trained behaviors rather than hand-tuned infrastructure policy.

The headline claim is not just better accuracy but better economics: 11.4% higher accuracy with 21.5% fewer FLOPs on BrowseComp-Plus, and a co-designed Suffix Cache Reuse scheme that trims a further 35% of server-side compute against standard SGLang at matched performance.

Key Highlights
  • CLMs treat the context window as a mutable file the model itself can rewrite without restriction, replacing external harness logic with intrinsic model behavior.
  • Zero-shot CLMs reportedly beat SOTA context-management strategies: +11.4% accuracy / -21.5% FLOPs on BrowseComp-Plus, and -59% FLOPs on 12-hour EdgeBench.
  • The abstraction extends to multi-agent swarms where each agent's context is an independent file, yielding 65% greater improvement on a 24-hour multi-repo task at equal compute.
  • Context-management policies can be taught in-context via evolved natural-language skills (+35.9 points) or parametrically via online RL (+47.6% for Qwen3.5-9B), suggesting it is a learnable skill.
  • Serving is co-designed: Suffix Cache Reuse tolerates invalidated prefixes and cuts server-side compute by another 35% relative to standard SGLang.

Pi 1.0
⬆ 1524 pts 💬 501 HN comments

Pi 1.0 marks the graduation of Earendil's agent harness from a cult-favorite minimal coding agent into a stabilized, dependency-bearing artifact. The release formalizes a methodology the team states explicitly: agentic tooling churns weekly, so Earendil imposes deliberate latency, waiting for techniques to prove themselves and weighing functionality against added complexity before adopting anything. The feature list reads as that filter's output — Codemode (native MCP plus non-LLM models like Jev and image models), extension-defined virtual models, deferred tool loading, cache warming for Anthropic, mid-conversation system messages for transcript-aware prompt and tool changes, a new TUI theme, and fullscreen-by-default. Architecturally Pi remains a four-primitive harness (read, write, edit, bash) whose source is readable by the agent itself, making self-authored extensions the primary extension mechanism. Alongside it, Earendil ships Pi Durable (@earendil-works/pi-durable, pi-ai, chord), an experimental substrate for long-running agentic applications, both MIT licensed.

The numbers frame the debate: 1,524 points and 501 comments, another top-of-HN showing, alongside a fixed cost every observant commenter cites — roughly 450,000 SLOC, a dozen direct dependencies collapsing into hundreds indirect, NodeJS/NPM, and a ~500MB install inside a fresh Debian container. Defenders reframe minimalism as architectural rather than LOC-based: "Minimal to Pi isn't lines of code, it's everything else." Real usage spans local Qwen and Ollama contexts where Pi's small system prompt survives tiny windows, to production Slack on-call harnesses on Kubernetes with DBOS-managed JSONL sessions, to claims of better token efficiency than Codex or Claude Code. Competitors crowd the thread (hax, Juggler, oh-my-pi, OpenCode, VT Code), and the sharpest irony is that Pi adopting MCP and fullscreen defaults cedes the identity it once sold against them.

"Pi is the 'I use arch btw' of harnesses right now" — while the top-voted counter-argument is blunter: "It's 450,000 SLOC and has at least a dozen direct dependencies." A 1.0 that simultaneously ships MCP, fullscreen, and calls itself minimal.

Key Highlights
  • Pi 1.0 ships Codemode (native MCP, non-LLM models like Jev and image models), virtual models, deferred tool loading, Anthropic cache warming, mid-conversation system messages, and fullscreen TUI by default.
  • The release formalizes Earendil's 'prove it first' methodology: adopt only techniques that survive scrutiny against added complexity, rejecting the weekly churn of agentic tooling.
  • Pi Durable launches as a separate experimental package (pi-durable, pi-ai, chord) targeting long-running, durable agentic applications — explicitly kept out of the minimal core.
  • The central controversy is definitional: critics cite 450k SLOC, NodeJS/NPM, and a ~500MB container install, while defenders argue minimalism means four tools and architectural simplicity, not line counts.
  • Strong positive testimony centers on local models with tiny context windows (Ollama, llama.cpp, Qwen 3.8 Flash Next), where Pi's small system prompt is the only harness that works.

GPT-Synopsys: Frontier Intelligence to Revolutionize Chip Design
⬆ 186 pts 💬 110 HN comments

OpenAI and Synopsys signed a multi-year strategic partnership to jointly develop GPT-Synopsys, a specialized frontier model explicitly trained to operate Synopsys' EDA toolchain rather than merely emit tool-call glue code. The stated technical leap is from general-purpose models wired to EDA via agentic harnesses to a model that behaves as a native expert user: running Fusion Compiler, PrimeTime, VCS and related flows, interpreting tool output, diagnosing timing/DRC/verification failures, and iteratively optimizing power, performance and area toward engineer-reviewed closures. It runs on OpenAI-hosted infrastructure, interoperates with customer agent harnesses, and is deeply integrated with Synopsys.ai and the Synopsys Autopilot agentic platform, with a bundled compute+model+license offering and revenue sharing. The pairing is strategically logical: the EDA duopoly controls a vast, proprietary, Tcl-driven corpus of signoff ground truth that public models cannot legally train on, making an exclusive RL partnership the only plausible route to genuine tool fluency.

The press release leans hard on trust boundaries: customer design data is never used for training, is encrypted in transit and at rest, and is governed by configurable retention, audit and permission controls. Sassine Ghazi frames it as expanding access to "ground-truth engineering tools" without compromising PPA or first-time-right silicon, while Greg Brockman positions it as a feedback loop — better chips enabling better AI. The distinction matters: the cap applies to submissions, not announced papers, so rejected work still consumes a submitter's monthly quota, because it is submissions that burn volunteer moderator hours. arXiv is not a journal with editorial gatekeeping; it is a moderated preprint commons whose quality signal is largely reputational, sustained by endorsers, moderators, and a corpus that the wider research ecosystem treats as the de facto venue of record in mathematics, physics, and increasingly computer science. Rate limiting is technically crude — it is a stopgap, explicitly framed as such — but it targets the scarce resource precisely: human triage capacity, not storage or bandwidth.

The numbers explain the urgency. September 2016: 9,869 submissions. September 2024: 20,569. September 2026: 40,363 — a record, roughly 9,000 support tickets in one month, and a doubling in only two years, with cs.AI up over 6X. Moderators report 'thin papers of narrow scope,' 'salami' papers fractionating one result into a set, and 'dense, AI-written papers' — the last a direct artifact of LLM-assisted drafting at near-zero marginal cost. Editorial Advisory Council chair Thomas Dietterich frames the policy as equitable distribution of moderator time against a minority of submitters generating a disproportionate load. Notable design choices: limits are per-submitter, not per-author (co-authors keep their own budget), apply across all categories, and papers deleted before announcement do not count. Second-order effects are already visible — STOC requires arXiv uploads with up to five submissions per author, pushing researchers toward hal.science and Zenodo for timestamping.

Key Highlights
  • Hard caps: 2 submissions per calendar month and 3 active submissions at any time, enforced per-submitter rather than per-author, across all categories.
  • Rejected and deleted-before-announcement papers are treated asymmetrically: rejections consume quota, pre-announcement deletions do not.
  • Submission volume doubled in two years (20,569 in Sept 2024 → 40,363 in Sept 2026), with cs.AI growing over 6X and ~9,000 monthly support tickets.
  • arXiv explicitly names AI-assisted drafting as a driver of thin, salami-sliced, and dense machine-written papers that fail scholarly-interest standards.
  • Knock-on effects for conference deadlines (STOC's required arXiv uploads with up to five submissions per author) and migration to hal.science, Zenodo, and viXra.
