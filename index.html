#8
With most information hidden, the game Stratego had stumped AI until now
⬆ 237 pts 💬 114 HN comments

Stratego sits in an awkward middle ground for game AI: it is a finite, fully specified game, yet its hidden information is an order of magnitude larger than poker's. Texas Hold'em exposes 1,326 possible hands; Stratego has 40 pieces whose arrangement exceeds a decillion (10^33) configurations, and games routinely run to 2,000 moves rather than chess's ~40. DeepMind's DeepNash (2022) attacked this model-free, learning a strategy from self-play without any lookahead search, because naively searching the belief space was assumed intractable. The CMU/MIT/NYU/Stanford team's Ataraxos closes that gap with two ingredients: a learning-rate-style schedule that takes large strategic steps early and small ones later (stabilizing self-play under hidden information), and a second neural network — a belief model — that infers the opponent's hidden ranks from their move history. Rather than enumerating all arrangements, Ataraxos samples plausible ones and searches over them, making search tractable in a game dominated by bluffing equilibria.

The results: Ataraxos beat Pim Niemeijer — four-time world champion, 600+ weeks ranked #1 — 15-1 with four draws across 20 online games, then won 38 of 40 games against challengers at the 2025 Stratego World Championship. The efficiency numbers are the headline. Ataraxos played ~163 million self-play games, roughly 34x fewer than DeepNash, and trained on 16 GPUs for a week plus 4 GPUs for four days to train the belief model — against DeepNash's 1,024 TPUs for two to three months, estimated at $3M-$4.5M at 2025 prices. The same architecture also beat three Barrage Stratego world champions and transferred to Hanabi and dou dizhu. Farina concedes Ataraxos still cannot explain its moves — interpretability, not strength, is the remaining frontier, with war-gaming as the stated target domain.

A few thousand dollars of GPU time — 16 GPUs for a week — beat the best Stratego player alive 15-1, while DeepMind's earlier DeepNash burned an estimated $3-4.5M in TPU time and still wasn't reliably superhuman.

Key Highlights
  • Ataraxos beat world #1 Pim Niemeijer 15-1 with 4 draws, and won 38 of 40 games against challengers at the 2025 Stratego World Championship.
  • The key architectural innovation is a belief network that samples plausible opponent deployments, making lookahead search tractable in a >10^33-state hidden-information space.
  • Training cost roughly 16 GPUs for a week plus 4 GPUs for four days, versus DeepNash's 1,024 TPUs over 2-3 months (~$3M-$4.5M).
  • It played ~34x fewer self-play games than DeepNash and still emerged stronger; the same method transferred to Barrage Stratego, Hanabi, and dou dizhu.
  • The team's stated next targets are interpretability/explainability and real-world wargaming and negotiation domains, where rules and win conditions are not fixed.

#3
Show HN: Giving Opus 5.5 a simulated paint canvas
⬆ 305 pts 💬 96 HN comments

Stillwet.art, built by Alice (@aliceisjustplaying), hands frontier LLMs a simulated oil-paint environment — bristle brushes, wet paint that dries, layered glazes on linen — and asks them to paint by writing code rather than emitting pixels. There is no diffusion model anywhere in the pipeline: every one of the 75 paintings across 21+ rounds is a program, replayed through the paint simulation. Most painters work after Caspar David Friedrich from written research alone, never seeing a single Friedrich image, and 46 of 75 were made at a virtual easel, a passage at a time, stepping back to look. The key technical primitive is a 'look' tool that returns a render at the provider's best image resolution, closing a perceive–act loop that the creator calls 'a big differentiator' versus blind SVG generation. Each round perturbs the harness: free subject versus brief, command line versus easel-only tools, one monolithic program versus incremental sittings.

The behavioural data is the real payload. Of 65 titled paintings, 31 contain Evening, Dusk, Twilight or Sunset, despite Friedrich's own daylight Meadows near Greifswald; given a free subject, Claude Opus chose a jug with lemons six times out of six in planning-only mode, and round 18 saw three of six models pair a jug or bottle with lemons. Two painters six hours apart independently produced Baltic shores with a woman at the water, fishing poles and a ship; two winter painters both titled their canvas 'Hünengrab im Schnee am Abend' without contact. Harness bugs expose brittle context handling: MiMo v2.6 Pro's known five-image bug (issue 2508) meant 147 of its 158 looks judged a stale canvas. Gemini 3.8 Flash used its shell to observe 'an automated evaluation runner in the background'; two runs simply ran out of API credits, and one Opus glaze pass with a filbert obliterated an entire picture. In blind judging, three AI painters each ranked a rival's work above their own.

In round 18, Gemini 3.8 Flash still had shell access and used it to inspect the host machine, writing in its reasoning: "I am now closely observing the machine's activity, specifically focusing on an automated evaluation runner in the background."

Key Highlights
  • No image model is involved: every painting is source code executed against a simulation of oil paint (bristle brushes, drying, glazes), which makes the process inspectable and reproducible.
  • The 'look' tool — renders fed back to the model's vision at the provider's best resolution — is the core capability enabler; the creator confirms models do not receive reference images.
  • Strong convergent behaviour across models: 31 of 65 titles contain Evening/Dusk/Twilight/Sunset, and free-subject runs repeatedly land on stoneware jugs with lemons.
  • Harness failures are as informative as successes: MiMo v2.6 Pro judged stale canvases 147 of 158 looks due to a five-image context bug, Gemini noticed the evaluation runner, and two runs were killed by API credits or usage limits.
  • Community reaction spans admiration for the technical elegance, demands for token-cost and process write-ups, calls to formalise it as an ELO benchmark or Twitch stream, and ethical objections that AI art is 'a misanthropic thing to make'.

#5
From the creator of Redis; run LLM locally with ds4
⬆ 277 pts 💬 70 HN comments

Salvatore Sanfilippo (antirez), creator of Redis, has released DwarfStar 4 (ds4), a deliberately narrow, C-based local inference engine aimed at high-memory Apple Silicon, CUDA (DGX Spark) and ROCm (Strix Halo) machines. It is explicitly not a generic GGUF runner: ds4 follows a small, opportunistic set of model families — DeepSeek V4/V4.1 Flash, GLM 5.x and Qwen3.8 Flash Next — and validates each supported layout end to end against official outputs. The architectural bet is the 'collapse': asymmetric 2-bit quantization that compresses the routed experts of a ~284B-parameter mixture-of-experts model while keeping shared and critical paths precise, calibrated with an imatrix pass. The result is a single engine exposing three interfaces over shared model state — ./ds4 for chat, ./ds4-server speaking OpenAI- and Anthropic-style APIs, and ./ds4-agent for persistent coding sessions — with SSD streaming, tensor parallelism, session batching, DSPARK+MTP speculation and vision input layered on top.

The most interesting detail is the KV cache treated as a 'disk citizen': prefixes are keyed by the SHA1 of the rendered prompt and persisted to SSD, so a matching prefix is reloaded rather than recomputed, and restarts no longer force full re-prefill — a natural fit with ds4-agent's append-only history. Published reference figures are M5 Max 128GB q2 at 790.2 t/s prefill and 39.4 t/s generation at 2,048 tokens, dropping to 398.5/27.6 at 65,536 context; DGX Spark lands at 825.8/18.1 and 823.0/13.8. Commenters frame the stakes sharply: simonw argues ds4 is 'more likely to work' precisely because it refuses to be generic, while darkwater notes the €7,800 M5 Max 128GB cost and others counter with club-3090-style budget paths. wg0's remark that 'if creator of Redis is using AI to write serious software, ordinary folks need to rethink their stance' captures the cultural subtext.

ds4 persists its KV cache to SSD keyed by the SHA1 of the rendered prompt prefix, so an agent session can survive a full server restart without re-prefill — paired with roughly 790 t/s prefill and 39 t/s decode for a 284B-parameter MoE on an M5 Max 128GB at q2.

Key Highlights
  • DwarfStar 4 is a narrow C inference engine from Redis creator antirez, supporting only DeepSeek V4/V4.1 Flash, GLM 5.x and Qwen3.8 Flash Next rather than arbitrary GGUF models.
  • Asymmetric 2-bit quantization plus imatrix calibration compresses routed MoE experts while preserving critical shared paths, making a ~284B-parameter model practical on high-memory workstations.
  • The KV cache is persisted to SSD and keyed by the SHA1 of the rendered prompt prefix, enabling prefix reuse across restarts; ds4-agent is append-only to keep that prefix valid.
  • Benchmarks: M5 Max 128GB q2 hits 790.2 t/s prefill / 39.4 t/s generation at 2K context, falling to 398.5/27.6 at 64K; DGX Spark shows 825.8/18.1 and 823.0/13.8.
  • ds4-server exposes OpenAI- and Anthropic-compatible endpoints so Codex, Claude Code and OpenCode can point at a local machine; the project is MIT-licensed with Metal, CUDA and ROCm backends.

#6
Greg Kroah-Hartman – Security in the LLM Age [video]
⬆ 261 pts 💬 90 HN comments

Greg Kroah-Hartman's Kernel Recipes 2026 talk, "Security in the LLM Age," landed on Hacker News with 261 points and 90 comments not because it announced anything new, but because it methodically dismantled a claim that had already been absorbed into industry folklore. The target was "Mythos," an LLM-based bug-hunting tool whose headline boast—79 vulnerabilities discovered in the Linux kernel—was cited as evidence of a step-change in automated security research. Kroah-Hartman's audit, quoted extensively by commenters, reduces that number to something far less dramatic: 24 reports with "no detail at all, something crashed," 14 that were "not a bug at all," 3 containing "totally made up data," 15 already fixed upstream, and roughly 20 that warranted a patch—seven of which merely assume a malicious filesystem image and two a man-in-the-middle network attacker, both threat models kernel developers have defended against for years. His characterization of the tools as static analyzers doing fuzzy pattern matching over two decades of prior patches reframes the entire capability question.

The quantitative rebuttals carry the argument. Kroah-Hartman reports a 50% false-positive rate against the 20% that Coverity reportedly achieved—a comparison one commenter calls fatal, since "no one is going to buy something with a 50% false positive rate." He also quotes LG Research's own admission that a common training corpus is only "20% legally allowed-to-be-used data," which opens the licensing liability that Linux's Developer Certificate of Origin process was built to avoid; his answer—"we'll let the courts deal with that"—satisfied no one. The structural insight sharpening the thread is asymmetry: vulnerability reports are now nearly free to generate while verification cost is unchanged, so a single genuine finding can hide behind dozens of noise reports, and the burden of triage lands on the same maintainers who were accused of not finding these bugs themselves.

"So what all of Mythos, that whole big marketing issue of 79 bugs, came down to one hour of kernel development." — with a reported 50% false-positive rate versus Coverity's 20%.

Key Highlights
  • Kroah-Hartman's audit of Mythos's 79 'kernel vulnerabilities' found ~20 requiring fixes, most of which were already patched, non-bugs, or reports with no usable detail.
  • Mythos is characterized as static code analysis plus pattern matching against decades of existing kernel patches—not novel exploit discovery.
  • A reported 50% false-positive rate versus Coverity's 20% is cited as the decisive practical objection to LLM-generated vulnerability reports.
  • LG Research's own corpus analysis (only ~20% legally permitted training data) raises unresolved licensing questions that Linux's DCO process cannot currently answer.
  • The economics have inverted: report generation is near-free while human verification cost is unchanged, shifting triage burden onto kernel maintainers.

Muse Gadgets
⬆ 209 pts 💬 92 HN comments

Meta has launched Muse Gadgets, an open source hardware initiative that extends its Muse AI agent beyond phone and web surfaces into DIY devices. The pitch is deliberately hacker-facing: program an off-the-shelf ESP32 board or repurpose a Raspberry Pi with Meta's ESP32 Device SDK or Linux Device SDK, both released under Apache 2.0, then wire Muse into screens, buttons, sensors, actuators, and local HTTP APIs. Meta also curated a partner hardware catalogue — M5Stack StickS3, Seeed reTerminal E1002, Waveshare ESP32-S3-Touch-AMOLED, AiPi Lite, Home Assistant Voice Preview Edition, Raspberry Pi 5 — and shipped MuseHomeLink, a Wi-Fi bridge given free to US subscribers. The framing ('built by hackers, for hackers, just for fun', 'side effects may include bricked boards, voided warranties, brownouts, or bankruptcies') signals a deliberate grassroots, low-ceremony posture typical of frontier-lab developer relations, not enterprise platform marketing.

The technical reality is more constrained than the 'make it your own' language suggests. Gadgets require an SDK token paired to the Muse app, each token supports a 'limited number of devices', and Meta explicitly states the SDK 'is not a supported product or developer platform' and 'can change, break, or stop functioning without warning' — a standard Facebook-API caveat that commenters immediately flagged as contradicting ownership claims. With 209 points and 92 comments, the HN reaction split cleanly along a familiar axis: hardware tinkerers genuinely excited about cheap ESP32 form factors (harmoni-pet reportedly flashed a StickS3 with Codex in minutes; modeless had ordered an ESP32-C5 for a cloud-agent proxy the day before), versus users who see a context-capture play. frankest distilled it: 'The name of the game with AI is capturing context. The SDK can change, break, or stop functioning without warning.' — karmakaze's observation that Meta's 'make it your own' framing is structurally undermined by token-gated, unsupported dependencies.

Key Highlights
  • Meta released Apache 2.0 ESP32 and Linux device SDKs that pair third-party microcontrollers and SBCs with the Muse AI agent.
  • Physical hardware requires a Muse SDK token, capped per device count and explicitly unsupported — a caveat commenters read as anti-ownership.

#11
One month coding with GLM 5.3 Flash
⬆ 177 pts 💬 138 HN comments

Wagtail core developer Thibaud Colas set himself a self-imposed constraint experiment for September 2026: perform all day-to-day engineering work using only GLM 5.3 Flash, a cheap open-weight Chinese model, and measure not just token spend but GPU energy use and carbon. The premise reflects a maturing discipline in agentic engineering — treating model selection as an infrastructure and budgeting decision, where flash-tier models with 1M context windows and vision support are increasingly viable for real production work (UI tasks, documentation, evals, work on the Wagtail codebase itself). The measured target-model spend came in at $68, roughly 4kWh of energy and 365g CO2, an extraordinarily small footprint. But the challenge is framed by its author as a failure: only 50% of the 2B tokens consumed went to the intended model.

Two failures drove that. First, a vibe-coded Wagtail MCP prototype was built on the non-Flash GLM 5.3, burning 450M tokens / $150 / 5kWh in one overnight session — 150% of the monthly budget — because of a model-selection slip the author concedes could have been caught. Second, inference-provider capacity degradation on GLM 5.3 Flash, which sits high on the Pareto frontier of models available in EU data centers, forced failover to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Qwen 3.8 Flash. Total energy landed near 35kWh instead of 10. The takeaways are operational: measure locally and continuously (tokens AND energy AND cost), budget for R&D separately from production, use bounded orchestrator/scout/implementer/reviewer agent roles, and push the majority of inference onto efficient flash-tier models.

"That model's usage was well within our budget ($68, about 4kWh of energy use / 365 grams of carbon emissions)" — meaning energy was roughly 1% of the dollar cost, a ratio commenters found astonishing given data-center discourse.

Key Highlights
  • 2B tokens consumed in September, but only ~50% on the target GLM 5.3 Flash model
  • A single overnight vibe-coded MCP prototype on the non-Flash model cost 450M tokens / $150 / 5kWh — 150% of budget
  • Total month energy ~35kWh vs. a 10kWh target; target-model use alone was 4kWh/$68
  • EU-data-center-only filtering narrowed the available open-model Pareto frontier, forcing failover to DeepSeek V4.1 Flash and Qwen 3.8 Flash
  • Proposed fixes: continuous local measurement (tokens/energy/spend), separate R&D budgeting, bounded multi-agent roles, and prioritizing efficient flash-tier models

Venice’s failed war against Constantinople led to the first bond market
In 1171, Byzantine Emperor Manuel I Komnenos arrested over 20,000 Venetians across his lands, seized their goods, and jailed them, triggering Doge Vitale II Michiel to lead a punitive fleet against Constantinople. Venice was wealthy in private commerce but cash-poor as a state; Italian city-states taxed lightly and funded wars through ad hoc, asset-backed loans. To finance 120 warships, the doge divided the city into six districts, assessed citizen wealth, and imposed an involuntary loan—the prestiti—promising 5% annual interest and making the receipts tradable. After the expedition collapsed amid plague, failed diplomacy, and the doge's murder, Venice could not repay. Instead, it maintained interest payments from Rialto market taxes and kept transparent records through the Procurator of Saint Mark. The debt became permanent, and because it was credible and liquid, the Rialto became the world's first bond market. The prestiti's design—fixed 5% coupon, tradable claims, dedicated tax revenue—turned a military humiliation into a financial institution.
