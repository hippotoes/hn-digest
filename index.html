#3
Build your own decision model
⬆ 393 pts 💬 87 HN comments

Nish Tahir's "Build your own decision model" reverse-engineers the class of narrow, single-pass classifier models popularised by Jev, arguing they are not a new architecture but a disciplined application of constrained decoding. The core mechanism is simple and well-understood: run a causal LM once over the prompt, take the logits at the final position, and select only over the token IDs corresponding to allowed answers (A–E) rather than softmaxing the full vocabulary. That collapses generation from N sequential forward passes — one per output token — into a single prefill, eliminating autoregressive latency entirely. The author demonstrates this with Qwen3-1.7B, encoding each option's first token via tokenizer.encode(opt, add_special_tokens=False)[0], formatting a multiple-choice prompt through apply_chat_template with enable_thinking=False, and reading torch.softmax(logits[option_token_ids]) to produce both a prediction and a per-option probability vector.

The post is most valuable for what it says about the numbers behind such probabilities. On a 1,221-sample CommonsenseQA holdout, the zero-shot constrained model scores 0.5938 accuracy (macro F1 0.5844); a quick finetune lifts that to 0.6241. But the raw output probabilities are badly calibrated: 809 of 1,221 predictions fall in the 0.90–1.00 confidence bin, where mean confidence is 0.9855 against actual accuracy of 0.7009. Temperature scaling — a single fitted parameter of T≈3.797 — redistributes those bin populations (109 in the top bin at 0.9333 confidence versus 0.9541 accuracy), producing a plausibly calibrated curve. The ambiguity test ("Where would you most likely find a bat?") exposes the failure mode bluntly: 0.9978 confidence for "Cave," despite genuinely contested options. The implicit warning is that masked-token probabilities measure next-token likelihood, not answer correctness, and calibration is a separate, fittable layer on top.

Of 1,221 CommonsenseQA predictions, 809 landed in the 0.90–1.00 confidence bin with mean confidence 0.9855 — but only 70.09% were correct; a fitted temperature of 3.797 is what makes those scores mean anything.

Key Highlights
  • Constrained decoding over a masked option-token subset reduces decision-making to one forward pass rather than one pass per generated token.
  • A 1.7B Qwen3 model reaches 0.5938 accuracy on a 1,221-sample CommonsenseQA holdout, rising to 0.6241 after a short finetune.
  • Raw softmax probabilities are severely overconfident: 809/1,221 predictions sit in the 0.90–1.00 bin at 0.9855 mean confidence but 0.7009 accuracy.
  • Temperature scaling (T≈3.797) — essentially Platt-style calibration — brings binned confidence into rough alignment with empirical accuracy.
  • Commenters note token probabilities reflect next-token likelihood, not answer correctness, and dispute whether such models are architecturally novel.

The Lightbulb Computer
⬆ 502 pts 💬 77 HN comments

Guillaume Ardaud, a French/American interface designer who spent over eight years protottyping at Apple on Face ID, Apple Pencil, and the iPhone camera system, has published a speculative hardware concept he calls the Lightbulb Computer: a projector fused with a computer-vision stack inside an Edison-screw bulb form factor. The thesis explicitly rejects the industry's eyewear-centric push toward VR/AR/MR headsets as constrained, thermally and computationally throttled, and 'fundamentally antisocial.' Instead, the device exploits a near-ubiquitous standard—the ceiling light socket—as both mounting point and vantage: a room's apex offers unobstructed sightlines for downward projection onto any surface. The reference prototype is deliberately bulky: a Mac running bespoke projection-mapping and rendering software, Apple's hand-tracking framework, a small consumer 4K laser projector, and a webcam for scene understanding.

The demos—un-edited, non-AI-generated, and captured from that real rig—span kitchen mise-en-place widgets, question-answering over books, shared map navigation with gesture pan and transit overlays, projected photo viewing, board games, point-to-control smart home devices, ambient bedside clock/calendar displays, and weather-overlaid 3D terrain maps. Ardaud situates the work in a lineage running from MIT's I/O Bulb and LuminAR through Dynamicland and Folk Computer to the Humane Pin, arguing none addressed personal home computing. Privacy is framed as first-order: on-device inference, hardware camera guardrails, a hard-wired indicator, and a physical shutter. With 502 points and 77 comments, the thread's fault lines are clear—awe at the interaction model versus unease about an always-listening 'jinn' and a potential 1984-style telescreen.

'A jinn which is always ready to grant your wish, but never leaves you alone, ever watching and listening.' — nine_k captures the thread's central tension between ambient magic and ambient surveillance.

Key Highlights
  • Projector plus computer vision mounted in a standard Edison lightbulb socket, leveraging the ceiling as an optimal room-scale projection and sensing vantage point
  • Explicit repudiation of eyewear-based computing (VR/AR/MR) as thermally constrained, power-limited, and antisocial versus shared, in-room projection
  • Real prototype: Mac with custom projection-mapping software, Apple hand tracking, consumer 4K laser projector, and webcam—no video editing or AI generation
  • Privacy architecture proposed via on-device processing, hardware camera guardrails, wired indicator lights, and physical covers, with author preferring a local open-source variant
  • Situated in a research lineage including I/O Bulb, LuminAR, Dynamicland, Folk Computer, and cancelled FAANG projector-assistant experiments

#5
Knuth reward check
⬆ 209 pts 💬 76 HN comments

Thomas Hühn recounts finding an error in the first word of the first paragraph of page 1 of Donald Knuth's *Computer Modern Typefaces* — Volume E of the *Computers & Typesetting* series — and receiving the legendary Knuth reward check, memo field 'E1'. The claim, that 'infinitely many alphabets can be generated' by METAFONT's parameterization, is mathematically false: the alphabet space is bounded by a finite parameter set with finite value ranges (followers compute it as 268,435,456^62 configurations). Knuth's standing offer — $2.56 per error, doubling for meaningful suggestions — has functioned for decades as a distributed, adversarial proofreading protocol over TeX, METAFONT, and TAOCP, with a certificate from the fictional Bank of San Serriffe replacing real cheques. Hühn's second submission was rejected, but a throwaway remark earned a fractional $0.32 credit — the ledger reads 0x$1.20 = $2.88.

The thread turns into a registry of holders and a referendum on whether LLMs have terminated the ritual. WillAdams cites check #790 from 25+ years ago; bsoles holds a $32 cheque from Bjarne Stroustrup for an error in *The C++ Programming Language*; jll29 earned one for correcting Knuth's attribution of Enigma breaking to Turing rather than Marian Rejewski. gucci-on-fleek notes that recent TeX bug reports 'explicitly acknowledge AI assistance,' while bariswheel calls it 'the end of an era.' The irony of the day: several commenters nitpicked the post's missing trailing slash, which broke relative image URLs — pedantry about pedantry, executed in public, at exactly the level of scrutiny Knuth's bounty was designed to cultivate.

Knuth's bounty is denominated in hexadecimal on a fantasy bank's paper — yet it remains the most credible peer-review incentive in computer science: $2.56 per error, 2× for a real improvement, and a check number people remember decades later.

Key Highlights
  • Knuth's error bounty ($2.56, hex-denominated, Bank of San Serriffe certificates) has operated as a decades-long distributed proofreading protocol for TeX, METAFONT and TAOCP.
  • Hühn's find is a boundedness error: 'infinitely many alphabets' is false because METAFONT's parameter space is finite; the 2021 edition reportedly reads 'Zillions of alphabets.'
  • Commenters maintain a de facto registry of checks: WillAdams #790, bsoles holds a $32 Stroustrup analogue, jll29 corrected the Rejewski/Turing Enigma attribution.
  • AI is already in the loop: TeX bug reports acknowledge AI assistance, and commenters debate whether mass LLM errata submission would collapse the reward's economics or its meaning.
  • The thread's own meta-nitpicking — a missing trailing slash breaking relative image URLs — mirrors the article's subject at one remove.

#6
Unikernels were hard. key word: were
⬆ 177 pts 💬 89 HN comments

Geoffrey Huntley's write-up of a pub-side conversation with Justin Cormack (MirageOS, Unikernel Systems) argues that unikernels were hard — past tense — because the friction was never conceptual but logistical: with no userland, no fork/exec and no shell, every TCP stack, DNS resolver, storage driver and payment client must exist as a linked library inside the application. MirageOS in 2015 had HTTPS but almost nothing for storage, so the team pulled NetBSD drivers into userspace. Huntley's thesis is that these hard problems — like Nix and Bazel — are now resident in model weights, so the difficulty is promptable away. The deeper claim is architectural: the multi-user operating system is design debt inherited from the era of a human operator in front of a mainframe, and twenty-six years of "don't put the compiler on production" has been incremental attack-surface trimming rather than inverting the design.

The concrete artifacts are what make the argument testable. Cormack had an agent reverse-engineer XFS on-disk formats to emit a byte-for-byte identical mkfs.xfs in Rust, validated by diffing against the original as a golden oracle across block sizes and flags; porting becomes a loop whenever an oracle exists. Huntley's Mirage folder includes a ported NTP client and a RADclock/TigerBeetle-inspired multi-source clock, plus "Spaceleans" — Microsoft Orleans' distributed actor system ported to OCaml and running as a unikernel, built in about a week. His gen-Z language Cursed cost roughly US$6,000, three times over, versus Go's corporate spend. HN replies push back hard: scottlamb swaps "attack surface" for "observability surface" and notes strace, eBPF, lsof and ss are lost wholesale; KaiserPro asks who tracks CVEs in ported libraries; fwip notes memory corruption still yields arbitrary machine code. Meanwhile a KVM escape paid out just $50,000.

"If there's no shell and no interpreter, there's nothing in the model weights that knows what to do next" — turning an automated drive-by into a targeted attack that needs your source code.

Key Highlights
  • Unikernels were hard for logistical reasons — no userland means libraries for TCP/IP, DNS, storage and payments must be linked in, not spawned — and Huntley argues LLMs have dissolved that friction to the point of prompting for it.
  • Cormack's mkfs.xfs-in-Rust experiment shows porting is a mechanical loop when a golden oracle exists: generate artifacts at multiple block sizes with both implementations and diff them.
  • Huntley built "Spaceleans" (Orleans ported to OCaml) as a distributed unikernel operating system in roughly a week, plus NTP, DNS, OTel, structured logging and PII-scrubbing at the logging boundary.
  • The security argument is contested: removing the shell destroys the attacker's next hop, but commenters note interpreters, memory-safety gadgets and the maintenance burden of ported libraries remain, and no benchmarks are offered.
  • The piece lands a broader cultural claim — AI use is becoming a minimum employability standard, and the labs trained on the commons — with Huntley urging managers to carve out experimentation time now.

The RAM shortage is bringing back DDR4
⬆ 121 pts 💬 106 HN comments

The Verge reports a genuine architectural backpedal: Gigabyte has announced that two motherboard lines will support "upcoming" processors on Intel's LGA 1700 socket — the 2021 Alder Lake platform that Intel abandoned for DDR5-only in 2024 — with new chips expected in early 2027. AMD is running the same play, reviving AM4 boards alongside a 10th-anniversary Ryzen 7 5800X3D relaunch. The mechanism is straightforward: LGA 1700 and AM4 carry memory controllers that already speak both DDR4 and DDR5 PHYs, so vendors can re-spin existing silicon rather than fund new tape-outs. DDR4 (2014) loses to DDR5 on bandwidth, on-die ECC, the on-DIMM PMIC, and subchannel granularity — but it retains a mature, cheap supply chain that AI datacenter HBM and server DDR5 demand has not fully cannibalized.

The economics are stark. A 32GB DDR5 Corsair Vengeance kit now runs $620 versus $260 for the DDR4 equivalent — more than double, and above what DDR4 cost in mid-2025. The Verge anchors this in a broader shortage narrative: PC shipments fell roughly 20% in Q3, Nvidia's Shield TV Pro jumped $100, and SSD prices are climbing as NAND and DRAM wafer capacity is reallocated toward AI accelerators. Commenters supply the ground truth: 512GB of registered DDR4-2133 ECC bought for $536 in 2024, 128GB DDR5 non-ECC now quoted near €2,000, and DDR3 R910/R920 servers appreciating on eBay. The comeback is less a technical vindication of DDR4 than a visible symptom of memory capacity being repriced as an AI input.

DDR5 32GB Corsair Vengeance kit: $620. The DDR4 equivalent: $260. "Somehow, DDR4 has returned."

HN reads the story as confirmation of a structural, AI-driven memory repricing — and splits between e-waste opportunists cashing in on cheap server RAM, skeptics calling the shortage artificial, and fatalists noting that consumer tech has now visibly regressed for the first time in decades.

Commenters document a booming secondary market: decommissioned server DDR4 ECC at $536 for 512GB in 2024, Chinese X79/X99 frankenboards soaking up scrap Xeons, and DDR3 servers gaining value.

The community consensus is that capacity (GB) matters far more than generation for most workloads — DDR4-3200 remains adequate for the majority of consumer and even gaming use cases.

#8
Rat's Register Allocator
⬆ 31 pts 💬 2 HN comments

Register allocation is one of the classic hard problems in compiler backends: mapping an unbounded set of virtual registers onto a dozen or so physical ones, spilling to the stack when pressure exceeds capacity, all while respecting the ABI's caller-saved/callee-saved split. The author of 'rat' — a smallish compiler backend with a semi-working C99 frontend — dismantles an aging linear-scan allocator of 1,392 lines, measures which features actually earn their keep, and rebuilds the whole thing as a 584-line priority bin-packing allocator in the same family as LLVM's greedy allocator, minus eviction, range splitting, and multi-pass retries. The five-step pipeline — live ranges, fixed registers, coalescing, picking, spilling — is described with unusual concreteness: slot-pair numbering (reads at 2i, writes at 2i+1), busy masks packed as U64 bitmaps with 64-slot OR summaries, union-find bundles, and the priority formula weight/sqrt(length). The charming emergent behaviour: nothing in the allocator says 'put call-crossing values in callee-saved registers' — it simply falls out of ordering caller-saved first, callee-saved last.

The measurement story is the real payload. Deleting features one at a time revealed that copy coalescing plus copy hints accounted for roughly a third of the code quality, live-range holes 10%, spill choice by use weight 5.5%, and hints to physical registers just 1%. The optimistic second try at spilled ranges consumed 37% of allocator time for a 0.01% instruction improvement, and rematerialization and the spill-slot cache were effectively free to drop. Net results against the old allocator: 58% fewer source lines, 3.6% fewer instructions emitted, and — because the allocator was so slow at -O0 — 77% less allocator time and 43% less total sqlite compile time, where stores dropped 22%. The author is candid about the cost of no eviction: a three-mov chain in a naive sum loop, shrugged off as 'three cold movs are not worth the extra code.' The lesson, stated plainly: 'measure the old code before I port it. Much of the old allocator did nothing.'

Rewriting a 1,392-line linear-scan allocator as a 584-line bin-packing allocator cut emitted instructions by 3.6% and sqlite -O0 total compile time by 43% — and one feature that consumed 37% of allocator runtime bought just 0.01% code quality.

Key Highlights
  • A 1,392-line linear-scan allocator was replaced by a 584-line priority bin-packing allocator modeled on LLVM's greedy allocator, minus eviction, splitting, and second passes.
  • Feature ablation showed coalescing/copy hints worth ~⅓ of code quality, live-range holes 10%, use-weight spill choice 5.5%, and physical-register hints 1%.
  • Clever encoding: registers numbered 1–40 fit in a single U64, slot busy-masks enable 64-slot-at-a-time scanning, and union-find bundles are merged by loop depth, deepest first.
  • The 'no eviction' design makes early decisions final, producing a three-mov chain in a naive sum loop the author accepts as too cold to optimize.
  • Results: -58% source lines, -3.6% instructions, -22% stores, -77% allocator time and -43% total compile time on sqlite at -O0.

Joel Sik's latest post from his from-scratch AArch64 operating system, floss, traces a single bug from a basic spin lock to the deepest corners of the Arm architecture. On real Raspberry Pi 5 hardware, however, the very first ldaxr raises a Data Abort (EC 37) with syndrome 0x96000410. Swapping in a plain ldar/str pair eliminates the fault, isolating the failure to exclusive operations. The diagnosis walks through memory types (Device vs. Normal), MAIR_EL1 attribute encodings, translation-table shareability bits, and Arm's guarantee that atomics are architecturally atomic only for Inner/Outer Write-Back, non-transient Normal memory.

The root cause is layered and beautifully non-obvious: the System Control Register bit SCTLR.C defaults to 0, disabling the data cache at EL1, so all Normal mappings are treated as Non-cacheable regardless of MAIR. His MAIR sweep confirms it empirically: Write-Through Transient, Non-cacheable, and Write-Through Non-transient all fault, while Write-Back entries (transient and non-transient) succeed. Cortex-A76 documentation states a page is cacheable only when both Inner and Outer attributes are Write-Back — everything else is 'downgraded to Non-cacheable Normal memory.' Sik connects this to exclusive monitors: shareable locations reach a global monitor, plausibly implemented via cache coherency logic, so cacheability becomes a hard prerequisite. QEMU never modelled any of this — its SCTLR_C constant is referenced nowhere — so the emulator provided false confidence until real silicon disagreed.

Cortex-A76 documentation: 'a page is cacheable only if the Inner and Outer memory attributes are Write-Back. In all other cases, all pages are downgraded to Non-cacheable Normal memory' — and exclusive operations on that memory then take a Data Abort.

In the late 1960s, liquid crystals were a laboratory curiosity; RCA's dynamic scattering LCD was power-hungry and short-lived. Hilsum, with Peter Raynes, realized that a nematic liquid crystal could be twisted 90° between two polarizers. Applying a small electric field untwists the molecules, altering light transmission. This electro-optic effect required only microwatts, enabling battery-powered flat panels that eventually displaced bulky CRTs. The TN LCD became the foundational technology for watches, calculators, laptops, and smartphones. Its low-voltage, low-power operation was the breakthrough that made portable electronic displays practical.

Despite its UK origins, the TN LCD was commercialized almost entirely by Japanese firms—Sharp, Seiko, Casio—who seized the market in the 1970s and 80s. The Economist obituary likely notes this irony: a British invention that generated trillions in value abroad. Hilsum, born in 1925 and elected FRS in 1979, also contributed to semiconductor physics and LEDs, and later warned about the UK's short-termism. The first TN LCD patent was filed in 1970 by Hilsum and Raynes, though Hoffmann-La Roche and James Fergason also filed patents, leading to disputes.
