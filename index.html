#1
Run Qwen 3.8 Flash Next (125B) on consumer hardware (RTX 4090) at 100T/s
⬆ 891 pts 💬 399 HN comments

Strata (Niko1221/Strata) is an MIT-licensed inference engine that runs Qwen3.8-Flash-Next — a 125B-parameter mixture-of-experts model with roughly 6B active parameters per token — on single consumer GPUs with 12 GB+ VRAM. Its core idea is hierarchical expert offload: frequently used experts are pinned in VRAM, the complete expert set (24,576 specialists) stays resident in system RAM, and cold experts stream from SSD via a lookup table, with CPU and GPU working concurrently. Speculative decoding (multi-token prediction) checks guessed tokens in parallel for a 1.6–1.8x speedup, and chunked prefill ingests up to 8,192 tokens at over 1,000 tok/s. The package includes one-click Windows/Linux installers, an OpenAI/Anthropic-compatible localhost API, an MCP server, and optional vision, targeting RTX 20–50 series and RDNA3/4 cards.

Reported numbers are striking: 94 tok/s decode and 2,650 tok/s prefill on an RTX 5070 with Q2_0, and community measurements of 110–124 tok/s on RTX 4090/128 GB DDR5 rigs, 60 tok/s on an R9700 32 GB, and even 7 tok/s CPU-only on a Ryzen 8845HS with 96 GB RAM. The model ships in heavily compressed quants (Q2_0, IQ2_XS, IQ3_XXS, IQ3_S, IQ4_XS), and the Coder variant drops half the experts to hit 91% of full-model SWE-bench Verified. But the HN thread (891 points, 399 comments) exposes a sharp split: hands-on users call it “game changing” and “surprisingly well,” while skeptics note that 2–3-bit quantization carries measurable degradation — one vision benchmark showed a median coordinate error of 154.8 px on Strata versus 46.5 px on llama.cpp with identical weights — and that the headline omits the quant entirely.

Strata runs a 125B MoE model on 12 GB VRAM by caching hot experts on the GPU, keeping all experts in RAM, and streaming cold ones from SSD — hitting 94–124 tok/s, but only in 2–3-bit quants whose quality cost is still being contested.

Key Highlights
  • Strata implements MoE expert caching/offload: hot experts in VRAM, full expert set in RAM, cold experts streamed from SSD, with concurrent CPU+GPU execution.
  • Speculative decoding and up-to-8192-token chunked prefill yield 1.6–1.8x decode speedups and >1,000 tok/s prompt ingestion.
  • Reported throughput spans 94–124 tok/s on RTX 5070/4090, 60 tok/s on R9700, and 7 tok/s CPU-only on a Ryzen 8845HS with 96 GB RAM.
  • Quality is quant-dependent: the Coder variant removes half of experts for 91% SWE-bench Verified; vision degrades markedly versus llama.cpp with identical weights (154.8 px vs 46.5 px median error).
  • Skeptics highlight vibe-coded provenance (“it’s all Claude”), the absence of accuracy metrics alongside speed claims, and llama.cpp’s human-understanding contribution policy as reason the technique isn’t upstreamed.

#2
Turn off Apple Intelligence on macOS 27 and get its disk space back
⬆ 711 pts 💬 476 HN comments

RemoveMacAI is a Swift-based CLI that targets a specific and telling regression in macOS 27: Apple removed the single system-wide toggle for Apple Intelligence, while the on-device foundation models — reportedly around 25 GB including image generation, Genmoji, Spatial Photos, Photos Clean Up and Xcode code completion assets — remain resident and are re-downloaded on demand. The tool works through two mechanisms rather than filesystem surgery: a configuration profile that applies Apple's existing restriction keys and forces settings that have no restriction key, plus removal of models via Apple's asset service. Notably, it avoids the usual blunt-instrument approach — System Integrity Protection stays enabled, nothing under /System is touched, and each removed model's download URL is redirected to a closed local port so macOS cannot silently re-fetch it. It is fully reversible, ships with a build-provenance attestation verifiable via `gh attestation verify`, and makes no network requests. It builds on `pared`, an earlier tool by 4evy that first mapped the asset service and model sets — a dependency commenters noticed immediately.

The reaction — 711 points, 476 comments — is less about the tool than about what it signifies. Commenters repeatedly frame this as macOS acquiring Windows-like debloat culture, invoking O&O ShutUp10 and the era of stripping printer drivers and language packs: "Something horrible must have happened, if macOS users are curling shell scripts from the internet to make their desktops more like Linux." The most concrete grievance is storage economics. On a 256 GB entry-level Mac, 25 GB is a double-digit percentage of usable space, and Apple's upgrade pricing turns that into real money — one commenter bought a used Xeon PC with 16 GB RAM and 1 TB NVMe for less than the 256→512 GB upgrade. Secondary threads cover the genuinely useful Xcode simulator cleanup (`xcrun simctl delete unavailable`), iOS equivalents (toggling Siri language reportedly purges models), and repeated requests for an equivalent iOS tool. Others push back: the models are local, off-cloud, and adequate for basic tasks, and Apple's on-device posture is exactly the privacy tradeoff the company bet on. The AI-quality critique runs underneath it all — "For years people have been begging Apple to make Siri respond to basic requests... now the technology that allows them to fix Siri is handed to them on a silver platter, yet they still mess it up."

"Something horrible must have happened, if macOS users are curling shell scripts from the internet to make their desktops more like Linux." — a single line that captures why a 1.5k-star utility for reclaiming ~25 GB became the top macOS story of the day.

Key Highlights
  • macOS 27 removed the single Apple Intelligence toggle while leaving ~25 GB of foundation, image-generation, Genmoji, Spatial Photos, Photos Clean Up and Xcode models on disk; RemoveMacAI disables features via a configuration profile and removes models through Apple's own asset service.
  • Unlike typical debloat hacks, SIP stays enabled, no /System files are touched, changes are reversible, and each removed model's download is redirected to a closed local port to block re-fetching.
  • The release is SHA-256 verified, run from a temp directory with nothing installed, and carries a GitHub Actions build-provenance attestation verifiable with `gh attestation verify`.
  • The project builds on `pared` by 4evy — which first mapped the asset service and model sets — prompting commenters to note the lopsided 1.5k-star vs. upstream star counts.
  • Community reaction centers on Apple's storage pricing and upgrade margins, macOS's drift toward Windows-style cruft maintenance, and demand for an equivalent iOS/iPadOS tool.

A browser-native classic Visual Basic VB6 IDE
⬆ 349 pts 💬 117 HN comments

Wieslaw Soltes' browser-native VB6 IDE (349 points, 117 comments) is a WebAssembly-era reimplementation of the classic Visual Basic 6 development experience, complete with a drag-and-drop form designer, palette of controls, property grid, and event-handler codegen. Unlike a VM emulation of the original toolchain, it is a from-scratch web application that treats the VB6 IDE paradigm as a UI specification: you draw controls on a form, edit properties visually, double-click a widget to attach a handler, and then export the result as a self-contained HTML file rather than a PE binary. The author, who has published 500+ repositories including GPU-accelerated GUI frameworks and a C# browser IDE (SharpForge), says he is adding a WebGPU rendering engine and basic Win32 API shims such as BitBlt for legacy source compatibility.

The comment thread splits sharply between affection for the RAD loop and skepticism about both fidelity and provenance. franga2000's top thread argues that modern tooling is worse at 'discoverability' — palettes, property editors, visual builders, one-click codegen — than a 1998 IDE, while waldrews calls the property grid 'the greatest general purpose UI of all time.' Critiques are concrete: badsectoracula posts annotated screenshots of 'crooked' window buttons and missing bevels, jordanscales points out that `border-color: var(--vb-light) var(--vb-dark) var(--vb-dark) var(--vb-light)` produces uncanny 45-degree corners (and donates MIT-licensed CSS from 98.css), and fg137 notes it is 'a retro version of wix and produces html and JavaScript.' booty and ndiddy counter that VB6 was 'a hostile mess' beyond simple GUI apps and that resizable windows break drag-and-drop design.

franga2000: 'Makes it that much more absurd that tooling on modern platforms isn't anywhere near this level of discoverability.'

#4
In the wake of Tippett Studios’ closure, a digital archive appears online
⬆ 209 pts 💬 26 HN comments

Tippett Studios, the Berkeley effects house Phil Tippett founded and the shop behind the stop-motion work for Star Wars, RoboCop, Starship Troopers and Jurassic Park, closed on 28 August after a period of financial distress described in the coverage as a Chapter 11 bankruptcy rather than a planned retirement. Decades of props, puppets and production art went under the hammer in a two-day auction, raising the real prospect that irreplaceable craft history would disperse into private collections and never surface again. The story turns on an unlikely engineering intervention: an anonymous buyer, posting as 'TippettFan', noticed a binder of burned CD-ROMs and optical discs among the lots, acquired it for an undisclosed sum, and instead of shelving it, imaged the contents and published them to the Internet Archive. Ninety key, non-duplicated discs were preserved as ISO images.

That choice of format is the technically significant detail. Rather than curate a gallery of cherry-picked stills, the donor published raw ISO images, preserving directory structures, filenames and disc metadata intact, so the archive is a forensic artifact rather than an edited product. Filenames alone index behind-the-scenes material from RoboCop and Starship Troopers, slides from the original Star Wars work, CGI creature test clips, a 2008 Phil Tippett interview, and unexpectedly, Catwoman publicity stills. Commenters immediately began stress-testing the numbers: the Archive.org item lists roughly 49.6 GB, not the widely repeated 90 GB, and community members linked discmaster.textfiles.com's CD-ROM search, which surfaces the old low-poly Starship Troopers meshes directly. Commentary pushed toward mirroring torrents before rights holders intervene, framing the upload as a race between preservation and takedown.

The closure and the rescue have become a case study in how fragile digital production history is: the artifacts survived only because someone physically carried degraded optical media home, and they persist now only so long as volunteer bandwidth and Archive.org's goodwill hold out.

An anonymous auction attendee calling themselves 'TippettFan' bought a binder of discarded CD-ROMs and turned it into the Tippett archive on Internet Archive: 'some of this information wasn't available online and might not ever be in the future in such high resolution.'

  • An anonymous donor acquired a folder of CD-ROMs at that auction and published 90 key non-duplicated discs as raw ISO images to the Internet Archive.

#5
The technology to eradicate mosquito-borne disease exists
⬆ 124 pts 💬 102 HN comments

Oxitec's mosquito platform is a self-limiting RIDL system: a dominant lethal transgene expressing tTAV, a tetracycline-repressible transcriptional activator that is harmless at low concentration but lethal once it accumulates past a threshold. Lab colonies are reared in tetracycline-laced medium to keep the gene silent; in the field, with no tetracycline, female offspring die before adulthood while males survive to transmit the construct for a few generations before it dilutes out of the population. Sustained releases cut Aedes aegypti by roughly 80% in the Cayman Islands and about 95% in Juazeiro, Brazil. The real story, however, is institutional: a technology that emerged from Oxford fruit-fly work in 2000 spent fifteen years in jurisdictional limbo, bounced from USDA to FDA to EPA under the 1986 Coordinated Framework, whose categories were designed for a pre-genomic era.

The FDA's guidance that 'altered genomic DNA in an animal is a drug' shows how badly the veterinary-drug template fits an organism whose function is population suppression by inheritance and dispersal, and the agency ultimately carved itself out entirely. Oxitec waited five years per agency; the EPA's Experimental Use Permit finally arrived in May 2020, a decade after the first application, and commercial registration remains pending. Meanwhile Wolbachia—naturally infecting ~60% of insect species, inducing cytoplasmic incompatibility and blocking dengue replication—sidestepped the GM pathway: Singapore trials cut mosquito populations 85% and dengue ~70%, and MosquitoMate won nationwide biopesticide registration in April 2024. With US dengue cases up 360% in 2024, the bottleneck is throughput, not science; the author's PDUFA analogy (29-month drug reviews falling to 10/6 months, ~3.3 months saved per 100 reviewers) is the sharpest policy lever offered.

Oxitec first sought US approval in 2010; it took until May 2020 to receive an Experimental Use Permit—and full commercial registration is still pending, with the scientific advisory panel postponed indefinitely.

Key Highlights
  • Oxitec's tetracycline-repressible tTAV lethal gene kills only female Aedes aegypti offspring, is self-limiting rather than a self-propagating gene drive, and achieved 80-95% population suppression in field trials.
  • The 1986 Coordinated Framework forced a 15-year jurisdictional shuffle from USDA to FDA (which classified engineered DNA as a veterinary drug) to EPA, with each stage consuming years.
  • Wolbachia-based control exploits cytoplasmic incompatibility and virus-blocking without genetic modification, reaching nationwide US registration via MosquitoMate in April 2024 and 85%/70% reductions in Singapore.
  • Debug's June 2026 request to release 64 million Wolbachia Culex mosquitoes restartsthe Experimental Use Permit process from zero despite identical underlying biology.
  • The author proposes FDA-style user-fee and staffing reforms for the EPA, citing the PDUFA precedent where review times fell roughly 3.3 months per 100 added reviewers.
