#1
We should be more ambitious with the software we build
⬆ 503 pts 💬 438 HN comments

Dan Luu's post is less a productivity brag than a claim about a shift in the *cost model* of systems software. His exhibit is pgrust, Michael Malis's Rust rewrite of Postgres, which Luu defends against the reflexive 'vibeslop' dismissal by pointing to the real upstream Postgres bugs the work surfaced: a numerical bug breaking valid SP-GiST operations, a format-cache bug corrupting JSONPath results, a pgcrypto failure mode, multiple memory-safety bugs in the dictionary loader, and a money overflow. Luu's argument is that agents have changed what is *feasible*, not merely what is fast — the "works for me" tier of tooling (a bespoke regex engine, a ripgrep fork compiled through the regex engine's native codegen, a Rust interpreter yielding ~50x cold / 2x warm build speedups) cost him low single-digit hours despite no background in regexes, string matching, or compilers. His diagnosis: engineers still carry a pre-agentic cost model, so complaints like 'Rust compilation is slow' persist while the fix has become tractable.

The concrete evidence is worth noting: with GPT-5.1/5.2 he reached superhuman Azul play in ~5 hours and apparent SOTA in ~20; with GPT-6 Astra, eval design became the bottleneck — Astra passes all registered zstd tests >99% of the time in every condition, including the performance-degrading TDD arm, at roughly $1 per implementation, forcing him to escalate to the IMAP RFC and Pandoc. Luu explicitly rejects 'software is solved': each stronger model moves his frontier further from 'it just works,' because the gap between what a model can *implement* and what it can *verify* without handholding widens. Yossi Kreinin's quoted appendix supplies the counterweight — most people should be *less* ambitious, since agentic loops need pass/fail criteria and giant black boxes resist reasoning — and Luu's answer is self-checking harnesses: the consistency-checking scheme he built to reconcile Guards of Atlantis 2's contradictory rulebook, and his long-standing preference for fuzzing and property-based testing over unit tests.

Luu reports that work he estimates would once have taken "a year or more" to reach "works for me" quality now takes "a low single-digit number of hours" — yet he insists software is *not* solved: with each more capable model he simply picks harder problems, widening the implement/verify gap rather than closing it.

Key Highlights
  • Luu reframes the debate from speed to feasibility: agentic tooling makes previously absurd projects (direct-I/O toolchains, custom compilers, database rewrites) 'totally doable' at the 'works for me' tier.
  • pgrust is defended via upstream artifact quality — real Postgres bugs found in SP-GiST numerics, JSONPath format caching, pgcrypto, dictionary-loader memory safety — not via Luu's own benchmarking.
  • Eval evidence: GPT-6 Astra passes all registered zstd tests >99% across conditions including TDD for ~$1, pushing Luu to harder targets (IMAP RFC, Pandoc).
  • Yossi Kreinin's appendix argues most engineers should be *less* ambitious: agentic loops fail without clear pass/fail criteria, and unmaintainable overfit optimizations are the default failure mode.
  • Commenters split on whether reimplementing existing software faster constitutes ambition at all, versus addressing novel problems or fixing organizational coordination costs.

#3
America has lost 200k of jobs in media, film and the arts. How much is AI?
⬆ 396 pts 💬 409 HN comments

Joseph Politano's Apricitas analysis frames a genuinely hard identification problem: US "creative" industries have shed more than 200,000 jobs in four years—roughly 50,000 in the past year alone—a contraction comparable in intensity and duration only to the 2001 and 2008 recessions, yet occurring without any generalized downturn. The methodological move is to treat the split between digital and physical arts as a quasi-natural experiment: every digital subsector (streaming, live broadcast, graphic design, publishing, film) is contracting while in-person entertainment grows, the pattern one would expect if generative models were absorbing routine production work. Politano is careful about confounders—Hollywood consolidation, offshored production, algorithmic UGC competition, and streaming's destruction of per-title economics—and about the fact that firms deliberately obscure AI use to protect copyright claims. The historical spine is instructive: Napster and Metallica showed that winning legally while losing the underlying business model is the real risk, and music survived only because live performance and merch provided a compensating revenue channel—something film and television structurally lack.

The hardest data sits in movie & sound recording, which has lost over 100,000 jobs—nearly a third of the sector—with summer employment at its lowest since 2008 and approaching a 30-year trough; written publishing is next at roughly 70,000 lost. Supporting evidence includes survey data showing ~32% of arts/entertainment/recreation workers using generative AI versus a 62% cross-industry average—enough to say essentially every large production pipeline plausibly contains AI somewhere, even where invisible to audiences. Politano's worst case is the newspaper trajectory: payrolls down ~85% from a half-million peak as bundled content unbundled into search, sports, weather, and finance verticals. He refuses determinism, though, citing TikTok's BookTok driving a 70% surge in book sales as proof that new platforms can revive an art form unpredictably, and argues AI art's real weight is cultural rather than macroeconomic—pointing to Anthropic's abstention from image/video/music generation and OpenAI's reported retreat from Sora.

Key Highlights
  • Over 200k creative-industry jobs lost in four years, ~50k in the last year alone—matching 2001/2008 recession intensity without a recession
  • Digital arts subsectors are all contracting while in-person entertainment grows, the article's strongest circumstantial evidence for AI displacement
  • Film/TV lost ~100k jobs (~1/3 of sector), lowest since 2008; publishing down ~70k; losses concentrated but not contained
  • ~32% of arts/entertainment workers use generative AI vs 62% cross-industry—low, but enough to permeate most production workflows invisibly
  • Counter-evidence: BookTok drove a 70% surge in book sales, and AI art's macro impact is small relative to coding and science applications

#2
The Goose Programming Language
⬆ 453 pts 💬 276 HN comments

Wouter van Oortmerssen (Aardappel), the author of Lobster, Cube 2/Sauerbraten and Amiga E, has published Goose: a memory-safe systems language with no heap allocator, no garbage collector and no lifetime annotations. The design collapses all dynamic memory into N statically-determined data stacks — large address-space reservations with bump pointers — where at most one resizable value is live per stack and always on top, so growth is a pointer bump that never moves existing bytes. That single invariant yields the language's headline property: references into a growing array stay valid for the array's lifetime, eliminating the vector-reallocation hazard that forces C++ to reserve and safe Rust to fall back on u32 indices. Everything else follows structurally: nested records with variable-size fields are inline byte runs (a 29-byte order versus 160 bytes and an allocation in C++), variable-mode ADTs size each value to its variant, and self-relative 1/2/4-byte links make structures position-independent, so serialization degenerates into a write plus a verifying read that rejects hostile bytes.

The performance claims are aggressive and specific: over sixteen benchmarks, 3.3x idiomatic C++, 1.16x hand-optimized C++, 1.12x the best safe Rust, on 1.9x, 1.3x and 1.2x less peak memory, measured as whole-process wall clock including teardown. The compiler emits one C file, TinyCC is bundled for in-process JIT-style builds, and `extern fn` binds C symbols directly. The costs are equally explicit: recursive functions cannot hold growable data across calls, arrays of variable-size elements iterate but do not index, closures cannot escape, dispatch is ADT tags only, and the model needs a 64-bit address space — the author confirmed wasm and 32-bit targets are effectively out of reach today. The HN thread's sharpest friction was not the design but the documentation, which commenters widely assume was AI-written.

"Over sixteen benchmarks Goose runs at 3.3x the speed of idiomatic C++, 1.16x hand-optimized C++ and 1.12x the best safe Rust" — with no allocator, GC, reference counting or lifetime syntax, and one store to free a million-element structure.

Key Highlights
  • Single memory model: N compiler-computed data stacks with bump pointers; growth advances a pointer, so references survive reallocation and scope exit is one store
  • Inline, pointer-free layouts — a nested order record is 29 bytes and zero allocations where C++ needs 160 bytes and one allocation
  • Self-relative 1/2/4-byte typed links make structures position-independent, so the in-memory layout is already a validated file format
  • Variable-mode ADTs and `return err from load` provide variant-sized enums and statically checked multi-frame error returns without unwinders or Result types
  • Costs: no recursion with growable state, no indexing of variable-size arrays, no escaping closures, whole-program compilation, and a hard dependency on large 64-bit virtual address space

#4
Node2Shell evolves to HTTP2Shell, blast radius affects many HTTP libs and apps
⬆ 340 pts 💬 164 HN comments

CVE-2026-111497 is an out-of-bounds write in Google's Brotli decoder, affecting versions 1.0.3 through 1.2.0 and carrying a CVSS v4.0 score of 9.2. The root cause is a conflation of intent rather than a classic bounds bug: DecodeBlockTypeAndLength() returns a bare boolean in which BROTLI_FALSE means either 'this stream is malformed' or 'I need more input,' and the fast path discards the return value outright. A crafted stream that declares a single block type, exhausts its declared length, and forces an impossible Huffman-tree switch leaves the decoder continuing with stale block state and writing past its working buffer. Upstream commit 0ed4268 replaces the boolean with an error code and adds BROTLI_DECODER_ERROR_FORMAT_BLOCK_SWITCH (-17), halting decode on both safe and fast paths. Because the primitive is bidirectional -- a malicious server can take over a client that accepts Brotli responses, and a malicious client can take over a server that decodes Brotli bodies -- the flaw spread from Node.js's bundled copy, dubbed Node2Shell, into Python's Brotli and brotlicffi wheels and then into the HTTP stack at large.

The blast radius comes from bundling and auto-detection, not from protocol design. Brotli sees roughly 82.8 million PyPI downloads per month, and requests, httpx, aiohttp and urllib3 enable br decoding automatically whenever the package is merely installed -- no import required -- so exposure hides behind optional extras such as httpx[brotli]. Patches now exist across Node (22.23.4, 24.22.1, 26.12.1), Bun 1.4.4, libbrotli 1.2.1 (with 1.0.10 and 1.1.1 on older lines), Envoy, Istio and curl's dependency tree. The coordination gap is the real story: the fix landed in February without being recognized as a security issue, no advisory database yet maps the CVE to the PyPI packages, so pip-audit, Dependabot and Safety stay silent, and Ubuntu's libbrotli1 remains 'under review as medium,' holding up libcurl, git and downstream packages. Interim mitigation is blunt -- strip br from Accept-Encoding and reject Content-Encoding: br at the edge before any decoder runs.

A fix for the decoder bug landed in Brotli's source in February but was never classified as a security issue -- so a 9.2-severity, bidirectional RCE shipped unpatched for seven months in every project that bundles Brotli.

Key Highlights
  • Root cause is an ambiguous boolean return in DecodeBlockTypeAndLength(), fixed by commit 0ed4268 adding error -17 (FORMAT_BLOCK_SWITCH).
  • Exploitable in both directions: malicious servers compromise clients, malicious clients compromise servers that decode br request bodies.
  • Transitive exposure is invisible -- HTTP clients auto-enable Brotli when the package happens to be installed, no import needed.
  • Advisory tooling lags: pip-audit, Dependabot and Safety still do not flag Brotli 1.2.0 because the CVE is unmapped in OSV and the GitHub Advisory Database.
  • Downstream OS packaging stalls the fix: Ubuntu's libbrotli1 rated medium under review blocks libcurl, git and Git-for-Windows updates.

#6
FreeDNS
⬆ 309 pts 💬 83 HN comments

FreeDNS (afraid.org) is a long-running, engineer-operated authoritative DNS and dynamic-DNS service occupying a narrow but durable niche: it is not a registrar, but a place to host zones, attach shared subdomains, forward URLs, and update A/AAAA records from cron jobs or routers. The Hacker News submission is a reminder that a Web-1.0-looking portal still serves millions of users and production DNS traffic. Its model is simple but powerful: users delegate NS records or use shared domains, then manage records through a dated UI or tokenized HTTP update endpoints such as sync.afraid.org/u/<token> and v6.sync.afraid.org for IPv6. Backup DNS, reverse IPv6, and DD-WRT guides make it useful for self-hosters who run one authoritative server at home or in a colo and need a redundant secondary.

The homepage numbers explain the reaction: 7,162,445 members, 5,602 premium accounts, 13,835,443 records, 1,276,032 zones, 23,230 shared-registry domains, and roughly 2,376 DNS queries/sec. The service advertises uninterrupted operation, no ads or upsells, unlimited domains, and premium at $5/month. HN commenters add operational detail: FreeDNS is a cheap secondary DNS provider when you already run one server, a stable name for dynamic home IPs, and a way to avoid registrar lock-in. They also raise concrete frictions: shared domains missing from the Public Suffix List create cookie-isolation and Let's Encrypt rate-limit problems, the shared management queue can let strangers attach subdomains, and the single-maintainer model raises continuity questions despite a 20+ year track record. For a sophisticated audience, the lesson is that reliable DNS is less about UI polish than stable redundant infrastructure, simple automation interfaces, and clear trust boundaries.

FreeDNS handles roughly 2,376 DNS queries per second across 13.8M records and 1.27M zones, yet only 5,602 of its 7.16M members are premium subscribers.

Key Highlights
  • FreeDNS provides free static/dynamic DNS, backup DNS, web forwarding, IPv6 reverse DNS, and shared subdomain hosting, but is not a domain registrar.
  • Scale is large: 7.16M members, 13.8M records, 1.276M zones, and about 2,376 DNS queries/sec, sustained by 5,602 premium accounts.
  • Dynamic updates use simple tokenized HTTP endpoints for IPv4 and IPv6, making it popular for routers, cron jobs, and home servers.
  • Commenters cite missing Public Suffix List coverage, cookie isolation risk, Let's Encrypt rate limits, and a shared management queue as operational concerns.
  • Long-term users value its 20+ year track record and backup-DNS role, but question continuity because the service is closely tied to one maintainer.

Roku developer repositories moved from GitHub to GitLab
⬆ 291 pts 💬 205 HN comments

Roku has migrated its developer-facing repositories from GitHub to GitLab, announced in a blog post that — at least as served to non-JavaScript clients — contains no substantive rationale at all. That absence is itself the story's engine: with 291 points and 205 comments, the thread is largely an argument about GitLab versus GitHub as competing organisational forges rather than a discussion of Roku's specific engineering decisions. Commenters note that Roku already runs GitLab internally, making this plausibly a standardisation play rather than a reaction to GitHub's well-publicised availability incidents, though 'a dozen outages in the last couple months' was offered as reason enough. The most technically granular objections concern workflow primitives rather than uptime: GitLab's long-standing refusal to support group-level reviewer assignment (only the `/request_review @group` description hack, which pings each member individually) was cited as a concrete, chronic DX deficit for large organisations.

The broader argument in the thread is about what a forge is for. One camp frames GitLab as a project-management-shaped tool aligned with how 'regular' companies actually work — pipelines, epics, deploy/monitor panes — while GitHub is characterised as an 'open source dev social network' that got 'Microsofted'. The counter-camp points at GitLab's left-hand nav, a graveyard of 'Secure', 'Deploy', 'Operate', 'Monitor', 'Analyze' checkboxes accumulated during its feature-parity years, and argues the product would improve if most of it were deleted. Meanwhile GitHub defenders are scarce: one commenter who asked 'GitHub seems good?' drew a reply about an account suspended after Claude ran parallel `gh` commands, with no support response for days. Notably, GitLab's newer credit-card requirements and storage limits are cited as a moat against AI-generated repository spam.

The announcement itself states no reason — commenters are left inferring outages, standardisation, or Microsoft fatigue, and the loudest technical complaint is that GitLab still can't assign a group as a pull-request reviewer.

Key Highlights
  • Roku moved public developer repositories from GitHub to GitLab; the blog post gives no explicit motivation
  • Commenters split between outage-driven explanations, internal standardisation (Roku already uses GitLab), and general Microsoft distrust
  • Concrete GitLab critique: group reviewers are unsupported by design, forcing `/request_review @group` workarounds in MR descriptions
  • GitLab's accumulated 'Secure/Deploy/Operate/Monitor/Analyze' menu bloat is cited as the main argument against it
  • Several threads warn of forge fragmentation returning, and of AI agents flooding both platforms with generated commits

Zagitta's gpu-unlock is a Linux kernel module that restores the full FP/BF16 FMA-into-FP32 instruction issue rate on consumer Ampere parts (GA102/GA103/GA104/GA106) by exploiting the card's own booter firmware rather than patching the proprietary driver. The mechanism is a two-stage inheritance: the CMP unlocker's V67/PLM technique supplies the privilege-escalation primitive, and the UEFI-unlock community's insight supplies the timing, running before nvidia binds the device. Concretely, the module leverages a classic stack buffer overflow in the encrypted-but-constructible debug booter firmware to gain ROP execution, writes the speed selectors SS0=0x88888888 (full BF16 rate) and the 4-bit SS1=0x8 IMLA4, then issues a function-level reset before the unmodified driver probes the card. Because it hooks the initramfs rather than the driver, it survives every NVIDIA release without re-patching — the single biggest maintenance complaint about prior unlocks, especially on multi-GPU, mixed-generation machines.

The benchmark deltas are large where the workload is issue-rate-bound: on a 3090 Ti at 450 W, FP16 8192³ GEMM rises from 87.46 to 118.39 TFLOPS, BF16 from 87.50 to 157.41, and TF32 from 42.58 to 82.61. Per-card reports range from +25% (a 225 W-capped 3090) to +80% (3060), with 3080 Ti OEM GA102-250 failing to unlock and GA103 untested. The gating factor is power: gains scale with the wattage ceiling, so overclockers push cards to 450 W on stock coolers. Matrix-heavy prefill and diffusion benefit most; decode — memory-bandwidth bound — barely moves. Outputs were reported bit-identical, cards can drive the display, and vfio-pci passthrough works, with a suspend notifier re-applying the selectors after resume.

Flipping two bits in a speed-selector register takes a 3090 Ti from 87.50 to 157.41 BF16 TFLOPS — an 80% gain from hardware the customer already owns.

The Importance of Limitations for Creativity and Learning (2023)
Dave Gauer's card argues that constraints, not abundance, are the engine of both creativity and learning — a claim grounded in a small domestic observation. His kids, given access to Scratch at school, rarely stick with it; yet a buggy easter egg in an online document editor absorbed them for hours, precisely because it was easy to invoke, free-form in use, and trivially breakable. The mechanism he identifies is cognitive rather than moral: an expansive feature surface imposes a choice-resolution cost before any play begins, while a narrow, fragile toy invites immediate experimentation and rewards boundary-pushing. The same asymmetry appears in visual arts (a blank canvas induces paralysis; scrap paper induces doodling), in narrative (structure — even arbitrary structure — is what makes a story bearable, while dreams are boring because they have no rules to violate), and in production budgets, where a $300 prop allowance forces the unexpected solution that a large budget only reaches through deliberate directorial restraint.
