#2
Ember-1
⬆ 554 pts 💬 238 HN comments

Fireworks AI's Ember-1 is the company's first internally trained model, built as a specialized distillation of Moonshot's Kimi K3. The premise is narrow but consequential: reasoning models like K3 spend more than 90% of generated tokens on internal deliberation rather than the answer, and in multi-turn agentic workflows this compounds brutally, since every turn replays prior reasoning traces back into context, making cost grow roughly quadratically with turn count. Fireworks found that simply dialing down K3's reasoning-effort setting forfeits too much quality—so instead they trained the model to reason efficiently, running 50+ training experiments and 200+ evaluations on their own Serverless Training platform, spanning math, coding, instruction-following, search, tool use, and software engineering. Notably, no customer data was used.

The headline result: Ember-1 matches K3-max quality at ~40% fewer tokens, with reasoning-token reductions of 35–50%. On Terminal Bench 2.1 it scores 82.0% versus K3-max's 80.9%; on DeepSWE 1.1, 75.2% versus 66.4%, while reportedly dominating K3-low outright. Two live customer A/B tests showed ~35% fewer tokens per task at comparable quality (score 0.753 vs 0.751), and an internal rollout across Fireworks' own developers went unnoticed. Yet the HN reaction is sharply divided: per-token pricing appears roughly double K3 on OpenRouter, complicating the 'half the tokens' pitch, and critics frame the release as a proprietary derivative of open weights—a tension Fireworks' 'Research Preview' access model does little to resolve.

Ember-1 claims Kimi K3-max quality with 35–50% fewer reasoning tokens—a Pareto-frontier result—yet HN's loudest objection is that it costs roughly double per token, so savings only materialize in long, quadratic agentic contexts.

Key Highlights
  • Ember-1 matches Kimi K3-max quality with ~40% fewer tokens by training away redundant reasoning rather than lowering an inference-time effort dial.
  • Multi-turn agentic contexts replay prior reasoning each turn, making token cost grow roughly quadratically—the core economic motivation.
  • Fireworks ran 50+ training experiments and 200+ evaluations on its own Serverless Training platform, without customer data.
  • Reported gains: Terminal Bench 2.1 82.0% vs 80.9%, DeepSWE 1.1 75.2% vs 66.4%, and two customer A/B tests at ~35% fewer tokens.
  • Critics flag roughly 2x per-token pricing versus K3 and the propriety of shipping a closed derivative of open-weight models.

Self-Hosting on the Dark Web
⬆ 309 pts 💬 104 HN comments

David Álvarez Rosa's write-up walks through turning a personal Hugo site into a Tor v3 hidden service with almost no ceremony: a HiddenServiceDir plus HiddenServicePort 80 127.0.0.1:8080 in /etc/tor/torrc, a restart of tor@default, and the 56-character .onion address that Tor derives directly from an Ed25519 public key stored in hostname. The nginx server block deliberately strips TLS, HTTP/2, and QUIC because Tor carries its own end-to-end encryption over plain TCP. The genuinely interesting engineering detail is the build step: because a static site bakes absolute URLs (RSS, canonical, og:url, microformats) into its output, the deploy pipeline rebuilds the same content a second time with --baseURL pointed at the onion hostname, then rsyncs each variant to its own web root so links never leak visitors back to clearnet.

The piece landed at 309 points and 104 comments largely because it is a working example of a pattern HN has long admired: an outgoing connection to the Tor network from behind NAT or CGNAT, requiring no port forwarding, no public IP, no certificate authority, and no DNS. Commenters immediately extended it — the Onion-Location header (which the author admitted he'd missed and was 'adding it now'), the HiddenServiceExportCircuitID knob Cloudflare documented for per-circuit telemetry, binding to 127.13.37.1 or a Unix socket to avoid accidental port collisions, and backing up the private key 'loss of which would cause you to have to change your onion address.' The recurring friction is performance and reachability: onion addresses 'impossible to share verbally,' latency that makes 'anything chatty feel broken,' and no free CA issuance for .onion, which is why HTTP/2 multiplexing is effectively unavailable.

"Everything on a .onion is ephemeral" — superkuh, reflecting on a decade of hosting a hidden service and the unilateral 2021 torv2 deprecation that wiped the address space.

Key Highlights
  • Hidden services need only a HiddenServiceDir/HiddenServicePort pair in torrc; the onion address is derived from a public key, so no DNS, CA, or exposed IP is involved.
  • Static sites must be built twice — once per base URL — because absolute links (RSS, canonical, og:url) would otherwise pull Tor visitors back to the clearnet domain.
  • The Onion-Location response header lets Tor Browser advertise the onion variant to clearnet visitors; the author had missed it and added it after a comment.
  • Tor-specific performance work is real: inline CSS, base64 assets, server-side rendering, no JS dependency, plus per-circuit telemetry via HiddenServiceExportCircuitID since all logs otherwise show the same IP.
  • Operational brittleness matters: v2 onions were unilaterally deprecated in 2021, addresses are unspeakable, first-connection reachability is slow, and losing the private key means losing the address.

Don't couple your Go code to GitHub
⬆ 306 pts 💬 164 HN comments

Iain Cambridge's post argues that Go's module system needlessly couples package identity to a specific git host. Because import paths like github.com/thetrueares/boneclone serve as both namespace and fetch instruction, moving a repository from GitHub to GitLab or Azure DevOps would require rewriting imports across all dependent code, or running multiple forges indefinitely. Cambridge cites a company paying for GitHub, GitLab, and Azure DevOps simultaneously because migration overhead felt prohibitive. His fix is a vanity import domain: go.iain.rocks/boneclone serves an HTML meta go-import tag that points Go tooling at the real VCS, while nginx redirects human visitors. The pattern, also used by go.uber.org and go.mongodb.org, decouples the stable import path from mutable hosting. The config checks for ?go-get=1, serving the meta tag only to Go tooling and issuing a 301 redirect for browsers.

HN commenters largely accepted the technical mechanism but split on whether it improves resilience. Proponents frame namespace control as fixability: 'It's not a matter of whether or not something can fail; it's a matter of whether or not you can fix it when it fails,' writes cjs_ac, while throwawayffffas adds that if GitHub fails 'all you can do is stare at their status page.' Skeptics counter that domains expire, get sold, or outlive maintainers, making custom namespaces another failure point. Others note go.mod replace can mask migration for direct dependencies, but transitive imports and old releases break, as MadVikingGod's A to B to C example shows. The thread exposes Go's deeper tension: location-as-identity versus a traditional package registry, with some arguing vanity domains plus proxy.golang.org merely reinvent an index. At 306 points and 164 comments, the debate clearly resonates.

The debate crystallized around cjs_ac's distinction: 'It's not a matter of whether or not something can fail; it's a matter of whether or not you can fix it when it fails.'

Key Highlights
  • Go import paths encode fetch location, coupling module identity to GitHub or GitLab.
  • Vanity domains with go-import meta tags redirect Go tooling to any VCS, e.g. go.iain.rocks/boneclone points to github.com/thetrueares/boneclone.
  • Counterargument: custom domains add operational burden and can expire, be sold, or be abandoned, making them less reliable than GitHub for OSS.
  • go.mod replace directives mitigate direct dependency migration but transitive deps and old releases remain problematic.
  • The debate reflects Go's design tension between location-as-identity and abstract or content-addressed package naming.

Alan Kay's answer to “Did the ENIAC have a BIOS”?
⬆ 171 pts 💬 56 HN comments

Alan Kay's Quora answer to 'Did the ENIAC have a BIOS?' sparked a rich Hacker News discussion on the prehistory of bootstrapping. Kay's core insight, as reflected in comments, is that ENIAC predated the BIOS concept—its 'BIOS' was a team of human operators physically rewiring patch cables. The conversation quickly expanded to early boot mechanisms: EDSAC's 1949 'initial orders' module, a bank of rotary switches acting as a boot ROM, loaded a paper tape loader designed by David Wheeler. This electro-mechanical ROM foreshadowed firmware. The thread also debated terminology: the term BIOS was coined in 1975 with CP/M, making its application to ENIAC anachronistic, yet the underlying need to initialize hardware and load programs transcends naming. The discussion highlights how foundational abstractions like bootstrapping emerged from physical labor and hardware constraints.

Commenters added technical nuances: PDP-11s used core memory that survived power cycling, allowing hand-toggled bootstrap code to persist, while DEC's BM792 boot ROM card required manually removing diodes to set boot code. NelsonMinar demonstrated modern relevance by having Claude Opus disassemble the CDC 6600 dead start panel, a 62-year-old computer, in two minutes—hinting at AI's potential for historical computing archaeology. Others corrected Kay on ENIAC's stored-program history: it was rebuilt in 1948 to operate as a von Neumann machine until 1955, as detailed in 'ENIAC in Action.' The thread also reflected on Quora as a platform: Adamddev1 praised Kay's presence as a 'gift,' while others lamented that LLMs are displacing expert Q&A, and that Quora's quality is erratic. The discussion underscores both the ingenuity of early computing and evolving modes of knowledge transmission.

Kay nails it: the ENIAC 'BIOS' was literally a team of humans with patch cables. Makes you appreciate modern bootloaders.

Key Highlights
  • ENIAC lacked a BIOS; programming required physical reconfiguration, with human operators acting as the bootstrap mechanism.
  • EDSAC (1949) implemented an 'initial orders' module—a boot ROM using rotary switches to load a paper tape loader, an early form of firmware.
  • The term BIOS was not coined until 1975 (CP/M), so applying it to ENIAC is anachronistic, though bootstrap concepts existed earlier.
  • ENIAC was later modified to be a stored-program computer (1948–1955), sparking debate about von Neumann's role in the stored-program concept.
  • The discussion highlighted Quora as a platform for expert answers from Alan Kay, with mixed feelings about its quality and the rise of LLMs replacing human expert interaction.

Malleable software: Restoring user agency in a world of locked-down apps (2025)
⬆ 138 pts 💬 68 HN comments

Ink & Switch's essay frames malleable software as a return to personal computing's original promise: tools users can reshape like clay rather than sealed appliances. The authors—Geoffrey Litt, Josh Horowitz, Peter van Hardenberg, and Todd Matthews—argue that modern software imposes centralized decisions on users, forcing workflow adaptation to rigid interfaces. They ground the vision in historical HCI research, notably MacLean et al.'s 1990 'gentle slope' from user to creator, where each increase in customization power requires only incremental skill investment. They critique settings, plugins, modding, and open source as partial solutions: each hits a developer-defined ceiling, a maintenance cliff, or a chasm between installing extensions and authoring them. Design patterns include tools rather than monolithic apps, shared data formats, composable UIs, and local communal creation, with prototypes like dynamic documents and Ambsheets.

The essay's sharpest analogy targets AI coding: bringing AI tools into today's app-store ecosystem is 'like bringing a talented sous chef to a food court.' Generation alone does not solve composability, editing installed tools, or precise small tweaks; without open substrates, users still rent sealed boxes. The authors cite Atul Gawande on electronic medical records driving burnout—'Spending the extra time didn't anger her. The pointlessness of it did.'—and a neurosurgeon's custom interface as proof local adaptation works. HN commenters largely agree with the diagnosis but split on feasibility. Some call customization a niche concern; others demand GPLv3, third-party client rights, file-based data, spreadsheet-like malleability, or Emacs/Winamp nostalgia. The thread's recurring tension: who actually wants to become a creator?

Ink & Switch's key insight is that AI code generation alone cannot fix locked-down software: 'bringing AI coding tools into today’s software ecosystem is like bringing a talented sous chef to a food court.'

Key Highlights
  • Malleable software is defined as an ecosystem enabling minimal-friction adaptation from settings to code, guided by MacLean et al.'s 1990 'gentle slope' from user to creator.
  • Settings, plugins, modding, open source, and AI coding are all partial remedies because they hit developer-defined limits, maintenance cliffs, or high skill barriers.
  • The essay proposes tools over apps, shared data protocols, composable UIs, and communal creation, backed by Ink & Switch prototypes like dynamic documents and Ambsheets.
  • AI's role is constrained: generation without open, composable substrates is like a sous chef in a food court—it cannot fix installed, closed tools.
  • HN reaction shows broad agreement on diagnosis but pessimism about ordinary users, plus advocacy for GPL, third-party clients, file formats, spreadsheets, and nostalgia for extensible computing.

Footguns with Postgres "at time zone 'UTC'"
⬆ 120 pts 💬 63 HN comments

The article takes aim at one of Postgres's most quietly hazardous idioms: `AT TIME ZONE 'UTC'`. The core technical point is that `AT TIME ZONE` is not an annotation or a formatter — it is a cast whose direction inverts depending on the input type. Applied to a `timestamp` (without time zone) it means "interpret these wall-clock digits as belonging to zone X and produce a `timestamptz`"; applied to a `timestamptz` it means "render this instant as wall-clock digits in zone X and produce a plain `timestamp`." The session `TimeZone` setting acts as a hidden third argument in related comparisons, so identical-looking SQL behaves differently on a UTC-configured production server than on a developer's laptop set to `America/Los_Angeles`. The author ties this to a broader critique: month arithmetic (`+ INTERVAL '1 month'`) is timezone-dependent, DST transitions make wall-clock time non-monotonic, and the SQL standard's `timestamp` type does not denote a unique instant at all.

The comment thread (120 points, 63 comments) quickly outgrew the article. `tibbar` reports finding a real correctness bug this summer: under a DST spring-forward gap, the `datetime_ops` btree family can satisfy `B > A` and `B < C` but `C = A`, causing index-backed queries to silently return wrong results — acknowledged on the mailing list as a bug with no clean fix. `heurekamala` demonstrates that the article's claim that mixed comparisons "always result in false" is itself wrong, reproducing a `SET TIME ZONE 'America/Los_Angeles'` case where the answer depends on session state. `paulddraper` offers the blunt TLDR: always use `timestamptz`, and rename the mental models to "instant" versus "datetime." `jokull` argues the author overfits the Postgres wiki's "don't use timestamp" advice, which is scoped specifically to storing UTC times.

A commenter reports a genuine Postgres btree correctness bug in DST spring-forward gaps: there exist values where B > A and B < C but C = A, so an indexed query can return the wrong answer — and the mailing list concluded it was a bug with no good way to fix it without a scary behavioral backpatch.

Key Highlights
  • `AT TIME ZONE` is a bidirectional cast, not a formatter: on `timestamp` it produces a `timestamptz`, on `timestamptz` it produces a `timestamp`, with the session TimeZone as a hidden third argument.
  • Mixed `timestamp` vs `timestamptz` comparisons are not 'always false' as the article claims — the result depends on session TimeZone, so dev laptops and UTC production servers diverge.
  • Under a DST spring-forward gap, the datetime_ops btree comparison family can violate transitivity, letting index scans return incorrect results.
  • Calendar arithmetic (`+ INTERVAL '1 month'`, `+ INTERVAL '1 day'`) is timezone-dependent and undefined for month-ends, so it is a domain/business-rule decision, not a database primitive.
  • The practical consensus TLDR: default to `timestamptz` (an instant), reserve `timestamp` for genuinely local/plain datetimes, and rename them mentally to 'instant' and 'datetime'.

Lunar Terminator Paradox
Dima Kogan's note 'Lunar Terminator Paradox' is an attempt to resolve, by simulation, a geometry problem that resists verbal explanation: why the illuminated face of the Moon can appear to point upward just after sunset, when the Sun is already below the horizon. The answer is scale and parallax, not any failure of illumination. The Sun's rays arrive at the Earth-Moon system essentially parallel (the Sun is roughly 400x farther away than the Moon), so a Moon well above the horizon is viewed from 'below', exposing a thin crescent of its unlit far side along the lower limb. A full or gibbous Moon at elevation therefore reads as 'pointing up' even though nothing about the illumination has changed. Kogan's plots sweep lunar elevation from horizon to zenith and swing solar azimuth from new to full moon to demonstrate the continuum, and the most pathological case is the full Moon at sunset: 100% lit on the horizon, dark-sliced at the bottom once elevated.
