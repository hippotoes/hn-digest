#2
Sebastian Raschka's from-scratch video course on building reasoning models
⬆ 771 pts 💬 104 HN comments

Sebastian Raschka has released an eight-part, roughly eleven-hour video companion to his Manning book *Build a Reasoning Model (From Scratch)*, extending the pedagogical arc he began with *Build a Large Language Model (From Scratch)*. The course deliberately sidesteps the prohibitive cost of pre-training by starting from a downloaded Qwen3 0.6B checkpoint, then layering on the post-training machinery that actually distinguishes a reasoning model: a math-answer verifier (answer extraction, normalization, symbolic equivalence checking) that serves double duty as both an evaluation harness on MATH-500 and the reward signal for RLVR; inference-time scaling via temperature scaling, multinomial sampling, top-p filtering, self-consistency majority voting, and sequence log-probability scorers used for tie-breaking and self-refinement loops; a simplified GRPO trainer with clipped policy ratios, KL penalties, entropy/advantage telemetry and format rewards; and finally supervised distillation from DeepSeek-R1-generated traces. The framing is architectural: the model is the engine, the agent harness is the machinery around it, and RLVR plus optional inference scaling is what upgrades a conventional LLM into a reasoning one.

The engineering choices are what make the series legible rather than merely illustrative. KV caching combined with `torch.compile`, explicit treatment of tensor shapes and logits, numerically stable log-probability computation, and honest discussion of a *simplified* KL formulation and its limitations all signal a course aimed at practitioners who want to see the seams. Raschka is candid about scope: early text-generation and evaluation chapters run on CPU, but the GRPO and distillation training loops effectively want a GPU. The timing is pointed — he notes that nearly all current-generation LLMs are reasoning models or expose a reasoning-effort mode — so this is less a niche curiosity than a map of the default architecture. The commercial scaffolding (Manning/Amazon book, a 90-minute LinkedIn Learning preview) is real, but the code is free and the repo ships hardware and environment guidance, including CUDA/Apple Silicon and remote-GPU-over-VS-Code workflows.

Eleven hours of video and eight chapters to go from a frozen Qwen3 0.6B checkpoint to a GRPO-trained, MATH-500-evaluated reasoning model — with the same verifier used both as a grader and as the RLVR reward signal.

Key Highlights
  • Eight videos (~11 hours) accompany the book, starting from a pre-trained Qwen3 0.6B rather than pre-training, to isolate evaluation, inference scaling, RL, and distillation
  • A math-answer verifier built in chapter 3 doubles as the MATH-500 grader and the RLVR reward function in chapter 6
  • Inference-time scaling is decomposed into sampling diversity (temperature, top-p, multinomial) plus aggregation (self-consistency majority vote, log-prob scoring, self-refinement)
  • GRPO is implemented in simplified form, then revisited with clipped policy ratios, KL terms, entropy/advantage tracking, and format rewards to expose reward hacking
  • Distillation from DeepSeek R1 traces is presented as an alternative path to reasoning capability that avoids RL entirely

#1
Three new discoveries in mathematics, computer science, and physics
⬆ 1014 pts 💬 560 HN comments

OpenAI has published three claimed research results produced by its internal models, each formalised in Lean within days of the natural-language output. In harmonic analysis, its models claim a proof of Sogge's 1991 local smoothing conjecture in every dimension—via a novel auxiliary PDE, and by a route commenters say differs from the Guth–Wang–Zhang 2D or Wang–Zahl 3D arguments—which would also settle the Kakeya and Stein restriction conjectures in all dimensions. In complexity theory, it claims a refutation of the Exponential Time Hypothesis (the Impagliazzo–Paturi–Zane framework underpinning hundreds of conditional hardness results), backed by a deterministic algorithm exactly counting k-CNF satisfying assignments in poly_k(n,m) space and 2^{n/(log n)^{1/2+o(1)}} time. In physics, it claims a constructive existence proof for four-dimensional interacting gauge theories with fermions, including QCD, a Standard-Model completion, and pure Yang–Mills existence—short of the Clay mass gap.

The more novel content may be the disclosure process. OpenAI ran three deliberately different release strategies chosen by external shepherds: raw output for the Kakeya proof; a public proof plus a convened group (Impagliazzo, Paturi, Williams) for ETH, with funded conference and raw data; and a materials embargo deferred to a Gross-led group (Douglas, Jaffe, Klebanov, Nekrasov) for Yang–Mills. Stated principles include no NDAs, no academic credit sought, and unpaid voluntary participation, alongside a $5M Simons Foundation contribution. HN's 560 comments split between disbelief at the ETH result—"superpolynomial but subexponential... Fucking wild"—and unease at a vendor adjudicating which experts see results first, given no peer review.

The ETH refutation rests on an algorithm running in roughly 2^{n/(log n)^0.5} time for exact model counting of k-CNF—superpolynomial but strictly subexponential, which is precisely what ETH forbids.

Key Highlights
  • Local smoothing proved in all dimensions via a novel auxiliary PDE, implying Kakeya and Stein restriction in full generality
  • ETH refuted by an explicit deterministic exact-counting algorithm, not a non-constructive argument
  • Constructive QFT existence for 4D QCD and a GUT-style completion; pure Yang-Mills existence without the mass gap
  • Three divergent disclosure strategies deliberately trialled, with named external shepherds and Lean artifacts
  • Criticism centres on lack of peer review, information asymmetry, and vendor-led credit allocation

#11
Cross-request KV cache exposure in the GLM-5.3 Workers AI model (155 responses)
⬆ 155 pts 💬 55 HN comments

Cloudflare has published a coordinated-disclosure post-mortem on a tenant-isolation failure in its Workers AI serving of @cf/zai-org/glm-5.3, reported September 8 by Knostic's Gadi Evron via HackerOne. The root cause was a compound failure, not a model defect: Cloudflare configured a 1,048,576-token advertised context window while the prefill KV cache pool on its GPUs held only 681,280 tokens, and SGLang's rejection path in disaggregated prefill/decode mode mishandled over-length prompts. Instead of aborting, `Req.set_finish_with_abort` recorded the error in `to_finish` and swapped in a one-token placeholder; because admission queues on both prefill and decode gatekept on `finished()` rather than rejection state, the placeholder passed admission, one page of KV was transferred over RDMA via Mooncake, and decode attended across eight reserved pages of which seven still held other tenants' prompts and partial generations — returning HTTP 200 with leaked context.

The scale is small but the mechanism is general: 155 affected responses between August 27 and September 9, all from prompts exceeding 681,280 tokens, with Cloudflare finding no evidence of deliberate exploitation after excluding researcher and internal test traffic. Remediation was dual-layered — expanding the prefill pool to roughly 2.04M tokens (matching GLM 5.2's config) so valid context always fits, and patching the engine to retire rejected requests at the disaggregation admission queue before any bootstrap, allocation, or transfer, coupled with a decode-side check that transferred token count matches reserved pages (upstream #38704, #38935, #36988; shipped in SGLang v0.5.21). Cloudflare concedes its pre-launch validation only checked HTTP status codes and latency at long contexts, never output coherence — a testing gap it now closes by reading pool size directly from the running engine.

Cloudflare knowingly shipped a 1,048,576-token context window backed by a 681,280-token KV pool — a 36% deficit that, combined with an SGLang admission bug, turned every over-length request into a cross-tenant memory read.

Key Highlights
  • KV cache pages are recycled without zeroing between requests, making the inference engine solely responsible for page ownership and lifetime tracking across local and remote (prefill) references.
  • SGLang's rejection path stored errors in `to_finish` rather than `finished_reason`, so `finished()` returned False and rejected requests passed disaggregation admission on both prefill and decode nodes.
  • Only 155 responses were affected, all from prompts over 681,280 tokens, with no evidence of deliberate exploitation found.
  • Fix was layered: pool enlarged to ~2.04M tokens (2× the context window) plus an engine patch retiring rejected requests before bootstrap/allocation/transfer.
  • Pre-launch testing validated only HTTP status codes and throughput at long context, never output coherence — the gap that let the bug ship.

CSS-Tricks has a new owner
⬆ 513 pts 💬 108 HN comments

CSS-Tricks, the front-end reference site Chris Coyier founded in 2007, is independent again: Geoff Graham — its editor and lead writer for roughly twelve years — has acquired the property from DigitalOcean, which bought it in 2022 and, by near-universal account in the thread, promptly deprioritized it. That neglect was visible enough to generate its own earlier HN thread, "CSS-Tricks in Limbo" (273 points), and former DO staffer `vanadium` confirms frustrations where the site "frustratingly [was] not a priority." The technical substance here is modest — a WordPress-backed corpus of evergreen CSS documentation with a strong backlink profile and brand equity — which is precisely why the economics are interesting: marginal hosting cost is near zero, so the asset was worth more to an individual with editorial credibility than to a cloud vendor seeking funnel attribution. The purchase converts an under-maintained reference corpus back into a stewardship problem rather than an operational one.

Graham's plan is a deliberately old-school monetization stack: real ad packages (self-hosted, display plus sponsored posts), a revived newsletter returning November 10, a paid membership tier with a Discord community, a merch shop, and a beginner-focused HTML/CSS course in development — with Graham positioning himself as "publisher" rather than sole author, cutting cadence to one or two articles weekly and reopening the guest-writing form while explicitly banning "slop submissions." It is a direct bet that human-authored, human-curated teaching survives the LLM era; the site even maintains an AI Statement. HN's 108 comments split accordingly: genuine affection for a formative resource against open doubt that ad-and-course revenue can sustain anything in 2026, when the same question is answered free by a model trained partly on CSS-Tricks itself.

"Buying a site about CSS in an era when LLMs can write any CSS for you in milliseconds takes either serious courage or a healthy dose of madness. I salute you either way."

#5
Oldest GUI application that still runs on Windows 11
⬆ 481 pts 💬 283 HN comments

A deceptively simple question — what is the oldest unmodified GUI binary that still launches on Windows 11? — turns into an exercise in Windows' ABI archaeology. The author first lands on FREECELL.EXE from Win32s, compiled 22-Feb-93 and fully playable on current Windows 11, a 33-year-old binary that survives because Win32's user32/kernel32 surface has remained call-compatible across three decades. Digging into NT 3.1 pre-releases on the Internet Archive, the trail goes cold for two concrete reasons: the 1991 builds emit a draft PE format that modern loaders reject, and builds through mid-1992 used cdecl rather than stdcall for Win32 API calls — so a `call [ShowWindow]` gets its arguments popped twice (once by the callee's `ret 4`, once by the caller's `add esp, 4`), corrupting the stack immediately. Build 347's CDPLAYER.EXE (16-Nov-1992) sidesteps a broken bundled CRT by linking against the system's crtdll.dll, which Windows 11 still ships in SysWOW64 — it runs, and with a stub cdemu.dll supplying synthetic audio, it plays.

The two structural constraints are worth naming: 64-bit Windows dropped NTVDM, so no Win16 binary is native, and the stdcall transition is the real dividing line between 'runs' and 'faults instantly'. The author explicitly rejects otvdm-based demonstrations — a Windows 1.01 REVERSI.EXE from 15-Nov-1985 is shown 'fully playable' but flagged as cheating, since the stated goal is double-clickable on vanilla Windows. Commenters push the boundary further: Win32s' FreeCell descends from a Win16 Entertainment Pack 2 build from 1991, and one commenter notes the ODBC administrator shipped in Win11 still uses Win3.0/NT3.5-era dialogs with 16-color icons. The thread's real payload is the contrast with the rest of the ecosystem — 'my 9-year old ipad cannot display half the web anymore' — and the practical archaeology of users porting registry-stored FreeCell high scores across machines for a deceased parent, or reverting to the Windows 7 calculator on every fresh install.

A CDPLAYER.EXE linked on 16-Nov-1992 runs unmodified on Windows 11 — nearly 34 years later — only because it dodges its build's broken bundled CRT and calls the system's crtdll.dll, which Windows 11 still ships in SysWOW64.

Key Highlights
  • Win32s FreeCell (22-Feb-93) runs unmodified on Windows 11 — 33 years and 8 months of binary compatibility.
  • The hard cutoff is not age but calling convention: pre-mid-1992 NT builds use cdecl and double-pop arguments against today's stdcall APIs, crashing on first GUI call.
  • 1991-era NT pre-releases emit a draft PE format that the modern loader simply refuses.
  • CDPLAYER.EXE (Build 347, 16-Nov-1992) survives by linking the system crtdll.dll instead of a broken bundled runtime, and can be coaxed into playback via a cdemu.dll stub.
  • Windows 1.01 REVERSI.EXE (15-Nov-1985) works under otvdm, but the author classes third-party emulation as cheating since 64-bit Windows has no NTVDM.

#6
Solar power is becoming bigger than nuclear
⬆ 440 pts 💬 622 HN comments

Solar's crossing of nuclear is less a story about a technology than about a manufacturing learning curve colliding with a forecasting establishment that modeled the wrong variable. A decade ago solar supplied roughly 1% of global electricity; in 2025 it edged past nuclear on Ember's numbers, and BBC's analysis of the trailing twelve months to September 2026 puts it 13% ahead. The mechanism is unglamorous: crystalline silicon modules are now a commodity produced at Chinese scale with chronic overcapacity, module prices collapsed again in 2023–24, and the panel itself is a minority of total system cost. Nuclear, meanwhile, has been generation-flat since 2000 while solar rose roughly 2,700-fold. The IEA and peers assumed linear growth and linear cost decline, and got both directions wrong — the recurring lesson being that exponential learning-rate technologies are systematically under-projected by institutional outlooks.

The consequence is that the binding constraints have migrated from the panel to the system. Solar met three-quarters of 2025's demand growth globally; Pakistan went from under 2% to roughly 24% solar electricity in about six years on private rooftop purchases; Australia has over four million rooftop households; India's new solar covered 60% of demand growth over two years; Africa installs on the order of 100,000 panels daily. Pairing with storage is now routine — California's solar-plus-battery fleet covered more than a quarter of the 19:00–21:00 evening peak in H1 2026 — and solar-plus-storage undercuts new fossil plants in sunny regions. Remaining barriers are grid interconnection, seasonal storage, fault-current behavior of inverter-dominated grids, and electrification pace; the IEA projects solar generation doubling by 2030 and overtaking hydro, with the caveat that China's revised support regime may slow the global installation rate for the first time.

A single solar panel now costs well under $100 and produces for 30–40 years with no fuel input — Ember's Dave Jones calls it "offensively cheap," while nuclear generation has stayed flat since 2000 against a ~2,700-fold rise in solar.

Key Highlights
  • Solar went from ~1% to ~10% of global electricity in a decade and now out-generates the entire world nuclear fleet by ~13% over the trailing year.
  • Module prices are now so low that the panel is a minority of system cost, shifting bottlenecks to labor, interconnection, land, and inverters.
  • Distributed, often unregistered rooftop adoption — Pakistan, Lebanon, Nigeria, the Philippines — is a large and poorly measured share of the boom.
  • Battery pairing has made solar dispatchable into evening peaks (California: >25% of 19:00–21:00 demand in H1 2026), but seasonal storage remains unsolved.
  • Grid reliability concerns — synthetic inertia, fault current ride-through, voltage rise — are now the gating technical problems, not module efficiency.

Key Highlights
  • A minimal `@media print` block plus `.onlyPrint`/`.noPrint` classes suffices to hide navigation, restore black link text for grayscale, and pin typography to physical points.

Type Server Protocol
⬆ 254 pts 💬 172 HN comments

Microsoft's Pylance team has published the Type Server Protocol (TSP), a JSON-RPC protocol that separates a Python type-analysis engine from the editor frontend that consumes it. TSP reuses LSP data shapes where convenient — zero-based lines, UTF-16 character offsets, Position/Range, string URIs — but introduces its own `typeServer/` method namespace and a snapshot model: a non-negative integer identifying a point-in-time analysis state, against which all returned type handles, declarations, and symbols are validated. Type results are JSON-serializable tagged objects with a numeric `kind` discriminator spanning ten variants — built-ins (`unknown`, `any`, `never`), functions, classes, unions, modules, type variables, overload sets, synthesized types, and `TypeReference` nodes used for deduplication and cycle-breaking. Three query verbs distinguish flow-narrowed computed types from source-written declared types and context-expected types, mirroring the type-at-point distinctions editors already surface loosely as hover strings and markdown.

The protocol has churned fast: 0.1.0 through 0.4.1 in a short window, with 0.x minor bumps explicitly allowed to break, and 0.4.1 adding an optional multi-connection mode where extra read-only IPC channels serve high-volume queries while the main connection retains exclusive ownership of LSP lifecycle, document sync, and state mutation — negotiated via `capabilities.experimental.typeServerMultiConnection`. Stale-snapshot requests are expected to fail with `-32802 ServerCancelled` so clients can retry cheaply, and type IDs are explicitly response-local rather than stable identities. The strategic payload is decoupling: Pylance's frontend can now front any conforming backend, and Microsoft has announced Pyrefly + Pylance integration in VS Code, while Astral's ty is expected to support TSP and basedpyright's maintainer has publicly criticized it.

"Type IDs are not a stable global identity for a snapshot and should not be compared across separate responses" — a protocol detail that reveals TSP is built for round-trip serialization of type graphs, not for caching or cross-session reasoning.

Key Highlights
  • TSP is a JSON-RPC protocol with nine messages that lets an editor frontend consume type-at-point results from an arbitrary Python type engine.
  • Snapshot semantics gate every request: handles and type objects are valid only for the snapshot that produced them, with stale requests failing via -32802 for cheap retry.
  • Type payloads are discriminated unions (kinds 0-9) with response-local IDs and TypeReference nodes to avoid duplicating recursive graphs.
  • Multi-connection mode (0.4.1) allows extra read-only IPC channels for concurrent queries while the main connection keeps sole ownership of mutable workspace state.
  • The protocol is namespaced under pylance-release with no formal standards announcement, and rivals' adoption — Pyrefly, potentially ty — determines whether it becomes an ecosystem interface.
