Xiaomi Mimo 2.6 live post-training dashboard
⬆ 474 pts 💬 135 HN comments

Xiaomi's MiMo team has published a live dashboard at mimo.xiaomi.com/rl/ that streams the post-training reinforcement-learning run behind MiMo 2.6 in real time — per-step benchmark scores, progress bars, restart events and cost telemetry, punctuated by a very literal "reconnecting…" state. The context matters: post-training RL is where most of today's coding and reasoning gains are actually manufactured, and it is normally the single most secretive phase in a frontier lab's pipeline. Commenters clarify the scope — one notes these are "post-training reinforcement learning steps," not pretraining, and another points out "this is the rl run, not the pretraining run" — while a third observes that "2 thirds of the training data would be source code," a striking window into the composition of a coding-optimized Chinese model. Xiaomi is effectively turning validation, the stage labs guard most jealously, into spectator sport.

The hard numbers are what make the dashboard legible. On DeepSWE 1.1, ricardobeat reports MiMo-v2.5-Pro scored just 19 percent, while Cookingboy watches 2.6-pro "reach 63.7% by step 10" and the flash variant hit "60.7% by step 12" — against Fable at 70 percent, Kimi K3 at 69, Astra and Gemini 3.8 Flash at 74. Cost telemetry is equally revealing: ttul estimates "$5 per second… ~$432K per day," roughly "3,000 B300 nodes on Modal," with ssn2000 noting "total run cost is $1.2M until now" and asking about MFU. The community splits sharply: converts cite ROI, skeptics allege replay or synthetic data because "refresh the page the progress goes back in time," and liuliu raises benchmark contamination as a genuine methodological concern.

At roughly $5/second (~$432K/day, $1.2M total so far), Xiaomi is publicly live-streaming an RL run whose DeepSWE 1.1 score climbed from 19% (MiMo 2.5-Pro) to 63.7% within 10 steps — a level of training transparency no US frontier lab currently matches.

Training a 4B model to produce 81% faster query plans than Postgres
⬆ 594 pts 💬 122 HN comments

Rohan Bansal's article details an experiment to post-train a 4B-parameter Qwen model via supervised fine-tuning and agentic reinforcement learning to generate Postgres query plans that outperform the database's native planner. Query optimization is famously hard: join ordering is NP-hard, and the search space explodes combinatorially—4,608 plans for a three-table join, over 86 quintillion for eight. Postgres uses dynamic programming and heuristics, but imperfect cardinality estimates often yield suboptimal plans. Bansal reframes the problem as reinforcement learning: the model emits pg_hint_plan hints (HashJoin, Leading, IndexScan, etc.), a harness executes each hinted query against Postgres, and scalar rewards (relative speedup versus the default plan) flow back to update the model's weights via a custom GRPO variant designed for noisy environments. Training leveraged off-policy distillation from 500 GPT-6 Astra agent trajectories and ran across a rented 2x H100 node and four Postgres containers on the author's desk.

The results: a 1.81x geometric mean speedup and 44.7% total latency reduction across 113 join-heavy queries from the Join Order Benchmark and Cardinality Estimation Benchmark. Initially, the 4B model could not produce any plan for 99 of those queries; after training, it consistently beat Postgres's defaults. The vanilla 4B baseline failed entirely, underscoring the necessity of both distillation and RL. The experiment cost roughly $1,200—$800 for a 2x H100 node for ~95 hours and $400 in OpenAI API fees. Hacker News commenters raised sharp critiques: the dataset is only 8GB and fully in-memory, `random_page_cost` may have been tuned to 1.1, and planning time plus inference latency are ignored. Some argue hints paper over bad statistics and that classic ML or adaptive plans are better fits. Yet the write-up was widely praised as an educational deep dive into RL fine-tuning and query optimization.

A 4B model went from being unable to produce any plan for 99 of 113 queries to achieving a 1.81x geometric mean speedup and 44.7% total latency reduction, all for ~$1,200 in compute and API costs.

Key Highlights
  • Rohan Bansal trained a 4B Qwen model via SFT and agentic RL to produce Postgres query plan hints that beat default plans.
  • The model achieved 1.81x geometric mean speedup and 44.7% total latency reduction across 113 join-heavy queries from JOB and CEB benchmarks.
  • The approach used off-policy distillation from GPT-6 Astra trajectories and a custom GRPO variant for noisy reward signals.
  • Training cost ~$1,200: $800 for a 2x H100 node (~95h) and $400 for OpenAI API fees; four Postgres containers ran on the author's desk.
  • HN commenters question realism (8GB in-memory data, tuned random_page_cost, planning time ignored) and suggest classical ML or adaptive plans may be more appropriate.

OpenSpec – A lightweight and configurable AI spec framework
⬆ 162 pts 💬 75 HN comments

OpenSpec, built by Fission-AI under an MIT license (v1.13.0), is a CLI-plus-agent-skills framework that formalises spec-driven development (SDD) for coding agents. Rather than prompt-and-pray, it structures work into a five-stage loop — /opsx: explore, propose, apply, verify, archive — emitting markdown artifacts (proposal.md, specs/, design.md, tasks.md) that both humans and agents read and re-read. It ships integrations for Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, GitHub Copilot, Gemini CLI and roughly thirty other harnesses, positioning the written spec as the shared contract between team and model. The stated aim is to “build the right thing and build it right”: refine requirements, validate that they describe the right thing, then verify the implementation matches. Its traction is the headline — 68k GitHub stars, 265k monthly developers, and a claimed new spec created every two seconds.

The HN thread, however, is effectively a referendum on whether spec artifacts survive contact with real codebases. One practitioner who ran OpenSpec for 6–9 months on two large solo projects reported “huge divergence from spec to code,” concluding that “the code IS the specification” — while still keeping the *process* and ditching the *specs*. Others flag organizational friction: “every change… turns into a large set of multiple markdown documents… always slop,” an “illusion of control.” Skeptics invoke the 1990s UML/Rational Unified Process failure and argue modern long-context models plan well enough unaided; defenders counter that enforced discipline saves tokens and stops agents “going round in circles.” With SpecKit, SpecDD, spekk-cli, lat.md and others crowding the space, the live debate is standardization versus spec drift.

A practitioner's verdict on long-lived specs: "Basically the old theory is true - the code IS the specification." Yet the platform claims a new spec is created every two seconds across 265k monthly developers.

Key Highlights
  • OpenSpec is a lightweight, MIT-licensed SDD framework (v1.13.0) by Fission-AI, claiming 68k GitHub stars and 265k monthly users.
  • Workflow is a five-command loop — explore, propose, apply, verify, archive — generating proposal.md, specs/, design.md and tasks.md.
  • Integrates with ~30+ agent harnesses (Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Copilot, Gemini CLI).
  • Critics argue long-lived specs drift from code, echoing the 1990s UML/RUP failure and the refrain that 'the code IS the specification'.
  • Org adopters report review burden and AI-generated 'slop' docs, while defenders value workflow discipline and token efficiency.

HarnessTax: How Much Does the Harness Matter for Coding Agents?
⬆ 161 pts 💬 62 HN comments

HarnessTax lands on a genuinely unsettled question: how much of a coding agent's real-world performance is attributable to the model versus the scaffolding wrapped around it — the execution loop, system prompt, tool schemas, context management, and denial logic. The site itself is a near-empty JS shell in the submitted snapshot, which is itself telling: the HN thread becomes the artifact. The title's framing of a measurable "tax" implies that bloated harnesses (Claude Code, Codex) charge users extra tokens and latency for instructions and safety machinery that may not improve task outcomes, while lean harnesses like Pi pursue token efficiency. The implicit claim is that harness choice is a first-class cost lever, not a rounding error — a claim the thread immediately contests on methodological grounds.

The most substantive pushback is that the dominant variable isn't prompt length at all but tool-schema alignment with the model's fine-tuning. One commenter notes Claude models are tuned for Edit(file_path, old_string, new_string, replace_all) while GPT models expect apply_patch_call(patch), and that newer models degrade on custom tools resembling defaults — a reference to Lucumr's "Better Models, Worse Tools." Others report empirically that "two different harnesses can make a similar number of model calls while consuming a very different amount of context." The strongest counter-framing comes from practitioners who argue the tokens that earn their keep are hooks that deny bad actions and stamp provenance, not prose instructions — "the prompt-size argument skips straight past it." Meanwhile Claude Code's 80KB initial instruction dump is cited as the bloat's real source, not security.

"The part of my harness that earns its tokens isn't instructions, it's the bits that take claims out of the model's hands" — a hook denying the seventh consecutive single browser call succeeded where the system prompt asking for batching did not.

Key Highlights
  • Harness choice affects cost and reliability more than raw model "intelligence," per multiple practitioners
  • Model-native tool schemas (Edit vs. apply_patch) may outweigh harness polish entirely
  • No reliable benchmark exists that tests all major harnesses against all open-source models
  • Claude Code and Codex bloat is attributed to ~80KB system prompts, not just safety guardrails
  • Deterministic hooks and provenance stamping beat prompt instructions for reliability

Nvidia announces native GPU programming in Rust
⬆ 776 pts 💬 315 HN comments

NVIDIA's CUDA Rust announcement lands where two trends intersect: Rust's steady takeover of the AI systems layer — the Nova Linux driver, Dynamo's serving core, NVTX bindings — and the one remaining holdout, the GPU kernel itself. The post ships two distinct tracks rather than one API. cuda-oxide is a custom rustc codegen backend that intercepts #[kernel] functions, routes them through Rust MIR, NVIDIA's Pliron IR dialects, and LLVM IR down to PTX, leaving everything else to the standard backend. It enforces safety with DisjointSlice<f32> — a type that splits one mutable borrow into per-thread exclusive pieces — plus #[launch_contract] declarations that prepare_* validates against live device limits, returning a proof token the safe launch method requires. cutile-rs operates a level higher: #[cutile::module] embeds kernel ASTs for JIT through CUDA Tile IR, with host-side .partition([128]) simultaneously fixing exclusivity, grid geometry, and static tile width.

The ergonomic gap is stark. cuda-oxide demands a pinned nightly-2026-04-03 toolchain, clang/libclang, a system LLVM, Linux, and compute capability 8.0+; cutile-rs needs only stable Rust 1.89+, CUDA 13.3, and no custom LLVM — and is already published on crates.io, shipping in HuggingFace's Grout inference engine and mistral.rs, while cuda-oxide sits in early alpha. Both compile-check the classic aliasing bug, but at different points: the SIMT track fails with E0502 (borrowing c_dev mutably while immutably borrowed), the Tile track with E0382 (use of moved value). SIMT shared memory still requires unsafe, which NVIDIA calls active work. At 776 points and 315 comments, HN's reaction split between genuine interest in GPU-side Rust and irritation that the announcement itself reads machine-written — 'Damn even Nvidia is putting out fully Claude-written articles.'

NVIDIA frames the kernel as the last Rust holdout in its own stack — Nova, Dynamo, and NVTX bindings are already Rust — yet the SIMT track still asks developers for a pinned nightly toolchain, something NVIDIA admits it wants to 'stop asking you for.'

Key Highlights
  • Two tracks ship: cuda-oxide (SIMT, custom rustc codegen backend via Pliron IR + LLVM to PTX) and cutile-rs (tile-level, JIT through CUDA Tile IR on stable Rust).
  • Memory safety is enforced at compile time — DisjointSlice plus launch contracts on the SIMT side, tensor partitioning and ownership on the Tile side.
  • Aliasing mistakes fail to compile in both: E0502 for cuda-oxide's borrow conflict, E0382 for cutile-rs's moved-value error.
  • cutile-rs is on crates.io and already used in HuggingFace's Grout and mistral.rs; cuda-oxide is early alpha and needs pinned nightly plus LLVM.
  • NVIDIA promises inter-language interop across CUDA Rust, C++, and Python, and credits rust-cuda, rust-gpu, cudarc, and VectorWare as prior art.

#3
Small programming tricks
⬆ 575 pts 💬 253 HN comments

Will Keleher's essay argues that a disproportionate share of engineering productivity comes from "small nuggets of knowledge" that carry almost no prerequisite conceptual overhead. His canonical example: you need zero Python knowledge to run `python3 -m http.server`, yet it can meaningfully unblock your work. The catalog is deliberately scattershot but each item is high-leverage: rebinding ctrl+r through fzf, replacing shell history with atuin's searchable SQLite store, `SELECT` without `FROM`, regex `\b` word boundaries, log10-bucketed metrics for distribution insight, Node's long-lived `https.Agent` for connection reuse, `git log -S` as a pickaxe, `git checkout -`, zsh's unenabled `compinit`. The thesis generalizes beyond tooling: inside companies, the same class of knowledge appears as `$PROBLEM -> $DATA_SOURCE`, "$PERSON knows a ton about $AREA," the rolling-restart incantation, or the signal to manually scale out.

The prescription is social, not just technical: Keleher used to post one trick per day to his engineering team's Slack, following a practice he credits to Julia Evans, on the theory that "even if you knew 9/10 tricks, that 10th doc or technique might save you some time." The post drew 575 points and 253 comments, and the thread's strongest validation of the premise was operational rather than aesthetic: commenter alexpotato recounts an SRE credentialing battle during a blue/green deploy where tcpdump and Wireshark analysis stalled until `tcpflow` revealed that TCP messages were "getting truncated on the problem side" — a protocol-analysis detail, not a networking-expert insight, that resolved the incident. The discussion then fractures along familiar lines: memorization mechanics, AI-mediated discovery, and whether such tricks constitute programming at all.

A single `tcpflow` invocation ended a blue/green deployment deadlock that tcpdump and Wireshark had failed to crack — the thread's proof that "trivia" is really compressed operational leverage.

Key Highlights
  • Small, low-infrastructure knowledge ("nuggets") compounds into outsized productivity gains, from fzf-rebound ctrl+r to `SELECT` without `FROM`.
  • The catalog spans history (atuin, per-directory-history), search (ripgrep, globstar, `git log -S`), regex (`\b`), metrics (log10 bucketing), and networking (`https.Agent` keep-alive).
  • Organizational equivalents matter more than tooling: `$PROBLEM -> $DATA_SOURCE` mappings, `$PERSON knows $AREA`, rolling-restart commands, manual scale-out triggers.
  • Top commenters argue awareness is insufficient — tricks decay without deliberate habit overturn (undo-then-redo), spaced repetition, or a greppable scratch file.
  • Dissent clusters around scope ("computing tricks, not programming tricks"), daily-tip fatigue, and AI agents both surfacing unfamiliar commands and eroding the incentive to learn them.

Backups Aren't Simple
⬆ 275 pts 💬 171 HN comments

Backups Aren't Simple is a first-principles walkthrough of how the one-line principle 'keep another copy' metastasizes into a full engineering discipline. Aleksandar Filipovski opens with a family trauma — an external drive of irreplaceable photos reformatted by a set-top box with no warning — then rebuilds the design space from scratch. RPO defines acceptable loss (sub-30s for financial institutions, 24h for small enterprises); mirroring is explicitly rejected because RAID 1 cannot rewind time; snapshot rotation forces retention policy; and the observation that file churn is fat-tailed — combined with an MPEG-style intuition about inter-frame redundancy — leads naturally to hard-link deduplication of the rsnapshot variety. Incremental (deltas between adjacent snapshots) is carefully distinguished from differential (deltas since the last full), each abstraction adding an adjective and a new failure mode.

The second act is operational reality, and it is where the essay earns its title. Docker containers writing root-owned files silently break non-root cron jobs; databases that buffer in memory and flush in batches restore into corruption; S3 discards POSIX metadata and prices many-small-file uploads punitively, forcing chunked tarballs that then defeat hard-linking. The recommended endpoint — use Borg or Restic instead of rolling your own — reads less as endorsement than as accumulated scar tissue. The 171-comment thread corroborates every step: a Veritas veteran correcting a friend that 'We are not in the backup business. We are in the restoration business'; ZFS plus sanoid/syncoid homelabs; Restic + Backrest; CloudNativePG + Barman for point-in-time restores; tar | zstd | gpg with GNU tar's --listed-incremental; and the perennial correction that Syncthing is sync, not backup.

'We are not in the backup business. We are in the restoration business.' — a Veritas engineer's correction that reframes the entire thread, echoed by 'Backups are boring. Restores, on the other hand, are often exciting.'

Key Highlights
  • Backup design is a chain of accreted complexity — RPO, rotation, GFS, deduplication, 3-2-1 — where each abstraction solves one failure mode and introduces another.
  • Mirroring and RAID 1 are explicitly disqualified because point-in-time recovery, not redundancy, is the goal.
  • Operational details break naive schemes: root-owned Docker files, databases flushing in-memory batches, and S3's loss of file metadata plus punitive small-file pricing.
  • GNU tar's --listed-incremental plus zstd and gpg is offered as a minimal, portable alternative to opaque repository formats like Borg's or Restic's.
  • The thread's strongest consensus is that restore drills — and the ability to mount and inspect a single file — are the actual feature; Glacier-style cold storage makes that nearly impossible.

#9
A 32-year-old bug walks into a Telnet server
⬆ 71 pts 💬 34 HN comments

watchTowr Labs has published a deep-dive on CVE-2026-32746, a pre-authentication remote code execution flaw in GNU inetutils telnetd whose root cause has lain dormant since 1994 — the year before the word "RISC" meant anything to most sysadmins, as the article's deliberately florid prose reminds us. The bug is a BSS-based buffer overflow in the LINEMODE SLC (Set Linemode Characters) negotiation handler: the server stores client-supplied three-byte SLC triplets into a fixed-size global array (slcbuf, 0x6C bytes) with no bounds checking, letting an attacker corrupt roughly 400 bytes of adjacent globals. Because most vendors forked or copy-pasted the same code, the blast radius spans Ubuntu, Debian, FreeBSD 13/15, NetBSD 10.1, Citrix NetScaler, Apple macOS Tahoe, Haiku, TrueNAS Core, uCLinux, libmtev, and DragonFlyBSD. Curiously, the client-side doppelgänger CVE-2005-0469 was fixed in 2005 with the identical bounds check — nobody looked at the server for twenty more years.

Exploitation, however, is a Pandora's box rather than a point-and-click affair. Each triplet byte is constrained: a func greater than 0x1e collapses the remainder to zero, func == 0 is discarded, and 0xFF bytes are doubled, which forbids odd-length runs of 0xFF and complicates pointer overwrites on 64-bit x86 where addresses like 0x0000000802215180 contain three consecutive null bytes. The payload must also fit a 0x200-byte subnegotiation packet, yielding alignment-shift tricks via 0xFF padding. The most potent primitive emerges from def_slcbuf: by responding to LINEMODE before TTYPE, the SLC data is deferred, malloc'd, and later free()'d after terminit flips — handing the attacker a controlled free() on an arbitrary pointer. Debian 12 bookworm 32-bit proved the most interesting target; watchTowr found no clean route to RCE on the systems examined, yet the exposure varies sharply per build.

A pre-authentication Telnet buffer overflow survived undetected from 1994 until 2026 — and its exact twin on the client side, CVE-2005-0469, was patched twenty years earlier while nobody thought to check the server.

Key Highlights
  • CVE-2026-32746 is a pre-auth BSS buffer overflow in GNU inetutils telnetd's LINEMODE SLC handler, corrupting ~400 bytes of adjacent globals since 1994.
  • The flaw lives in code copied into Ubuntu, Debian, FreeBSD, NetBSD, Citrix NetScaler, macOS Tahoe, Haiku, TrueNAS Core, uCLinux, and DragonFlyBSD implementations.
  • Exploitation is gated by per-byte triplet constraints (func > 0x1e dropped, 0xFF doubling, 0x200-byte subnegotiation cap), making 64-bit x86 overwrites especially painful.
  • Deferring SLC processing via def_slcbuf yields a controlled free() primitive on an attacker-chosen pointer after terminit is satisfied.
  • The Telnetd fix author publicly disputes the article's claim that inetutils 'shamefully' failed to release a patched version, noting he sent a patch within a day.
