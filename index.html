Project Glasswing: An Initial Update
⬆ 432 pts 💬 256 HN comments

Anthropic's Project Glasswing leverages Claude Mythos Preview, a highly capable AI model, to systematically identify vulnerabilities in the world's most critical software. In its first month, the initiative—backed by approximately 50 partners including Cloudflare, Mozilla, and major financial institutions—has unearthed over 10,000 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities. The model demonstrates a striking 90.6% true positive rate on triaged findings, and organizations like Cloudflare report finding 2,000 bugs (400 critical) with false positive rates better than human testers. Mythos Preview also excelled on independent benchmarks, solving both UK AI Security Institute cyber ranges end-to-end and outperforming prior models on ExploitBench and ExploitGym. However, these achievements highlight a paradigm shift: AI now excels at discovery, but the bottleneck has moved to human-driven verification, patching, and deployment.

The open-source scanning component alone identified an estimated 6,202 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities across 1,000+ projects, with 1,094 already confirmed. Yet only 75 of 530 reported critical bugs have been patched, underscoring the strain on maintainers—some of whom have asked Anthropic to slow disclosures. The patch cycle remains the critical constraint; for example, wolfSSL's certificate-forging exploit (CVE-2026-5194) was fixed only after careful triage. Anthropic is responding with tools like Claude Security, which has patched 2,100+ vulns in enterprise settings, and the Cyber Verification Program for legitimate security research. Looking ahead, the company plans to expand partnerships with US and allied governments, while emphasizing that general availability of Mythos-class models awaits stronger safeguards—a position that sparks debate on timing and motives.

Anthropic's Mythos Preview has found over 10,000 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities in the first month, with a 90.6% true positive rate—but only 75 have been patched so far.

  • Mythos Preview identified >10,000 high- or critical-severity vulnerabilities across partners like Cloudflare and Mozilla.
  • True positive rate of 90.6% on triaged findings, with 62.4% confirmed as high- or critical-severity.
  • Open-source scanning found 6,202 estimated high- or critical-severity bugs across 1,000 projects.
  • Patching bottleneck: only 75 of 530 reported critical bugs have been fixed, highlighting human capacity limits.

Antigravity 2.0 Tops the OpenSCAD Architectural 3D LLM Benchmark
⬆ 386 pts 💬 150 HN comments

This benchmark evaluates six AI coding tools (Antigravity 2.0, Codex 5.5 High, Claude Opus, Claude Sonnet, Cursor Composer, and ModelRift) on a challenging OpenSCAD task: creating a parametric 3D model of the Pantheon from two reference images. OpenSCAD was chosen for its text-based, deterministic, and constructive solid geometry approach, which aligns well with LLM strengths in code generation and spatial reasoning. The Pantheon's mix of radial symmetry, repeated columns, portico, dome, and oculus provides a rich middle ground between trivial primitives and organic shapes. All systems successfully produced renderable OpenSCAD, but quality varied dramatically—from Cursor's weak placeholder to Antigravity's detailed autonomous output.

Notably, Antigravity 2.0 (powered by Gemini 3.5 Flash High) achieved the best autonomous result by independently searching for real Pantheon dimensions, implementing the signature interior coffered ceiling, and adding a cutaway mode. Codex 5.5 High showed impressive detail including the entablature inscription, but suffered from an export mismatch that degraded final quality. Speed did not correlate with quality; Cursor was fastest but weakest, while slower runs like Sonnet produced cleaner massing. The overall takeaway: while all systems can produce valid OpenSCAD, geometric judgment, external knowledge retrieval, and human-in-the-loop annotation remain critical for production-quality CAD, especially for complex architectural features.

Antigravity 2.0 was the only autonomous agent to search for real Pantheon measurements and implement the interior coffered ceiling pattern—a detail not visible in the provided reference images, demonstrating emergent knowledge retrieval and spatial reasoning.

Key Highlights
  • Six AI tools were benchmarked on OpenSCAD generation of the Pantheon from two reference images.
  • Antigravity 2.0 (Gemini 3.5 Flash High) produced the best autonomous result, including accurate dimensions and interior coffers.
  • Codex 5.5 High had strong detail but a critical preview-export mismatch that lowered its score.
  • Speed did not predict quality: Cursor was fastest but weakest, while Sonnet took longest and was strongest among initial autonomous runs.
  • Human-in-the-loop annotation (ModelRift) improved quality over fully autonomous approaches, highlighting current limitations in pure AI spatial reasoning.

Microsoft starts canceling Claude Code licenses
⬆ 263 pts 💬 203 HN comments

Microsoft is canceling most Claude Code licenses for its internal developers, just six months after rolling out the tool to thousands of employees. The initiative, part of an effort to let non-coders experiment with AI-assisted development, backfired: developers strongly preferred Claude Code over Microsoft’s own GitHub Copilot CLI. According to an internal memo from Rajesh Jha, the Experiences + Devices group will phase out Claude Code by June 30, the end of Microsoft’s fiscal year, citing a need to converge on Copilot CLI as the primary agentic command-line tool. However, sources confirm the decision is also financially motivated, as Claude Code’s popularity drove up operating costs. Notably, Microsoft will still allow Claude models through Copilot CLI, maintaining model flexibility while enforcing tooling homogeneity.

The move highlights a growing tension in enterprise AI adoption: cost versus developer satisfaction. Claude Code’s token consumption was a recurring complaint—developers noted it burns through allowances rapidly, sometimes exhausting monthly budgets in days. In contrast, Copilot CLI, while less feature-rich, offers tighter integration with Microsoft’s engineering workflows and lower operational overhead. Community discussion draws parallels to the “lines of code” metric, warning that token usage could become a perverse incentive. Some developers advocate for cheaper alternatives like DeepSeek, which promise comparable capability at a fraction of the cost. This internal shift may foreshadow broader industry trends, as companies balance AI’s productivity gains against ballooning expenses, potentially influencing hiring and layoff decisions.

Open source Kanban desktop app that runs parallel agents on every card
⬆ 220 pts 💬 126 HN comments

KanBots is a local-first, open-source desktop Kanban board that orchestrates Claude Code and Codex agents in parallel, with each card spawning its own git worktree and isolated branch. Unlike chat-based agent interfaces, it provides a structured pipeline: cards move through columns (Inbox, Backlog, In Progress, Review) as agents execute, with human-in-the-loop decision prompts that pause runs for approval. The app stores all state in a local SQLite database, claims zero telemetry, and is MIT licensed. It offers an Autopilot mode that cycles through customizable personas (Product Manager, Senior Engineer, etc.) with up to 4 parallel slots, splitting parent issues into subtasks and respecting a configurable cost budget. Cost analytics track per-run, per-card, and per-session spend, with hard caps to prevent surprise bills. The MCP server exposes the board to other MCP-aware tools like Cursor and Claude Desktop, making agents first-class citizens in a broader toolchain. This represents a significant shift from ephemeral agent chats to a persistent, auditable, and collaborative workflow where agent activity is visible, contained, and reviewable.

The project has garnered 220 points and 126 comments on Hacker News, reflecting intense interest and skepticism. A key tension is whether parallel agent execution violates Kanban's core principle of limiting work-in-progress (WIP) — one commenter notes Toyota's original intent was to avoid doing too much at once. Others praise the local-first approach as 'table-stakes' for serious adoption, with one user stating 'No cloud account, no telemetry, no HTTP server' is essential. Technical concerns surface around worktree infrastructure management: how to handle unique ports, environment variables, and database instances per worktree. Comparisons to Vibe Kanban (now abandoned) and other tools like Cline's Kanban and OpenAI's Symphony are frequent, with some seeing this as a natural evolution. The most poignant critique comes from a commenter who laments that 'no one is reviewing the code' in the age of agentic development, calling it 'a very sad era of software engineering.' The app's Electron shell and mobile-unfriendly UI also draw criticism, but the core idea of a board-as-orchestrator resonates deeply with the engineering community.

The app claims 'No cloud account, no telemetry, no HTTP server. Your code never leaves the machine.'

#2
Why Japanese companies do so many different things
⬆ 683 pts 💬 324 HN comments

The article explores why Japanese corporations exhibit extreme diversification, exemplified by Toto—a toilet manufacturer whose advanced ceramics division now produces electrostatic chucks for semiconductor memory chips, driven by AI demand. This phenomenon is rooted in the 'J-firm' model theorized by economist Masahiko Aoki: a bundle of complementary practices including lifetime employment, seniority-based promotion, horizontal coordination (e.g., Toyota's andon cord), and insulation from shareholder pressure. These practices form a coherent system where employee skills are firm-specific, making diversification a natural survival strategy—companies expand into new domains to redeploy talent and buffer against market shocks, rather than maximizing short-term returns. The article contrasts this with the American 'H-firm' model that prioritizes focus and shareholder value, arguing that diversification is not a bug but a feature of Japan's corporate ecosystem.

Data points underscore the scale: Toto's e-chuck business now generates the majority of its operating profit (up 230% YoY), while Kyocera spans ceramics, printers, and even kitchen knives. The article cites Paul Milgrom and John Roberts's theory of complementarities: a firm cannot easily adopt individual practices from the J-bundle without the whole package; for instance, Toyota's Kentucky plant pulled the andon cord 2,000 times weekly vs. Ford's 2. However, the system has downsides—low labor mobility, zombie companies, and stagnation. Commenters highlight this trade-off: the J-firm's focus on survival over profit can lead to inefficiency, while the U.S. model's obsession with shareholder value can starve small innovations. The debate reflects broader tensions between resilience and growth in corporate strategy.

Toto’s advanced ceramics division, which makes e-chucks for memory chips, now generates the majority of the company’s operating profit — a toilet company turned semiconductor supplier.

Key Highlights
  • Japanese firms diversify not by accident but due to a systemic 'J-firm' bundle of lifetime employment, horizontal coordination, and shareholder indifference.
  • Complementary practices mean that adopting one element (like flexible machinery) without others (e.g., broad training) yields little benefit.
  • Diversification allows redeployment of lifetime employees and buffers against demand shocks, as seen with Toto's e-chuck business.
  • The model trades off financial efficiency for stability and long-term survival, risking zombie companies and slow economic growth.
  • Western firms historically had conglomerates but shifted toward focus due to shareholder value pressure, while Japan's structure remains unique.

Sleep research led to a new sleep apnea drug
The breakthrough stems from decades of foundational neuroscience by University of Toronto professor Richard Horner, who mapped the neural circuits controlling upper airway muscles during sleep. His lab identified two key pathways: a noradrenaline-mediated 'go' signal that activates the tongue muscle during wakefulness and REM sleep, and a muscarinic receptor-mediated 'stop' signal that suppresses it. The drug AD109, developed by Boston researchers, combines atomoxetine (a norepinephrine reuptake inhibitor) and aroxybutynin (an antimuscarinic) to simultaneously boost the excitatory signal and block the inhibitory one, thereby preventing airway collapse. This pharmacological approach addresses the root neurochemical imbalance rather than mechanically splinting the airway like CPAP. In a phase 3 trial involving patients with mild to severe obstructive sleep apnea, AD109 reduced the apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) by an average of 4 events per hour compared to placebo, with improvements in oxygen saturation.
