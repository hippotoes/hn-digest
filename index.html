Show HN: Subvocal – Talk to your computer without making noise
⬆ 787 pts 💬 364 HN comments

Subvocal is a Show HN launch of a camera-free silent-speech interface embedded in smart glasses. The core claim is that RF sensing can capture articulatory gestures around the face and decode them into text at speech speed, allowing users to talk to a computer without audible sound or visible lip movement. The team positions this against camera-based lip-reading projects such as LipFlow and LipNet: as one commenter summarized, 'All of these require a camera. Their solution does not.' Architecturally, antennas sit in the glasses temples, while decoding runs through a companion laptop or phone app, with the founder claiming end-to-end latency is usually under 500ms. The pitch combines accessibility, hands-free input, and privacy-preserving interaction, but the HN reaction immediately tests whether the hardware form factor, RF exposure, and cloud dependency match those promises.

The founder's replies add the most concrete data. They argue speech is faster than typing: 'Most people type at 40-50 WPM... most people can talk at 120-200 WPM,' while noting developers may type at 70-100 WPM. They also describe a possible denoising mode where audible speech is cleaned using the RF signal, open-ear speakers in future glasses, and no initial wake word. But the implementation is early: internal speech has only been tested on the two founders and one employee, requiring user-specific personalization. Commenters press on SAR limits near the emitter, cancer and hearing risks, offline inference, battery life, and the mismatch between 'the glasses decode them' and cloud processing. Fit is another practical fault line: adjustable nose pads, big heads, low bridges, and prescription frames all expose the difficulty of a one-size-fits-all wearable.

The thread is overwhelmingly intrigued, but not credulous. The strongest enthusiasm is for silent input in noisy or socially awkward settings and for users with carpal tunnel, tracheostomies, or speech impairments. The strongest objections are not about whether the ML could work, but whether the product should be glasses-only, whether RF and cloud decoding are safe and private, and whether the launch page explains the technology clearly enough to avoid seeming like vaporware.

The founder's most persuasive framing is speed: 'Most people type at 40-50 WPM... most people can talk at 120-200 WPM' — and Subvocal aims to capture that speech-rate input without making noise or using a camera.

Key Highlights
  • Subvocal uses RF sensing in glasses temples to detect facial/articulatory movements and decode silent speech without a camera.
  • Decoding runs through a companion app on a laptop or phone/cloud, with claimed end-to-end latency usually under 500ms.
  • Founders claim speech input at 120-200 WPM versus 40-50 WPM typing, plus future RF-based denoising of audible speech.
  • Internal speech has only been tested on two founders and one employee, so user-specific personalization remains unproven at scale.
  • Major concerns include glasses-only fit, attachable/earbud alternatives, SAR/RF safety, privacy/offline inference, and launch-page clarity.

Writing seen in PHerc. 306B, for the first time in 2000 years
⬆ 29 pts 💬 0 HN comments

PHerc. 306B is one of the carbonized papyri from Herculaneum, reduced to a fragile black cylinder by the 79 CE eruption of Vesuvius. For two millennia, its text has been inaccessible not merely because the roll is brittle, but because the ink and substrate are both carbonized; any physical unrolling would destroy the object and probably the writing. The reported milestone—writing visible again in PHerc. 306B—comes from the computational pipeline championed by the Vesuvius Challenge/Scroll Prize: high-resolution X-ray micro-CT captures the internal 3D structure of the rolled scroll, virtual unwrapping segments and flattens individual papyrus layers, and machine-learning ink detection infers letterforms from faint density and texture signals. The significance is less a single decoded word than proof that a complete, unopened scroll can yield legible text through non-destructive imaging and computation.

The technical difficulty is formidable. Synchrotron CT volumes are enormous, layer segmentation must track sheets that touch and deform, and carbon ink provides almost no native contrast against carbonized papyrus, so detectors must learn subtle 3D patterns from sparse labeled fragments. The phrase 'writing seen' is therefore precise: it indicates visible strokes or letterforms, not necessarily a continuous scholarly transcription. Still, each new scroll expands the corpus of recoverable classical texts and tests whether the 2023 breakthrough generalizes beyond isolated fragments. The societal payoff could include lost works of philosophy, poetry, and history; the methodological payoff is a reusable stack for fragile manuscripts. It also sharpens questions about open scan data, reproducibility of ML inferences, conservation ethics, and who gets credit for—and access to—digitally recovered cultural heritage.

The breakthrough is not that the scroll was unrolled, but that a carbonized object can be read in silico: X-ray tomography plus ML turns a 2,000-year-old density signal into visible writing.

Key Highlights
  • PHerc. 306B is a carbonized Herculaneum scroll that cannot be physically unrolled without destroying it.
  • The recovery pipeline combines high-resolution X-ray micro-CT, virtual unwrapping, layer segmentation, and ML-based ink detection.
  • The milestone is visible writing after 2,000 years, not necessarily a complete or scholarly verified transcription.
  • Carbon ink and carbonized papyrus have very low native contrast, making ML inference and labeled training data central bottlenecks.
  • The work could recover lost classical texts while raising questions about open data, reproducibility, conservation, and attribution.

Katamari Damacy but it's The Met collection
⬆ 98 pts 💬 29 HN comments

Jay Mollica's browser toy applies the Katamari Damacy mechanic — roll a sticky sphere, absorb smaller objects, grow, absorb larger ones — to the Metropolitan Museum of Art's Open Access collection, where every object is a genuine CC0 catalogued artifact rendered at its real physical size. That constraint is the actual design insight: the Met's holdings happen to span a naturally graded size ladder, from coins, thimbles and netsuke up through furniture and architectural fragments to the Temple of Dendur, which is precisely the progression curve a Katamari clone needs. Implementation-wise it is a WebGL scene with WASD/arrow rolling, mouse-drag joystick input, a per-run timer, a leaderboard with share links and CSV export, and a full-collection preload step before the first run — the latter a clear pain point flagged by mobile users.

The Hacker News thread (98 points, 29 comments) is a case study in a community that praises the concept while auditing the execution against the original's very specific feel. Complainants are unusually precise: vunderba calls the soundtrack something that "sounds like it was procedurally generated by Claude Code," thepuglor argues every clone misses "the transition between zoom levels," and codingdave reports a reproducible right-click-while-moving input-state bug that leaves movement stuck — a classic missing pointer/blur handler. Others note excessive camera elasticity at small ball sizes and Firefox mobile over-sensitivity. The deeper critique is conceptual: codingdave argues that a game built to squash artifacts into a ball "might not be the best way to help people actually appreciate" them, countered by ChrisRR's "a silly game to mess about with them isn't a bad thing" — while Larrikin notes the Sackler wing still appears, folding institutional critique into a browser game.

Every object in the gallery is a real Metropolitan Museum artifact from the Open Access (CC0) collection rendered at its true physical size — the size ladder from coins and netsuke to the Temple of Dendur is what makes the Katamari growth loop work at all.

Fujifilm to boost tape production 5x as AI fuels archiving demand
⬆ 97 pts 💬 30 HN comments

Fujifilm will sink roughly ¥10 billion (about $63 million) into its Odawara site in Kanagawa prefecture to raise LTO tape production capacity fivefold against FY2025 baselines, with the build-out running across fiscal 2026–2028 and focused on the current-generation FUJIFILM LTO Ultrium10 media. The company frames the demand curve as an AI artifact: generative workloads are producing what it calls "exponential growth" in data needs, and the cheapest place to park cold bytes remains a plastic cartridge on a shelf. Fujifilm's lineage here is unusually long — magnetic tape since the 1950s, first LTO Ultrium product in 2000, first commercial barium-ferrite tape in 2011, and Gen 10's "fine hybrid magnetic particles" blending barium and strontium ferrite. Tape wins not on latency but on $/TB, near-zero idle power, and the ability to be physically air-gapped — properties that matter more as datasets balloon and threat models harden.

The economics explain why hyperscalers never left. Gen 10 offers 30TB and 40TB native variants; HN commenters surfaced street pricing of roughly $259–$300 per 30TB cartridge (~$8.63–$10/TB) and $491–$555 for the 40TB SKU (~$12.28–$13.88/TB), with the older 18TB LTO-9 at $92–$129 (~$5–$7/TB). The hidden tax is CapEx: standalone LTO-10 drives are quoted near $25,000, which is why drive rental and AWS Snowball-style services persist. Competitive alternatives are not close — M-Disk media runs roughly $150/TB versus ~$6/TB for tape, a factor of ~25 once drive costs amortize. Fujifilm explicitly markets the air gap as ransomware defense, saying tape is "attracting increasing attention as a means of protecting data from threats such as ransomware" — though the thread's real lesson was operational: verify archives by restoring, not by trusting a write-time checksum.

A standalone LTO-10 drive costs around $25,000 while the media runs ~$8.63–$10/TB — tape's cheapness is a media story, and its adoption barrier is entirely CapEx.

Key Highlights
  • Fujifilm commits ~$63M to quintuple LTO tape output at its Odawara plant from FY2026–2028, targeting LTO Ultrium10 media.
  • Gen 10 uses hybrid barium/strontium ferrite particles; capacity has grown every generation, with Gen 11–14 projections already published by the LTO consortium.
  • Media costs ~$5–$14/TB depending on generation, but LTO-10 drives at ~$25,000 dominate upfront cost.
  • M-Disk optical alternatives are roughly 10–25x more expensive per terabyte and capped at 100GB per disc.
  • The air gap is the real selling point: tape can be physically detached, making it a last line of defense against ransomware and archive-now-decrypt-later campaigns.

Killing what can't be handled – putting a spaghetti demon to eternal rest
⬆ 19 pts 💬 3 HN comments

The article recounts a long-overdue rewrite of the control-flow-graph structurizer in dxil-spirv, the component that translates unstructured DXIL control flow into SPIR-V structured control flow for Vulkan translation layers. The old implementation had become a 9,000-line “black hole” with a bus factor of one: it relied on brittle heuristics intended to preserve human-readable output, and it repeatedly collapsed on monster path-tracing shaders—one was too large for Graphviz to render in finite time. After years of patching leaks with “scotch tape,” the author abandoned the readability-first approach and ported Mesa’s goto_if structurizer, the algorithm used for unstructured OpenCL SPIR-V, while retaining performance as the primary constraint.

The rewrite is validated by unusually rigorous tooling. A fake SPV_FROG_unstructured_control_flow path sends raw spaghetti to the driver, showing RADV parity; structurizer-test was upgraded to emit runnable SPIR-V; and a fuzzer generates random reducible CFGs, executes 4,096 randomized walks per graph on the GPU, and checks bit-exact feedback buffers, with CPU simulation guarding against infinite-loop hangs. The algorithm builds serialization levels from immediate-dominator children and dominance frontiers, uses path selectors and outer-merge dispatch, splits switch blocks into conditional branches, lowers PHI nodes to OpVariable/OpStore, and handles loops via back-edge candidates, break redirection, skippability analysis, and maximal reconvergence. The trade-off is deliberate: less debuggable generated code, but far greater correctness under adversarial shader inputs.

The old structurizer was a 9,000-line “black hole” with a bus factor of one; the replacement trades human-readable output for correctness and is stress-tested with 4,096 randomized CFG walks per graph plus bit-exact GPU feedback.

#7
Antimony: CAD Software Evolved from Lisp Machines and Not Drafting Tables
⬆ 13 pts 💬 0 HN comments

Antimony is Matt Keeter's research-grade CAD system, grown out of personal-scale CAD/CAM work at the MIT Center for Bits and Atoms and descended from cad_ui, kokopelli, and fabserver. Its premise is a deliberate counterfactual: CAD as it might have evolved from Lisp machines rather than drafting tables — symbolic, script-first, programmatic — instead of the feature-tree-and-mouse idiom that SolidWorks and Fusion inherited from the drafting board. Architecturally it rests on three mostly-orthogonal axes: a DAG framework that tracks information flow and auto-propagates changes downstream, a CSG geometry engine, and a standard library of shapes and transforms. Nodes are Python 3 scripts extended with a few 'magic keywords' — input() and output() inject typed variables into the local namespace and declare ports — so a circle node is four lines of importable Python, and the design file is executable source rather than an opaque binary document.

The geometry substrate is the technically interesting part: Antimony models solids with functional representations (signed distance fields), which compose cleanly under boolean union, intersection, and difference. A circle is literally the prefix string '-r+q-Xf%gq-Yf%gf%g', a parseable-for-machine, cryptic-for-humans encoding of sqrt((x-x0)^2 + (y-y0)^2) - r; the mapping to infix is relegated to Appendix A of Keeter's thesis. Shapes render as shaded bitmaps blitted into a 3D viewport, with UI features defined in the same node script — sb.ui.wireframe and sb.ui.point calls are drawn over the render and wired back into the graph, so dragging a corner point pushes changes upstream. Export targets heightmaps for 2.5D processes and STL with feature detection to keep corners and edges sharp. Keeter is candid that maintenance tapered off around 2016 and that the project sits at beta stability, 'solid, but not recommended for mission-critical use.'

In a thread with essentially no community commentary, the author's own remark is the only substantive signal: he asks for a '(2015) label', reports that he has been 'tapering off maintenance since 2016,' and redirects readers to the more actively-maintained fork stibium — 'albeit with heavy LLM assistance, because it's 2026!' The hedge 'YMMV – I haven't used it or closely inspected the changes' is a notably disciplined disclosure from an original author endorsing a successor he cannot fully vouch for.

Antimony's circle primitive is the literal string '-r+q-Xf%gq-Yf%gf%g' — a prefix-encoded signed distance field — evidence of a CAD stack where geometry is program text rather than a feature tree.

Key Highlights
  • Antimony inverts CAD lineage: it descends from Lisp machines and symbolic programming rather than drafting tables, making designs executable Python rather than GUI-authored documents.
  • Three orthogonal axes: a DAG information-flow engine with automatic downstream propagation, a functional-representation CSG geometry engine, and a shapes/transforms standard library.
  • Nodes are Python 3 scripts using input()/output() magic keywords; UI widgets (wireframe, points) are declared in the same script and feed edits back into the graph.
  • Geometry is encoded as signed-distance-field prefix strings — compact and parseable, but 'a bit cryptic to decipher,' with the infix mapping documented in the thesis appendix.
  • Original author mkeeter confirms maintenance tapered off since 2016 and points to the fork stibium, which is actively maintained 'with heavy LLM assistance, because it's 2026!'

Jailbreaking my car's navigation system just so I can change the time
⬆ 9 pts 💬 1 HN comments

A 2014 Renault Latitude owner in Estonia discovered that his TomTom Carminat LIVE head unit (codename beaumont, Samsung S5P6440 SoC, Linux 2.6.28.10) simply cannot change its clock without a legitimate, DRM-signed navigation SD card inserted. The Settings applet's menu definition is itself read from a CID-locked card, and the card authenticity checks (CID-bound file signatures) reject clones. Rather than pay ~60 EUR for a shipped card just to fix a daylight-saving offset, he reverse-engineered a firmware update image, discovered an ext3 root filesystem inside a ttsystem blob, and stood up a QEMU emulation of the unit with a patched 2.6 kernel and a minimal CAN stub to probe it — discovering a defence-in-depth posture (read-only rootfs, noexec on every writable mount, unionfs over SD) that is unusually strong for 2011-era embedded hardware.

The eventual exploit chain is a small masterpiece of composable primitives. The master.script automation language yielded two useful bugs: a SHEX/SHELLEXECUTE path that shells out via launchserver but accepts only one whitespace-delimited token, and a LOG directive with classic path traversal writing printable ASCII (0x20–0x7E) as root. Neither is sufficient alone. The author borrowed the core_pattern technique from kernel exploitation: write |/bin/sh /media/sdcard/payload.sh into /proc/sys/kernel/core_pattern via LOGFILE ../../proc/sys/kernel/core_pattern, then use SHEX /bin/dbus-monitor (which SIGABRTs without a system bus) to trigger a coredump the kernel pipes to a root shell — valid because the feature landed in 2.6.24. From there, dd'ing the eMMC revealed /mnt/flash/settings.dat, a custom binary holding only a raw local-offset in minutes (180 → 120): the unit has no timezone concept at all.

The absurdity is the point: a shipped consumer vehicle hides a trivially correct setting behind a paid, DRM-locked accessory whose absence also blocks the entire Settings applet, forcing a full jailbreak to perform an operation every 1990s alarm clock exposed in a menu. It is also a preview of a coming wave of orphaned firmware — Estonian winter time was the trigger this year; Canada's DST abolition and repeated timezone-policy churn will strand older head units whose rules and offsets can no longer be updated through supported paths. The safety, security and right-to-repair lessons converge: noexec mounts and signature checks raise the bar, but do not stop a determined owner armed with an emulator and a known-good kernel primitive, and they impose real costs on legitimate users.

The kernel's own coredump handler was the way in: writing `|/bin/sh /media/sdcard/payload.sh` to `/proc/sys/kernel/core_pattern` with a path-traversal log directive, then crashing `/bin/dbus-monitor`, yielded root — the entire fix was changing 180 minutes to 120 in a binary settings blob.

Key Highlights
  • Time settings live behind a CID-DRM'd SD card; no card means no Settings applet at all, not merely missing maps.
  • Modified firmware images are rejected by an unseen recovery-partition verifier with an apparent DSA/SHA-1 40-byte signature layer.
  • Composable primitives: single-token SHEX execution plus root-level LOG path traversal, unified by the kernel's core_pattern pipe handler.
  • Mount hardening (ro rootfs, noexec on /tmp, /var, /mnt/flash, SD, unionfs) forced a non-traditional escalation path.
  • Final fix required no maps: /mnt/flash/settings.dat stores a bare UTC offset in minutes, with no timezone database.
