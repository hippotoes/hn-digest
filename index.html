#1
A curmudgeon tries a language server
⬆ 89 pts 💬 55 HN comments

The article chronicles a self-described curmudgeon's attempt to bring Lisp-like live development to Haskell. The author's baseline workflow is the classic edit-compile-run loop in a terminal, and he envies Lisp programmers who hot-swap code inside a living process. To close the gap, he assembles a stack around Emacs Eglot, the Haskell Language Server, ghcid for fast reloads, and the foreign-store library to preserve state across restarts. For a toy differential-equations visualizer, he implements a custom update loop that runs tests, cancels an async rendering thread, and reuses SDL resources stored in MVars. The setup works, but only after compromises: ghcid's inability to track multi-component projects forces all code into one executable, and the exact invocation that finally worked remained unexplained—it functioned only inside a Nix dev shell.

The experiment yields mixed returns. The author reports that the ghcid-and-foreign-store experience is genuinely satisfying for exploratory coding: changing constants or adding book-derived examples instantly updates the visualisation. Yet sharp corners abound—Eglot adds enough latency to disrupt Vim-style editing, reconnect is sometimes needed after dependency changes, and the high-level Rapid library was abandoned for hand-rolled plumbing. HN commenters largely refuse to accept the Lisp framing. Common Lisp developers say source files remain primary and that image-based development is less anarchic than portrayed, while others call the whole idea a disaster and compare it to Jupyter notebook chaos. Some defend LSP as orthogonal to live reloading, and a few share GDB and Clojure as pragmatic alternatives. The post ultimately reads less as a solution and more as a map of unresolved tensions in developer tooling.

Instead of creating a complicated gui for defining differential equations and initial conditions and whatnot, I can change constants in the code and the visualisation updates. The tradeoff was accepting unexplained tooling quirks, from Nix-shell-dependent ghcid to LSP latency that breaks Vim muscle memory.

Key Highlights
  • Assembling the toolchain requires Nix flakes, direnv, envrc, and Eglot; getting hls to connect is finicky and occasionally needs manual reconnects.
  • ghcid can only watch one component at a time, forcing all code into one executable; this kills the workflow for serious multi-component projects.
  • Live reload is achieved with foreign-store MVars and Async cancellation, but the high-level Rapid library was too unstable.
  • The apparently working ghcid command mysteriously failed outside a Nix development shell, despite direnv.
  • The post's Lisp ideal is questioned by actual Lisp developers, and the broader HN thread debates whether image-based development is a wonderful ideal or a dangerous mess.

#2
The turbulent AI era is here
⬆ 71 pts 💬 322 HN comments

Bill Gates' latest essay, 'The turbulent AI era is here,' published on his personal blog, frames AI as a double-edged sword: a tool for immense productivity gains and a potential source of mass displacement and geopolitical turmoil. The HN thread, however, quickly diverges from Gates' nuance into sharper economic and political debates. Many commenters seize on the piece's acknowledgment that the transition will be painful, but they challenge his proposed solutions—such as a robot tax and an expanded social safety net—as either insufficient or dangerously naive. A recurring theme is the 'consumption paradox': if AI erases employment, who will buy the goods and services the machines produce? Several commenters reference historical analogies (Henry Ford paying workers enough to buy cars) and note that AI's unique ability to automate the jobs it creates may make this technological shift fundamentally different from the Industrial Revolution.

A distinct sub-thread focuses on policy comparisons: some call for direct democracy, universal basic income, or international AI treaties, while others dismiss these as unworkable given capital interests. One data point from a commenter cites that data center construction alone added 315,000 skilled-trade jobs over five years, but questions whether that is cycle-specific. Another highlights Gates' own 2016 Reddit AMA prediction about computers reading and understanding text, noting how far AI has come. Distrust of Gates himself runs deep, with references to his friendship with Epstein and accusations that his 'human-centered' rhetoric is fear-based marketing in the style of Anthropic. A smaller set of comments reflects on AI's social impact—from addictive AI companions to the loss of in-person serendipity—and one reader is struck by the phrase 'Human Reserved' as a depressing label for an AI-run future.

The most resonant question from the thread: 'If all jobs are taken away for the sake of robot driven productivity, who will consume the products and services being offered that result in profit?!' — encapsulation of the economic paradox.

Key Highlights
  • Gates calls for proactive policy (robot/token tax, social safety net) but HN commenters question motives and feasibility.
  • The economic debate centers on whether AI is unlike prior tech revolutions – it may automate the very jobs it creates.
  • Commenters highlight a consumption paradox: if labor income disappears, aggregate demand collapses.
  • There is a broad distrust of billionaires setting AI policy, citing past behavior like Epstein association.
  • Some see AI driving decentralization and human/social changes, from city decline to addictive AI companions.
