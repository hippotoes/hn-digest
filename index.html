A portentous reunion
⬆ 107 pts 💬 29 HN comments

Bryan Cantrill recounts attending his 30th college reunion, where a prevailing anxiety among peers centered on AI's impact on the future and their children. However, the reunion also triggered a nostalgic revival of BattleTris, a two-player Tetris duel game he built in 1993 that became legendary at Brown University. The game had been lost to bit rot for over two decades—running only on 32-bit Solaris—until Cantrill and co-creator Adam Leventhal used Claude (an LLM) to port it to modern Linux. Claude solved tedious compatibility issues, including a stack smashing bug caused by a 32/64-bit integer size mismatch that surfaced during their first match in two decades. The fix enabled dozens of reunion attendees to play BattleTris deep into the night, creating a profoundly human moment enabled by the very technology causing their unease.

Cantrill highlights the paradox: LLMs like Claude restored a piece of personal computing history that nostalgic engineering effort alone had failed to resurrect. The porting process was 'speculative' and 'indeterminate'—hard to gauge progress until Claude rapidly cleared obstacles. The game itself has deep lore: Cantrill met his wife Brigid through a BattleTris match she won, and a couple at the reunion recalled the game 'almost broke us up over.' One classmate, Adam Leventhal, who organized a BattleTris tournament in 1999, later joined Cantrill at Sun and co-created DTrace. The article closure teases a public release. Cantrill uses this experience to argue that LLMs are a tool, not a dehumanizing overlord, and predicts more 'BattleTris-like' joyful moments ahead.

"The game that almost broke up a couple now reunited by Claude—and a stack smashing bug from 32-bit legacy."

Unlike traditional RPA, which relies on brittle scripts, Minicor uses deterministic Python code for core workflows and employs LLM-based vision agents solely for error recovery and UI adaptation. This hybrid architecture achieves 93-96% click accuracy, significantly outperforming pure computer-use models that hover at 80-85%. The platform is HIPAA compliant and SOC 2 Type II certified, supporting on-premise, cloud, or Citrix deployments. Minicor's technical foundation revolves around self-healing: a reflection agent verifies every action against the screen state, automatically correcting when UI elements shift. Observability is built-in, offering full video recordings of automation runs and Slack notifications for failures. The system exposes a simple REST API—one POST request triggers a workflow, returning structured JSON. Founder fchishtie notes that automations run as code for speed and cost efficiency, with LLMs used only for edge cases. This pragmatic approach resonates with engineers frustrated by maintenance overhead. However, commenters raised valid concerns about PHI leakage in screenshots, which the team addressed by cropping to non-sensitive regions and allowing log storage within customer VPCs.

That Methyl Methacrylate Tank
⬆ 344 pts 💬 138 HN comments

The incident involved a 80,000-gallon tank of methyl methacrylate (MMA) monomer in Garden Grove, California, which began leaking after a fitting failure. MMA, a precursor to plexiglass, is volatile and prone to polymerization in an exothermic runaway reaction if not properly inhibited. The tank's contents started to polymerize, generating heat and pressure. A crack developed, allowing vapor to vent and preventing a catastrophic boiling liquid expanding vapor explosion (BLEVE) that could have leveled city blocks. Workers attempted to inject inhibitors but the system failed. The leak persisted for days, raising environmental and health concerns, though MMA is not highly toxic. This event parallels historical accidents, such as the 2007 T2 Laboratories explosion and the 2013 West Fertilizer explosion, highlighting the dangers of reactive chemicals.

Hacker News commenters pointed to the lack of passive safety systems and regulatory oversight in the US chemical industry. One user noted that inhibitor chemicals are the passive protection, but can't be used in excess without ruining the product. Others drew comparisons to the Fukushima disaster, emphasizing the need for fail-safe designs. The thread also referenced a paper on styrene and butyl acrylate incidents from IOMosaic. Commenters lamented the local government's poor communication, relying on Xitter for updates. A notable insight was that the polymerized MMA becomes a large, inert plastic block, which could be safely removed. The incident underscores the tension between industrial efficiency and safety, and the need for robust emergency response plans.

A fortuitous crack in the tank prevented a catastrophic BLEVE, turning a potential city-leveling explosion into a controlled polymer release.

#9
BadHost – CVE-2026-48710: Starlette Host-Header Auth Bypass
⬆ 69 pts 💬 25 HN comments

A critical vulnerability (CVE-2026-48710) has been disclosed in Starlette, a lightweight Python ASGI framework that underpins FastAPI, vLLM, LiteLLM, and countless AI/ML inference servers. The flaw allows an attacker to bypass path-based authentication middleware by injecting a crafted Host header. For example, a request to `/protected` with `Host: example.com/health?x=` will cause Starlette to parse `request.url.path` as `/health` instead of the actual path, granting unauthorized access to protected endpoints. This arises from incorrect URL parsing when the Host header contains a path component, a classic case of parser disagreement—the HTTP specification allows such malformed headers, but Starlette's parser interprets them differently than the reverse proxy or downstream middleware. The vulnerability affects virtually all versions of Starlette prior to the patch, making it a widespread issue in the Python web ecosystem, particularly in AI-heavy deployments where these frameworks are used for their async performance and large-file handling capabilities.

Despite the severity of the bypass, the vulnerability has been rated as 'Medium' by the maintainers, a decision criticized by security researchers like ostif-derek, who notes that 'medium understates how hard it hits thousands of downstream projects and billions of installs.' The timing just before a major US holiday weekend likely delayed patching across many organizations. However, the attack is significantly mitigated if Starlette is not exposed directly to the internet but instead sits behind a reverse proxy (nginx, Apache, CDN, or API gateway) that validates the Host header. Yet, as commenters point out, many MCP servers and AI agent frameworks run directly on public ports, especially in fast-moving AI deployments where security hardening is often overlooked. The vulnerability underscores the dangers of path-based authentication (condemned by janci as 'bad practice') and the perennial wisdom of never using string manipulation for URIs, as magnio recounts from bitter experience.

Rating it a medium understates how hard it hits thousands of downstream projects and billions of installs. People need to patch asap.

Key Highlights
  • CVE-2026-48710 allows Host header injection to bypass path-based auth in Starlette and FastAPI.

Finite projective planes, known from the card game Spot It!, provide a different set system where any two lines (blocs) intersect in exactly one point. By assigning nodes to points and designating each line as a valid quorum, the protocol can achieve consensus with far fewer nodes than a majority—e.g., 8 instead of 29 in a 57-node cluster.
