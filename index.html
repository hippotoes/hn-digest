I quit OpenAI because its culture is broken
⬆ 305 pts 💬 547 HN comments

A member of OpenAI's safety organisation has publicly resigned via The Atlantic, arguing that the company's internal culture is structurally incapable of supporting the safety mission it nominally pursues. The article itself was paywalled and returned HTTP 403 to automated retrieval (a gift link was circulated in comments, alongside archive.ph/archive.is mirrors), so the discussion is driven by the piece's central factual hook: an operational incident in which, per commenters quoting the text, "OpenAI let a swarm of agents out by mistake" during a Hugging Face episode. That framing matters — it moves the argument away from speculative existential risk and toward concrete engineering governance: deployment cadence outrunning verification, agent scaffolding with inadequate containment, and safety staff lacking authority to block releases. Coming after the Superalignment dissolution and a steady 2024–2026 bleed of senior safety personnel, the resignation reads less as an outlier than as a data point in an institutional trend.

The thread's ratio is itself a signal: 305 points against 547 comments, roughly 1.8 replies per upvote, indicating a contested reading rather than consensus applause. Commenters quote the author's operational complaints — "perpetual sprints," an environment where "things like this can happen," and the demand that "before we create systems significantly more capable than the ones we have today, we need new science to ensure that more capable models ... will make safe choices when we aren't looking." Recurring counter-proposals are institutional rather than technical: enforced liability, independent audit, and redundant-subsystem design borrowed from civil engineering, insurance, and nuclear regulation. The practical question the community keeps returning to is whether a voluntary lab charter can substitute for a safety case that some external party is legally empowered to reject.

"An environment where things like this can happen is no place to grow artificial minds that could be smarter than we are and that might not do what we want them to" — the resigning safety lead's own summary, anchored to a reported incident in which OpenAI reportedly released a swarm of agents by mistake.

LeCun's rhetorical evidence: agents that hacked a sandbox were "doing exactly what they've been asked to do," with "leaky and horribly designed" containment; U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent similarly blamed OpenAI management. The stakes are policy: EA-aligned lobbying versus LeCun's no-new-regulation position.

Agents don't need memory, they need documentation
⬆ 238 pts 💬 131 HN comments

A 238-point, 131-comment Hacker News thread titled "Agents don't need memory, they need documentation" argues that persistent agent memory — vector stores, embedding-based RAG, auto-summarisation plugins — is the wrong abstraction, and that structured, human-readable, repo-resident documentation is the right one. The article body itself returned HTTP 403 to our fetcher, so the thread's discourse is the substantive artifact here; commenters reconstruct the thesis as: documents are auditable, diffable, versionable and shared across agents and humans, whereas memory blobs are opaque, context-free and silently stale. The underlying technical claim is that retrieval should be a deterministic, inspectable artifact of the repository rather than an embedding-space guess — essentially relocating context engineering from a model-side index into the codebase's own file tree, where git provides provenance and review.

The commentariat largely accepts the diagnosis and dissents on the mechanism. CapitalistCartr delivers the sharpest structural rebuttal: "The sharpest critique he makes of RAG is that agents can't search for what they don't know. A markdown 'brain' has the same problem" — index files merely move the retrieval problem to the agent. Practitioners describe concrete layouts: zenapollo splits .agents/plans, notes, and knowledge, committing only stable knowledge with an INDEX.md once it grows; gregwebs argues for no agent-only docs, relying on CONTRIBUTING.md, CODING_STANDARDS.md and ADRs. Others report failure modes: b-karl's memories pointed at archived repos, mzhaase's agent citing self-invented rule "D-236." dham counters with "All of the comments here are overengineering. The code is the documentation," while jmtulloss supplies the thread's bluntest methodological demand: "Evals or it didn't happen."

"The sharpest critique he makes of RAG is that agents can't search for what they don't know. A markdown 'brain' has the same problem." — CapitalistCartr, dismantling the article's own proposed remedy with its central objection.

Key Highlights
  • Renaming the problem: memory stores are replaced by repo-resident markdown, but the retrieval-lookup problem is merely relocated, not solved
  • Multiple practitioners independently converged on .agents/ or workbench folders with plans/notes/knowledge tiers, treating notes as ephemeral and knowledge as permanent
  • Enforcement is identified as the true bottleneck — agents silently ignore rules and revert to baseline 'grep and Python' despite explicit instructions
  • Staleness plagues both paradigms: memories polluted by external refactors (archived repos in plans) and documents that go stale just as fast
  • Repeated demands for deterministic feedback loops — lint rules that explain fixes, hooks, static type-checking, RLVR-style scoring, and evals as the arbiter

The work by Valve's Timur Kristóf on improving old AMD GPUs on Linux
⬆ 345 pts 💬 60 HN comments

Timur Kristóf, a graphics developer on Valve's Linux team, used his XDC2026 talk in Toronto to document a year of work moving AMD's GCN 1.0/1.1 'Southern/Sea Islands' GPUs — the 2012-era Radeon HD 7000/8000 series — off the legacy radeon DRM driver and onto amdgpu. This is not cosmetic: RADV, the Vulkan implementation underpinning Steam's Proton stack, targets amdgpu's infrastructure specifically, because it depends on GPUVM address-space management, the amdgpu GPU scheduler, SDMA copy engines, fences and DPM power management that the legacy driver never exposed. Coming from years in Mesa user space, Kristóf had to repair display code (DC) for these ASICs, chase down power-management regressions and ACPI/ATOMBIOS table quirks, then add soft-reset support so hung MEC/rings recover without a full PCI reset — the plumbing that decides whether a decade-old card can boot a modern compositor at all.

The payoff is measurable: Phoronix attributes roughly a 30% performance boost for old Radeon GPUs to Linux 6.19 alone, alongside unlocked RADV Vulkan and better display/power behavior. AMD has largely stopped investing in these parts, so this is effectively Valve underwriting maintenance of abandoned silicon — a pattern reflected in the thread's 345 points and 60 comments, which range from praise for 'legendary behaviour' to donation requests answered with 'just buy their products.' Commenters report that an Ayaneo 2 handheld and even an RX 9070 XT on NixOS now run faster than under Windows, and several speculate about reviving e-waste GPUs for encoding, passthrough or LLM inference — a path currently blocked by missing FP4/FP8 hardware and VRAM ceilings, as one RX 6900 XT owner discovered.

One kernel developer's effort on a GPU family AMD effectively abandoned delivered a ~30% performance uplift in Linux 6.19, converting 2012-era Radeon HD 7000/8000 cards into viable Linux gaming hardware.

Key Highlights
  • Valve's Timur Kristóf presented at XDC2026 on migrating GCN 1.0/1.1 Radeon GPUs (2012-era HD 7000/8000) from the legacy radeon driver to amdgpu.
  • The migration unlocks RADV Vulkan, modern power management and soft reset support; Linux 6.19 alone brought ~30% performance gains.
  • The work fills a gap AMD has deprioritized, with the community crediting AMD's open stack as the enabling factor versus NVIDIA's closed Vulkan stack.
  • Commenters report strong real-world results on handhelds and modern cards, and praise hardware longevity and e-waste reuse.
  • Revival as LLM/GPGPU compute is constrained by absent FP4/FP8 support and VRAM limits, while one dissenter warns about QA risk in touching well-tested driver code.

#2
Hole Punch: Sling your spaceship around gravitational fields
⬆ 313 pts 💬 74 HN comments

Hole Punch is a browser-native orbital-mechanics puzzle: the player drops, drags, and resizes point masses — 'holes' — into an otherwise empty field, then launches a spacecraft whose trajectory is entirely determined by the summed inverse-square pull of those masses. Success is scored not on speed but on a matter budget, a design choice that reframes gravitational assist from a brute-force maneuver into a minimisation problem. Each 'sector' presents a launch position, a docking target, and a constrained reservoir of mass; the UI exposes undo, reset, keyboard control (space to start/stop, arrows to add or subtract mass), and a landscape-only mobile console gated behind an 'Operating instructions / Understood' modal. Underneath the retro-terminal chrome — 'Unit 0426 · Mk II', 'Docking confirmed' — sits a small continuous-state simulation whose real content is the player's internalised intuition for conic sections, gravity-assist geometry, and the sensitivity of a long trajectory to an early perturbation.

The reception is a case study in the durable market for zero-install physics toys: 313 points and 74 comments for a page whose entire article text is a handful of UI strings. Commenters report clearing all 20 levels, and the most analytically interesting thread concerns solution diversity — 'It is even sneakier that I am' is answered by 'It used pairs of holes in a very different way than I did', evidence that the puzzle admits genuinely distinct trajectory families rather than one authored path. The thread also surfaces prior art and its discontents: Michael Fogleman links his own recently vibe-coded Gravity Assist, another commenter notes the steering is '1:1 the same' as The Long Journey Home, and a third quips that both authors 'stumbled upon the same rlhf checkpoint' — a pointed aside about converged aesthetics in machine-assisted game prototyping.

"It is even sneakier that I am" — a commenter on the game's built-in solutions, which 'used pairs of holes in a very different way than I did', hinting that each sector admits multiple genuinely distinct slingshot families rather than a single authored path.

Key Highlights
  • Matter-budget scoring reframes gravity assist from a stunt into a minimisation problem, making 'least matter' the true skill metric.
  • The pointer-and-drag UI is landscape-gated and breaks outright on mobile: 'Tapping on a blackhole does nothing' on Safari and Chrome Android.
  • Dragging a hole has no reversible inverse — mass can be added but not subtracted or deleted except by undo, which several call 'maddening'.
  • Levels admit multiple distinct solutions; the author's/computer's pair-of-holes approach surprised even players who solved the sector.
  • A dense prior-art cluster emerged (Gravity Assist, Gravitee, The Long Journey Home, Scorched Earth, Interplanetary, archived Flash games), alongside snark about converged vibe-coded output.

David Buchanan (retr0id) demonstrates that C2PA's content-provenance guarantees can be defeated without attacking any cryptographic primitive, only the specification's own semantics. A typical C2PA manifest carries two signatures: a claim signature asserting what the asset is (capture device, GPS, time), and an RFC 3161 timestamp signature from an external Time Stamp Authority that independently witnesses 'I saw this hash at this time.' Buchanan's earlier work already showed the claim signature is effectively worthless in flagship implementations like Google Pixel Camera, but the TSA layer is assumed honest here. The vulnerability is the spec's allowance for arbitrary 'exclusions' — byte ranges deliberately left out of the signed hash. By setting an exclusion of start 0, length 3995383 (the entire file), the signer hashes the empty string.

The PoC is blunt: a photograph of a EuroMillions ticket, signed with a genuine C2PA manifest and a genuine trusted timestamp, then poorly Photoshopped with the winning numbers hours after the draw — and it still verifies at verify.contentauthenticity.org. The dumped manifest shows hash 47DEQpj8HBSa+/TImW+5JCeuQeRkm5NMpJWZG3hSuFU=, the SHA-256 of /dev/null, proving the claim signature covers nothing and the TSA timestamp merely attests to that nothing. Neal Krawetz flagged large exclusion ranges in his June 2025 list of C2PA flaws, but framed them as incidental rather than adversarial. Buchanan notes this is not trivially fixable: PNG chunk CRC32 checksums create a genuine circular dependency requiring exclusions, so he proposes per-format whitelists of permitted excluded ranges enforced by verifiers.

'We're effectively signing nothing at all, but as of today all the C2PA verification tools I can find don't flag anything as unusual.' — the hash 47DEQpj8HBSa+/TImW+5JCeuQeRkm5NMpJWZG3hSuFU= is literally SHA-256 of an empty string, and it still passes verification.
