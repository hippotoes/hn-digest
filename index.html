⬆ 162 pts 💬 59 HN comments

The article '5NF and Database Design' offers a critical reassessment of traditional pedagogy surrounding the fifth normal form in relational databases, arguing that conventional explanations are needlessly convoluted and disconnected from practical engineering. Author Alexey Makhotkin advocates for a more intuitive design methodology rooted in business requirements and logical modeling, emphasizing that normalization should emerge naturally from real-world scenarios rather than abstract mathematical dependencies. By introducing patterns like the AB-BC-AC triangle (illustrated with ice cream preferences) and ABC+D star (exemplified by musician performances), the piece demystifies 5NF, showing how to derive schemas that avoid redundancy without invoking complex formalisms. This approach shifts focus from rote memorization of normal forms to a systematic process that begins with entity-relationship modeling and progresses to physical table design, making advanced normalization accessible to practitioners.

Specific implementations detailed include using junction tables for many-to-many relationships in the ice cream example and opting for composite or synthetic primary keys based on uniqueness constraints in the musicians case. The article cites concrete examples, such as the 'Traveling salesman' scenario from Wikipedia criticized for its artificiality, and contrasts it with more plausible models like brand-flavor-friend preferences. Hacker News comments reveal a polarized discourse: some engineers, like jerf, dismiss numbered normal forms as academic overhead, while others, such as cremer, value them as teaching tools. This reflects broader industry tensions between formal database theory and agile, NoSQL-influenced practices, underscoring the enduring relevance of foundational design principles in optimizing data integrity and query performance across modern systems.

You don’t really need to involve 5NF to design your table schema. You begin with the logical model, and apply a textbook table design strategy that preserves normalization.
