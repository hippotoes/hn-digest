#3
A Jev that can ask Opus for help gives Opus accuracy at 4x lower cost
⬆ 527 pts 💬 125 HN comments

Hightouch's data team turned an internal nuisance — verifying that an identity resolution graph hasn't over-merged distinct people — into a controlled benchmark of the emerging 'decision model' class: small, non-reasoning models that emit a calibrated 0–1 confidence for a single yes/no proposition. Using pseudopeople (a synthetic US town generator from IHME at UW), they built 1,000 identities, half single-person-with-noise and half deliberate over-merges, and asked Claude Opus 5.5, Gemini 3.1 Pro, GPT-6 Sol, GPT-6 Luna (Decisions API) and Jev to judge each profile. The framing matters: identity resolution has no ground-truth answer key in production, human review runs ~50 identities/hour, and rare over-merge rates need >1,000 samples for ±1% precision — so a cheap, parallelizable judge would compress months of IDR validation into seconds.

Raw accuracy told a flattering story for the small models — Luna 97.3% at $0.10/1,000 and 0.17s versus Opus's 99.0% at $3.86 and 2.2s — but the harder, adversarially constructed test (twins, Jr./Sr., typo-corrupted key fields) collapsed Luna to 88.8%, level with Jev's 89.1% and well under Opus's 95.1%. The differentiating variable was calibration, not accuracy: Jev's errors clustered near 50% confidence while Luna's landed in the 90–100% bucket. That made an escalation harness viable — send only the 30–90% confidence band to Opus — yielding 98.8% at $0.88/1,000 on the easy set and 95.3% at $2.94 on the hard set, versus $5.39 for Opus alone. Because the band widens automatically as data gets harder, spend scales with difficulty without prompt changes.

Jev's inferior out-of-the-box accuracy (95.6% vs Luna's 97.3%) was an asset, not a liability: its mistakes sat near 50% confidence while Luna's clustered at 90–100%, so escalating only Jev's 30–90% band to Opus hit 98.8% accuracy for $0.88/1,000 identities — roughly 4x cheaper than Opus alone at 99.0%.

Key Highlights
  • Decision models (Jev, GPT-6 Luna Decisions API) cost $0.05–$0.10 per 1,000 identities at ~0.15s each, up to 90x cheaper and 10x+ faster than frontier LLMs
  • On adversarial cases (twins, Jr./Sr., corrupted key fields) frontier models pull ahead: Opus 95.1% vs Jev 89.1% and Luna 88.8%
  • Jev's confidence is well calibrated — errors near 50% — while Luna is overconfident, with mistakes in the 90–100% bucket
  • A confidence-band escalation harness (Jev first, Opus for 30–90%) reaches 98.8% at $0.88/1,000 vs $3.86 for Opus alone; on hard data 95.3% at $2.94 vs $5.39
  • Because the escalation band widens on harder input, cost scales automatically with difficulty — no harness or prompt change required

Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash
⬆ 350 pts 💬 240 HN comments

Google DeepMind's Gemini Omni 1.2 Flash is a video-generation model positioned as a production-grade upgrade to Omni 1.1, not a successor to the Gemini 4 family. Its technical pitch is native 15-second generation, up from 10 seconds, with one-second duration increments from 3 to 15 seconds, stronger reference adherence across character, product and style images, short motion clips and audio cues, multi-turn conversational editing, improved first-to-last-frame consistency and more natural multi-speaker lip-sync. The workflow is deliberately tiered: draft at 360p or 720p, iterate with follow-up edits rather than full regenerations, then upscale to 1080p or 4K. It is generally available via the Gemini API and Google Cloud with Provisioned Throughput and SynthID watermarking, and surfaces in Flow, Google Vids, the Gemini app and Google Ads. The strategic logic is clear: video generation is compute-expensive, so Google is selling cost-controlled iteration and brand consistency rather than one-shot novelty.

The pricing is the sharpest technical-economic detail: Google says it cut the price 30% from Omni 1.1 to $0.07 per second for 720p, with token pricing at $1.50 per million input tokens and $9/$12 per million output tokens for text/video. But the commentariat immediately found the seams. One user calculated that a 720p 10-second clip in Flow went from 15 credits under 1.1 to 30 under 1.2; another said it is cheaper via API but double the cost in Flow, where credits have historically been 5-6x cheaper than API pricing. The model's limitations also matter: several creatives report that without first and last frames, idiosyncratic character designs regress to 'TV commercial blandness,' and that iteration remains slow and expensive. The launch thus exposes a broader tension: Google is optimizing a video model for ad-scale production while its developer surfaces, naming and entitlements remain fragmented.

Google cut API pricing 30% to $0.07/sec for 720p video, but users immediately flagged that Flow credits for a 10-second 720p clip doubled from 15 to 30 — the 'cheaper' story depends entirely on which Google surface you use.

  • 30% API price cut to $0.07 per second for 720p, with draft-at-360p/720p and upscale-to-1080p/4K workflow.
  • Generally available on Gemini API and Google Cloud with SynthID watermarking and Provisioned Throughput.
  • Community backlash centers on confusing product naming, fragmented rollout and Flow credit costs doubling despite the lower API price.

Tesla to wind down Cybertruck production
⬆ 1073 pts 💬 1927 HN comments

Tesla has confirmed it will wind down Cybertruck production at its Austin gigafactory around mid-2027, repurposing the line capacity for the Cybercab — the butterfly-doored, steering-wheel-less robotaxi Musk has made the centerpiece of Tesla's pivot from automaker to AI/robotics company. The decision closes a loop opened at the 2019 unveiling, when Musk promised a stainless-steel exoskeleton pickup built 'like origami' from folded sheet; the production vehicle never delivered that architecture, shipping instead with aluminum castings and a welded unibody wearing stainless panels as non-structural skin. Other engineering choices — a 48V low-voltage architecture, steer-by-wire, and paint-free bodywork — were genuinely novel, but the truck's unyielding wedge geometry collided with crash regulations governing pedestrian impact, making European homologation effectively impossible and confining sales to a handful of markets.

The commercial data is brutal and unambiguous. Kelley Blue Book puts US Cybertruck sales at 20,237 units last year, down 48% from 38,965 in 2024, against Musk's pre-launch projection of 250,000 annually. Former Aston Martin CEO Andy Palmer delivered the epitaph: 'It doesn't make sense to keep it going when it's not meeting its targets... Every rational auto guy would say the same.' Analysts note the failure is not uniquely Cybertruck's — the Ford F-150 Lightning also flopped — but the segment-wide collapse of electric pickups in a US market where EV share sits near 6% removes the strategic cover. Cybertruck lead engineer Wes Morrill framed the transition as continuity: 'Cybertruck was an incredible technology development platform and lives on through Cybercab' — a claim now complicated by pending NHTSA questions over whether the Cybercab has been properly self-certified.

Tesla sold 20,237 Cybertrucks in the US last year — down 48% year-over-year and roughly 8% of Musk's original 250,000-unit annual forecast.

Key Highlights
  • Cybertruck production ends at Austin around mid-2027, with capacity redirected to Cybercab
  • US sales fell 48% to 20,237 units in the latest year versus Musk's 250k projection
  • The stainless exoskeleton concept died in engineering; production uses castings plus welded unibody
  • Pedestrian-impact crash rules made European homologation impossible, capping the addressable market
  • Electric pickups broadly underperformed — the F-150 Lightning also failed — undercutting a segment-wide thesis

#2
Libghostty 1.0
⬆ 814 pts 💬 129 HN comments

Libghostty-vt 1.0 marks the stabilization of a terminal-emulation engine extracted from Ghostty and packaged as a portable C library. The release matters less as a version bump than as an infrastructure event: the C ABI is now stable, semver-governed, and enforced in CI with libabigail baselines on Linux plus golden type manifests on macOS and WebAssembly. The design deliberately avoids ABI fragility through opaque handles, enum-keyed getters and setters, size-carrying structs, pinned enums, and GHOSTTY_UNSUPPORTED returns when newer headers meet older libraries. The library is MIT-licensed, links only libc by default, can be built freestanding without SIMD, and ships proper SONAMEs, pkg-config files, CMake configs, signed tarballs, and offline dependency bundles for distro packaging.

Adoption is already broader than the Ghostty application itself. JetBrains has moved its IDE terminal to libghostty, reporting over 12.5 million recurring active users; Neovim has an approved PR replacing libvterm, closing a decade-old scrollback-reflow issue; KDE developers are experimenting with a Konsole replacement; Turborepo, devenv, Antithesis, OrbStack, Ghostel for Emacs, and ghostty-web all embed it. CI covers Linux glibc/musl, macOS, iOS, FreeBSD, Windows, Android, and freestanding WebAssembly, with secondary targets including bare-metal RISC-V and thumb, WASI, illumos, and 32-bit x86. Hashimoto reports running 10,000 active terminals on a 4-core, 8 GB Linux server, and argues a feature-rich terminal can now be built in roughly 1,500 lines of C.

JetBrains alone reports 12.5 million recurring active users on libghostty-powered terminals — more people than use Ghostty the application, by a very large multiple.

Key Highlights
  • libghostty-vt 1.0 stabilizes both the library and C ABI with semver and CI-enforced ABI checks.
  • Opaque handles and enum-keyed accessors let the API grow without adding symbols or breaking binaries.
  • Packaging is distro-ready: signed tarballs, offline dependency cache, SONAME, pkg-config, CMake config, and debug info.
  • Major adopters include JetBrains IDEs (12.5M users), Neovim, KDE experiments, Turborepo, devenv, Antithesis, Emacs Ghostel, and OrbStack.
  • The library is MIT-licensed, libc-only by default, optionally freestanding, and tested across Linux, macOS, iOS, FreeBSD, Windows, Android, and WASM.

US FAA says it will phase out floppy disks from air traffic control by 2028
⬆ 364 pts 💬 295 HN comments

The FAA has announced it will retire floppy disks from air traffic control operations by 2028, framing the move as part of a broader modernization of the aging U.S. ATC system — a program carrying a $12.5 billion price tag, with Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy seeking a further $30 billion in upgrades. The affected system is the Integrated Display System, IDS-4, which runs on MS-DOS and uses 3.5-inch diskettes to load critical weather and flight data into controller workstations. Commenters with direct knowledge, including one who worked on FAA NOTAM digitization, describe a safety risk management regime that is genuinely rigorous but structurally glacial — where an 80-something engineer who refuses to retire is not a punchline but a load-bearing part of institutional memory. The disks are not merely archaic, they are the physical substrate of a certification boundary that no one wants to be the person to break.

The technical substance matters more than the headline: the debate is not about whether the FAA should stop using floppies, but what replaces them and at what blast radius. One HN commenter connected the announcement to an October 13 incident in which a fiber cut outside Houston George Bush Intercontinental severed data services between FAA facilities and forced a ground stop — with others clarifying that the severed link was itself the backup, while the primary was under inspection. That is the real anxiety: an air-gapped, physically-carried 1.44MB workflow has a failure mode of a bad sector and a human courier, while a networked replacement has a failure mode of correlated, fleet-wide loss. The community's sharpest edge was directed less at the FAA than at the framing: "21st-century system" is a procurement slogan, and the $12.5B-plus-$30B ask drew immediate skepticism about cost discipline versus consumer-grade compute.

"Anyone who's worked around systems in that category knows that the fact that they're replacing floppies is more scary than the fact that they're still using floppies" — bita_nidir, capturing why legacy replacement is riskier than legacy itself.

Key Highlights
  • The system in question is IDS-4 — MS-DOS based, early-1990s vintage, using 3.5-inch floppies to distribute weather and flight data, not the 1980s as often reported.
  • An October 13 fiber cut near Houston forced a ground stop; commenters noted the severed cable was the redundant path while the primary was being inspected, raising questions about true path diversity.
  • Cost skepticism dominated: $12.5B already allocated plus a $30B request, versus back-of-envelope estimates of roughly $1–3M per airport for compute, cabling, radar and radios.
  • Commenters with FAA exposure describe a rigorous but slow safety risk management process, staffed partly by engineers well past retirement who are the remaining authority on undocumented behavior.
  • Physical media offers de facto air-gapping, but floppies suffer magnetic degradation, head wear and bad sectors — and any workflow writing to a disk still touches a networked machine upstream.

#6
Firecracker VM Escape: PwnEscape Part 2
⬆ 260 pts 💬 110 HN comments

PwnEscape Part 2, published by Paulos Yibelo of PWN Research, is the operational sequel to a host-memory primitive disclosed earlier: here the guest turns that read/write aperture into control of the very host thread running the microVM. The chain is a clean four-stage address resolver. A nonce-stamped guest page provides a known GPA; an EPT-root scan walks nested page tables until a candidate resolves to host physical memory containing that nonce, yielding guest_page_hpa (0x165610000). A reverse page-table mapper then searches native host page tables for that physical page, recovers Firecracker's PGD (0x14afc2000), and confirms it by re-walking in the forward direction. An ELF image matcher verifies byte signatures in Firecracker's write(2) wrapper to compute the ASLR load bias (0x7298c2400000); finally a frame finder scans for a pt_regs candidate whose RIP sits at the KVM_RUN ioctl continuation and whose syscall number, request (0xae80), fd, stack pointer and segment selectors all agree — a single frame survives at 0x13a5bff48.

The payload is deliberately minimal: overwrite the saved RIP, RDI, RSI, RDX and RSP to invoke Firecracker's own write wrapper as write(2, "N8V23M2\n", 8), with the marker staged on Firecracker's private userspace stack. A two-entry return sequence, beginning with xor eax,eax; ret, normalises the write's return of 8 back to KVM_RUN's expected 0 before resuming the original ioctl continuation. A single write to guest serial port 0x3f8 forces the VM exit that restores the doctored registers; three stack words are then restored. The host captured exactly eight bytes on Firecracker's stderr, PID 2936 stayed alive, guest SSH remained responsive at 1615.72s uptime, and executable text and shared libraries were unmodified. The critical caveat: the target ran as root under systemd-run with no jailer, so the demonstrated privilege is VMM-level, not a jailer or namespace escape.

The demonstrated primitive is a genuine reversible hijack of a live vCPU thread, not a crash: execution leaves and rejoins Firecracker's ioctl path without corrupting state. Yet the team's own disclosure scopes it to bare-metal Firecracker v1.16.1 on kernel 7.0.0-1011-aws (CVE-2026-99999), and the article is explicitly silent on privilege gains past the jailer boundary — precisely where production deployments place seccomp, namespaces and UID drops. HN's reaction splits along that line: exploit-chain significance versus the missing second-stage LPE, with a vocal minority dismissing the writeup as LLM-generated marketing and a rebuttal from Thomas Ptacek calling it the first general-purpose Firecracker escape in recent memory that avoids nested virtualization.

The entire proof was eight bytes written to Firecracker's own stderr — write(2, "N8V23M2\n", 8) — triggered by a single guest write to serial port 0x3f8, proving code execution as root on the host while the VM stayed SSH-reachable.

Key Highlights
  • Four-stage resolver chains EPT walk (nonce page → guest_page_hpa) to a reverse host page-table scan recovering Firecracker's PGD, defeating both address spaces
  • ASLR is defeated by matching known byte sequences in Firecracker's write(2) wrapper and the ioctl continuation to derive a load bias
  • pt_regs frame identification requires seven correlated invariants, not just a return-address match, to exclude stale stack contents
  • Return-value hygiene (xor eax,eax; ret) lets the hijacked thread resume KVM_RUN seamlessly — write returns 8, KVM_RUN expects 0
  • Target ran as root with no jailer: the escape reaches VMM privilege, not containment-defeating host control, per the researchers' own caveat

GitHub's new Git storage architecture
⬆ 174 pts 💬 42 HN comments

GitHub has unveiled "Project Rubicon," a ground-up rewrite of its Git data plane motivated less by today's traffic than by a projected agent-driven future in which a single repository absorbs sustained thousands-of-writes-per-second bursts. The design rests on one observation: Git objects are immutable and content-addressed, so two writers can store the same pack concurrently without agreeing on anything. Only reference updates require consensus, and they are tiny. Rubicon therefore decouples what was historically coupled — packs and read accelerators (multi-pack indexes, reachability bitmaps) live in Azure Blob Storage, while references and the pack manifest live in a purpose-built replicated log, Ref-CAS, accessed via compare-and-swap. Stateless "virtual replicas" serve reads and writes with disposable local caches, Spokes routes for cache warmth but never correctness, and a separate compaction fleet merges packs directly against blob storage so serving nodes spend their CPU on requests.

The engineering narrative is essentially a tour of rejected designs. A single-blob write-ahead log for references gave correct, cheap reads but capped writes at one in-flight conditional write per round trip — "mid-hundreds per second" in practice. Sharding references across segment blobs raised that ceiling but forced every read to check all segments, trading write latency for read latency in a system where reads outnumber writes by orders of magnitude. Ref-CAS exists because neither object-storage approach satisfied both axes. Internal testing claims 5,000 concurrent agents on one repository at 7,200 pushes/second with low-latency reads, and GitHub frames the requirements as two orders of magnitude of headroom. Notably, today's busiest repos see only "dozens of writes in a single second," so this is capacity built for a trend — one repository they cite grew traffic 4x in three months — rather than for present demand.

"Very little of a write needs to be ordered" — because Git objects are immutable and content-addressed, coordination collapses to the per-reference compare-and-swap, letting storage, serving, and maintenance scale independently.

Key Highlights
  • Ref-CAS: a dedicated replicated log with compare-and-swap for references and the pack manifest, replacing object-storage conditional writes that capped throughput in the mid-hundreds/sec
  • Packs, indexes, bitmaps, compaction leases and checkpoints all live in Azure Blob Storage since they are write-once to distinct keys
  • Stateless virtual replicas with rebuildable local caches; Spokes routes for cache affinity but correctness never depends on placement
  • Separate compaction fleet works directly against Blob Storage and Ref-CAS so serving nodes aren't starved by pack merging
  • Claimed benchmark: 5,000 concurrent agents, 7,200 pushes/sec to one repository — roughly two orders of magnitude beyond today's busiest repos

Notes on San Marino
⬆ 99 pts 💬 39 HN comments

Matt Lakeman's three-day visit to San Marino is really an essay about how a sovereign political entity survives: the world's oldest continuously operating republic (population 33,500, 61 km², GDP per capita ~$65.5k nominal) has no documented founding, only a 1243 notary deed naming two Consuls. The article's analytical spine is a taxonomy of 13 attempted invasions or annexations between 1300 and the present, and why each failed — diplomatic pre-emption, buffer-state utility to Urbino/Montefeltro, papal reluctance to be seen marching on a poor and pious monastery-town, and sheer contingency (Borgia's 1502 occupation collapsed only because Cesare's regime imploded in 1503; a 1543 mercenary night raid was foiled by fog). Lakeman's thesis is counterintuitive: San Marino survived because it was too meek to be worth conquering, then repeatedly re-narrated itself — Christian piety first, republicanism and conservatism later — to stay legible to whichever power held the peninsula.

The modern data points are the payload: median age 48.6, life expectancy 86 (ranked #2 globally on some lists), zero murders per 100,000, 0% population growth, 1.5% GDP growth, and an export profile dominated by 'washing and bottling machines.' Internally, the country is nine *castelli* — the capital is only the third-largest at ~4,000 people, hollowed out by tourism and government, while Serravalle holds a third of the population as a commuter suburb of Rimini. The essay also flags the 20th-century comic compression of Sammarinese politics: a Fascist period, an immediately following Communist period, and then mild moderation. The broader point — that a European microstate can have a functionally lost origin story only 140 miles from Rome — is what pulled HN readers into an argument about what 'country' even means.

San Marino claims the title of world's oldest republic, yet its founding can only be bracketed to a 900-year window (300–1243 AD): the first hard evidence of self-governance is a 1243 land-sale deed referencing two Consuls — an office structure the country still uses today as the Captain Regents.
