#5
Tokens too cheap to meter
⬆ 321 pts 💬 214 HN comments

jyn's "Tokens too cheap to meter" argues that the cost of machine-learning inference is collapsing by orders of magnitude per year — roughly 2.5 orders on raw token cost and ~100x on cost-per-task in the last year alone — and that this trajectory is driven by compounding, mostly independent technical vectors. GPU power efficiency is doubling about every two years (a log-linear fit of slope 1.3, the steepest since 1960s Moore's Law); Mixture-of-Experts deactivates expert layers to cut compute at equal benchmark quality (a 7x parameter reduction, 6B to 0.8B, at parity); Mamba and Mamba-Transformer hybrids replace unbounded KV memory with lossy streaming state, letting Nemotron-H-47B hold over a million tokens in 32 GB of quantized VRAM where a comparable Llama-3.1 60B needs ~120 GB; and inference engines remain immature, with vLLM improving ~40% in 15 months, NVIDIA's MLPerf stack up to 50%, and Intel showing 2.4x throughput from software alone on fixed silicon. The author's conclusion is that we are nearing a regime where quality and access, not token count, are the binding constraints.

The second half extrapolates consequences. Using Fermi estimation, a GPT-5.6 Luna tool-decision turn (10k tokens, ~0.33¢) is 4.5 orders of magnitude more expensive than a local grep and only 1.5 orders above a 30-second cargo build — so embedding models inside tools like build schedulers and jgrep-style filters becomes attractive. jyn invokes Jevons paradox on both sides: supply (cheaper inference invites more capex regardless of margin compression) and demand (what do you actually do with a billion tokens?). The most striking data point is TypeSafe's Jev: $0.042 per MTok input and free output — "$42 per billion tokens," or ~3 cents to read five books — plus open-weight Laya, which is faster and more accurate when fine-tuned but needs ML expertise and is capped at 512-byte contexts. Predictions include worsening cybersecurity, centralization on hyperscalers, software moats shifting from codebases to operations and security, and a "fourth option" for buyers: tell an LLM to build it.

"Output tokens: FREE (too cheap to meter)" — TypeSafe's Jev classifier costs $42 per billion input tokens, about 3 cents to read five books, while carrying a title deliberately echoing Lewis Strauss's 1954 promise of nuclear power "too cheap to meter" — a precedent HN commenters did not let pass.

Key Highlights
  • Composite claim: ~2.5 orders of magnitude cheaper tokens and ~100x cheaper cost-per-task in one year, from stacked improvements in GPUs, models, inference engines, and specialized classifiers
  • Architectural shifts matter more than raw token pricing: MoE cuts compute ~7x at equal quality, and Mamba hybrids cut local memory needs 5x+ (1M tokens in 32 GB vs ~120 GB)
  • Specialized yes/no models (Jev, open-weight Laya) drop costs another 1-2 orders of magnitude and enable tools like jgrep at ~$0.012 per 994 HN titles
  • The Fermi comparison of LLM turns to grep/cargo build anchors the "tokens cheaper than tool calls" thesis, though critics note grep is itself a moving lower bound
  • Community consensus: the trend is real, but the evidence base (Artificial Analysis Pareto charts, GPU regression lines) is methodologically weak, and business-model viability is the article's unexamined assumption

#10
Mercury 2.5 LLM hits 770 tokens per second
⬆ 131 pts 💬 74 HN comments

Inception's Mercury 2.5, a proprietary reasoning model released September 2026, lands at a striking point on the speed-intelligence Pareto curve: 780.8 output tokens per second on Artificial Analysis, ranking #2 of 173 models, yet only 12 on the AA Intelligence Index, below the median for its price tier. It supports text-only input and output, a 260k-token context window, and is priced at $0.25 per 1M input tokens and $0.75 per 1M output tokens, with a 90% cache discount and a blended rate of $0.14. The AA Index v4.3.2 aggregates ten evaluations, including Terminal-Bench 4.0, SciCode, Humanity's Last Exam, and AA-Omniscience, so a score of 12 reflects broad deficiency across coding, knowledge, and agentic work rather than one benchmark. The HN discussion treats Mercury as an embodiment of diffusion-style or otherwise non-autoregressive LLM serving, contrasting it with frontier autoregressive models where reasoning depth and tool-use reliability still dominate.

Commenters quickly reframe the tradeoff: whalesalad asks what you use a 'stupid model that is fast' for, while rvz argues 'the speed means absolutely nothing' when a model finishes 'almost dead last.' freakynit reports Mercury feels 'on par with any 14B model' and worse than GPT-OSS-20B; bearjaws points to Cerebras gpt-oss-120b at 1400 tk/s, though LoganDark warns that deployment 'screws up tool calls' and costs '$5-10/minute.' Artificial Analysis notes Mercury 2.5 is concise (35M output tokens vs 85M median) but has 2.91s TTFT, above the 2.22s median. Others, like timClicks, counter that speed enables iterative draft-refinement with weaker models, while nextaccountic argues the real bottleneck is tool calling and hosting the model near the code repository and reference context. The thread's deeper concern is that in agentic workflows, tool-calling reliability, co-location with code and context, and efficient hardware sharing may matter more than raw token velocity.

Claude discovers a novel enzyme system with CRISPR-like repeats
⬆ 701 pts 💬 714 HN comments

Anthropic's newly formed life-sciences group—staffed by veterans of CRISPR-evolution and genome-mining research and backed by a Bay Area BSL-1/BSL-2 lab—gave Claude a single high-level prompt to mine public DNA databases for uncharacterized reverse transcriptases (RTs), the enzymes that copy RNA back into DNA. Roughly 950 parallel agents burned 210 million tokens over 21 hours, gathering ~200,000 RTs, distilling 3,500 candidate systems, and writing 20 human-readable reports. One agent flagged a "tandem repeat array … CRISPR-like" beside an odd jumbo-phage RT: array-associated reverse transcriptases (ART), comprising the RT, a partner gene, and evenly spaced non-coding repeats transcribed into discrete short RNAs. The critical caveat is that the underlying RT was already known; Claude's novelty lies in connecting the repeat array and accessory protein.

The technical claim is genuinely intriguing—Feng Zhang called the RNA-repeat/RT association "genuinely intriguing and merits further investigation"—but the framing drew fire. Commenters noted the system resembles retrons, that function is uncharacterized, and that no editing capability has been demonstrated, prompting one PhD drug-discovery lead to call it "the saddest excuse for scientific discovery… excruciating lack of rigor, methods, or disclosure." Anthropic did release a pre-print and a technical report crediting human authors, and describes the work as autonomous hypothesis generation with human lab validation. Still, the episode crystallizes a broader tension: labs building frontier models now run internal biology programs, raise biosafety questions ("Also Anthropic: Claude discovers a new way to edit your genome!"), and shift the narrative from tools to scientific actors.

"I can see by eye a tandem repeat array … that's a CRISPR-like … repeat array?!" — roughly 950 Claude agents spent 210 million tokens across 21 hours to surface ART, a jumbo-phage reverse-transcriptase system whose actual function remains unknown.

Key Highlights
  • Anthropic spun up an internal life-sciences group and lab, then ran ~950 Claude agents for 21 hours on 210M tokens, sifting ~200,000 RTs into 3,500 candidate systems and 20 reports.
  • ART (array-associated reverse transcriptases) pairs an RT with a partner gene and a CRISPR-like non-coding repeat array, mainly in jumbo phages; the array is transcribed into short RNAs.
  • The underlying reverse transcriptase was previously identified—Claude's claimed novelty is noticing the associated repeat array and accessory protein, not the enzyme itself.
  • Feng Zhang endorsed it as "genuinely intriguing," and Anthropic released a pre-print plus technical report crediting human authors alongside the agentic workflow.

Linux support is coming to Snapdragon X2 Series
⬆ 495 pts 💬 204 HN comments

Qualcomm used Snapdragon Summit 2026 to announce that Linux support is coming to the Snapdragon X2 Series, framing it inside a broader push around 'agentic AI PCs' and Googlebooks. The article itself is JavaScript-gated and near-empty, so the substantive signal lives in the announcement text quoted by commenters: Qualcomm says it is 'upstreaming core drivers for Snapdragon X2 Series — including the Hexagon NPU and Adreno GPU — to open the door to developers and partners.' Technically this matters because ARM laptops sit at the awkward intersection of UEFI/ACPI and device trees: as hurricanepootis notes, a SoC can be supported upstream yet be useless without a per-device DTB, and the ACPI tables Qualcomm ships are reportedly coupled to proprietary Windows drivers. The counter-evidence is real, if partial: Qualcomm hired developers from Linaria, has been posting patches to the Linux ARM MSM mailing list for months (including an EC driver for the Asus ZenBook A16), and OpenBSD's Tobias Heider has already committed arm64 pieces getting USB, keyboard and touchpad working in ACPI mode on an HP EliteBook X G2q — with ARM EL2/KVM now functional.

The scope caveats are the story's sharpest edge: the work covers laptops only, excludes desktops, earlier Snapdragon X platforms and dev boards, and 'readiness also varies by OEM design and Snapdragon X2 Series variant.' Commenters give separate Debian and Ubuntu timelines as evidence of distro-specific binary drivers rather than mainline integration — prmoustache calls it 'a trap' where a vendor-stopped driver could break on later kernels. On performance, nr378 cites Geekbench showing the X2E-96-100 'isn't too far off the Apple M5 Pro,' though sharktheone counters that single-core still trails the M6. With 495 points and 204 comments, the thread is dominated not by enthusiasm but by institutional memory: the X Elite was announced with near-identical language and, per cogman10, Qualcomm will 'half-ass support until the next product is announced and then they'll disappear into the ether.' The latent upside is integration — one SoC means one DTB and no Wi-Fi/audio/camera lottery — plus hopes for a Framework X2 mainboard.

"Every single one of the 'X' series had this identical announcement... Qualcomm has proven that they'll continue to half-ass support until the next product is announced and then they'll disappear into the ether." — cogman10, capturing a comment section where 495 points of attention sit on top of deep institutional skepticism.

Key Highlights
  • Qualcomm commits to upstreaming Snapdragon X2 core drivers — Hexagon NPU and Adreno GPU — rather than shipping a semi-proprietary Chromebook-style stack.
  • Scope is narrow: laptops only, excluding desktops, earlier Snapdragon X platforms and development boards, with per-OEM readiness variance.
  • Separate Debian and Ubuntu timelines suggest distro-locked binary drivers rather than mainline kernel integration, raising kernel-upgrade fragility concerns.
  • Prior art contradicts the marketing: X Elite received the same announcement, saw partial upstreaming, yet devices remain stuck on Windows for many owners.
  • Concrete engineering progress is visible — Linaria hires posting to the Linux ARM MSM list, an Asus ZenBook A16 EC driver, and OpenBSD/arm64 USB-keyboard-touchpad support on an HP EliteBook X G2q.

#3
Fixing the Portobello Police Station Clock
⬆ 470 pts 💬 106 HN comments

Portobello Police Station, built from 1877 to house the burgh council's meetings, courts and administration before becoming a library and then a police station, is now community-owned thanks to Action Porty and the Scottish Land Fund — and its three-faced turret clock had stopped being settable by anyone who knew how. The author joined a friend for an afternoon of ladder climbing and attic crawling to find a mechanism plausibly original to the building but retrofitted over decades: small electric motors now drive the time train, and the hour shaft is split three ways, one branch per dial, turning the minute hands directly while a gear on each face derives the hour hand. The key discovery was mechanical — a pawl on one gear could be lifted to free the shaft from the motor, letting the hands be set by hand against unseen counterweights.

The harder problem was the chime. An unmarked, single-layer, hand-assembled control box — containing a PIC 16F628 with a suspected 2001 date code, relays, a power supply and a sealed lead-acid battery — mediates between a mains-powered striker motor, a switch on the striker that counts chimes, and an hour-trigger switch on the clock shaft. It has no real-time clock at all, only an hour counter; the 'advance' button must be held for seconds and released to step the chime forward, and the status LED blinks an unexplained long-long-short-short-short pattern. The team verified 4pm struck four times, then disconnected the chime motor because residents had already objected. The write-up reached 470 points and 106 comments, drawing Morse-code theories, battery-life warnings and analogies to Big Ben and Paris's Panthéon.

The chime controller has no real-time clock at all — just an hour counter driven by a switch on the mechanism, plus a status LED whose 'long-long-short-short-short' pattern one commenter decoded as Morse for 7: "long-long-short-short-short is 7 in morse code."

Key Highlights
  • Community ownership (Action Porty, Scottish Land Fund) turned a derelict 1877 police station into a volunteer restoration project with a settable-but-unknown turret clock.
  • Time-setting was solved mechanically: lifting a pawl disengages the motor so the three-way hour shaft can be turned by hand and verified against hand counterweights.
  • The chime is governed by an undocumented single-layer board with a PIC 16F628 (~2001), relays, a charging circuit and a sealed lead-acid battery of unclear health.
  • The controller tracks only an hour count from a shaft switch, with a hold-to-activate 'advance' button and an undecoded Morse-like status LED.
  • Residents' complaints led the team to disable the chime after successfully verifying the 4pm strike — a local taste problem, not a technical one.

Meta VR Glasses
⬆ 445 pts 💬 394 HN comments

Meta has published a product page for its new VR/AR glasses, a tethered, glasses-form-factor headset that reads as a deliberate answer to Apple's Vision Pro: roughly comparable angular resolution in a far lighter chassis, with the compute offloaded to a puck. The HN thread (445 points, 394 comments) is less a review than a referendum on Meta as a steward of the category. Much of the technical discussion is unusually concrete — angular resolution, field of view, waveguide economics — while the strategic discussion keeps circling the same theme: the hardware appears competent, but the platform owner is the product's biggest liability. Several commenters frame the device as a stopgap until true transparent AR, while a smaller cohort argues that Meta's multi-billion-dollar losses are the only thing keeping low-latency computing alive at all, with ksec arguing a convincing AR/VR experience requires sub-10ms results and would finally force developers to treat latency as a first-class constraint.

The specs surfaced in-thread tell the story of the tradeoffs: micro-OLED panels at 2412 × 2288 per eye, 37 PPD, 6DoF tracking with eye, face and hand tracking, 12GB RAM with 128GB storage expandable to 1TB via microSD on the puck, color passthrough at 26 PPD, and a $1300 price. The flashpoint is a 70° × 66° field of view — narrower than the Quest 3's ~103° × 96° — which jdprgm calls out as stagnation, noting that new headsets 'never seem to just be universally better than whatever they are replacing.' Counterpoint comes from the productivity camp, who want a monitor substitute rather than a VR arcade: modeless notes you can connect a laptop and that 'the FoV should be plenty for productivity,' while others demand a device that is 'dumb, doesn't need software to use it,' echoing the Bigscreen Beyond 2 comparison ($959, 116° diagonal, but base stations required).

At 37 PPD the panel is sharper than Vision Pro, but a 70° × 66° field of view is narrower than the Quest 3's ~103° × 96° — resolution improved while immersion regressed, for $1300.

Making Tailscale Faster
⬆ 193 pts 💬 72 HN comments

Tailscale's engineering post is a granular walk through the data plane of a userspace WireGuard implementation, and it is notably honest about where the wins come from. The headline insight is mundane but consequential: most packets are ~1 KiB, yet wireguard-go provides exactly one 64 KiB buffer to unpack GRO-coalesced reads into, so every small packet was copied into its own oversized allocation. The concrete 5% speed-up was achieved by leaving packets in place. The broader arc — shortening packet queues because measured depth went unused, then reinvesting the freed memory as a multi-queue pipeline where each stream keeps its lane instead of sharing one ordered single-thread stage — is a bandwidth-versus-buffering tradeoff argued from profiling data rather than benchmarks of convenience. writev lets Tailscale describe scattered packet vectors instead of copying them together before handing data to the Linux kernel, and netmap caching lets a device start against a cached network map when the control plane is unreachable.

The rollout cadence is explicit: memory-buffer changes on Linux/Android in v1.104, multi-queue for subnet routers and app connectors afterwards, writev gains partially shipped in spring 2026, netmap caching behind a feature flag today. The most striking number is that tailnets with poor control-plane reachability begin sending through the data plane "one to two orders of magnitude faster" on a warm cache start — a direct answer to hotel Wi-Fi and filtered captive portals, though it demands persistent disk and enough storage churn to make SD-card devices poor candidates. Alexander Valiushko frames multi-queue as "lower latency, essentially faster processing of data from the moment we read it off the wire to the moment we send it to the OS." Against 193 points and 72 comments, the community's response is notably not about the post's numbers at all.

Tailscale's buffer rewrite — keeping small packets in place rather than copying each into its own 64 KiB allocation — yielded only ~5%, but the same campaign lets netmap caching start data-plane traffic "one to two orders of magnitude faster" than a cold start on tailnets with poor control-plane reachability.

Key Highlights
  • wireguard-go's single 64 KiB unpack buffer forced a per-packet copy for typical ~1 KiB packets; Linux/Android now reference offsets inside one large read instead, yielding ~5% throughput and meaningful memory savings.
  • Packet queues were shortened after measurement showed most depth went unused; the reclaimed memory funded multi-queue pipelines where each stream stays on its lane and lanes run parallel across CPU cores.
  • Multi-queue targets subnet routers, app connectors, and exit nodes — the shared bottleneck previously serialized independent streams through one ordered single-thread stage.
  • writev on Linux lets Tailscale pass multiple packet fragments to the kernel as vectors, cutting copies and write syscalls before data reaches the OS.
  • netmap caching stores the tailnet map on disk so devices can negotiate direct connections before reaching the control plane; it is flag-gated now, default in v1.104, and skirts the ~100 ms control-plane round trip on hostile networks.
