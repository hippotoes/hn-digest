FUTO Swipe – A new swipe typing model
⬆ 555 pts 💬 186 HN comments

FUTO Swipe is a new open-source model for swipe typing, developed by FUTO as part of their fully offline Android keyboard. The system uses a three-part architecture: a universal layout- and language-agnostic encoder (635k parameters), a small context-aware language model (1.5M parameters, mostly embeddings), and a layout-specific decoder (304k parameters) that achieves leading accuracy. Together with a beam search of width 300, the model achieves a top-4 fail rate of only ~4% on the test set, and below 1% when ignoring out-of-vocabulary words. The models are extremely lightweight, running in milliseconds even on low-end devices, and were trained on a single workstation GPU, making them environmentally friendly. FUTO also released a dataset of 1 million QWERTY English swipes under MIT license, collected via voluntary contributions on swipe.futo.org.

The inference and beam search are handled by an open-source C++ library (GPLv3), while the models themselves use a separate FUTO Model License that requires visible attribution. The Android keyboard app uses a more restrictive license criticized by some commenters. Despite licensing concerns, the community welcomes the availability of an open, on-device swipe model that matches big tech's accuracy. Users report it's 'as good as Google keyboard' (popcar2) but note issues like random capitalization and lack of context awareness (blopker). The model supports only QWERTY English for now, but the encoder is designed to be layout- and language-agnostic, enabling future expansion. The project also supports voice dictation with local inference, which users find surprisingly good. Overall, FUTO Swipe represents a significant step toward privacy-respecting, high-quality mobile input methods.

The FUTO Swipe model achieves a top-4 fail rate of only ~4% on the test set, and below 1% when ignoring out-of-vocabulary cases, matching big tech keyboards with a total parameter count of under 2.5 million.

Key Highlights
  • FUTO released a family of open swipe typing models and a dataset of 1 million swipes under MIT license.
  • Architecture includes encoder, ContextLM, and decoder, with total active parameters under 1.4 million.
  • Models run on-device in milliseconds; trained on a single workstation GPU for low environmental cost.
  • C++ inference library handles beam search and dictionary-constrained decoding.
  • Currently only QWERTY English, but encoder is designed to be layout- and language-agnostic.

Vulnerability reports are not special anymore
⬆ 292 pts 💬 157 HN comments

In his blog post, Filippo Valsorda, former lead of the Go Security team, argues that vulnerability reports have lost their special status due to the rise of LLMs. He contends that the core value of such reports—providing scarce insight and enabling confidential coordination—has evaporated because LLMs can now find almost any security researcher's level of bugs, and attackers can equally leverage them. The bottleneck has shifted from discovery to triage: maintainers are flooded with AI-generated reports, many real but low-impact, making coordinated disclosure less meaningful. Valsorda suggests that the job now is triage, rapid remediation, and prevention, rather than treating every vulnerability report as a precious gift.

The comments reflect the community's struggle with this new reality. Several maintainers report receiving 2-5 unsolicited reports per week, mostly LLM-generated spam about issues like bad CSS or frivolous findings. Others note that while the volume has surged, genuine critical reports from trusted researchers remain invaluable, though harder to spot. A common sentiment is that this is temporary: once easy bugs are fixed, reports may become special again. There is also a push to focus on exploit development and impact demonstration as the true measure of value, rather than raw bug discovery. The article and comments collectively highlight a paradigm shift in open source security maintenance.

"The insight is not scarce and precious anymore. The bottleneck now is not finding potential issues but assessing which ones are real."

Key Highlights
  • LLMs have democratized vulnerability discovery, making insight and confidentiality no longer scarce.
  • Triage of reports has become the primary bottleneck, with many reports being real but low-impact.
  • There is a significant increase in spam and low-quality vulnerability reports from automated tools.
  • Trust relationships with skilled researchers remain valuable for high-severity or complex issues.
  • The long-term impact may be a shift toward prevention and exploit development as the key security activities.

In memory of the man who put red and green squiggles under words
⬆ 403 pts 💬 65 HN comments

Raymond Chen's tribute to Tony Krueger, the engineer behind the ubiquitous red and green squiggles in Microsoft Word, reveals a profound intersection of usability, technical pragmatism, and serendipity. Krueger reimagined spell checking from a blocking, user-invoked task into a real-time, non‑intrusive visual indicator. Instead of forcing users to wait for a full scan, he introduced asynchronous underlining that drew attention to potential errors without disrupting the writing flow. This design pattern – later adopted by nearly every word processor, code editor, and browser – exemplifies how a subtle change in interaction model can transform a feature from a burden into a seamless assistant. The squiggles were not a product requirement; they emerged from Krueger's own intuition about reducing cognitive friction.

The article underscores the often‑invisible nature of such foundational UX innovations. Krueger's earlier accomplishment – porting Chip's Challenge to Windows via reverse engineering without source code – hints at his technical tenacity. Yet it was the squiggles that touched billions. The HN community's reaction, ranging from genuine appreciation to sharp criticism about color‑blind accessibility and multi‑language nuisance, reflects the feature's dual legacy. Notably, the comments also debate prior art (e.g., Prowrite on Amiga) and lament the lack of software credits. As Weird Al's 'Word Crimes' video and Penn Jillette's enthusiastic endorsement show, the squiggles transcended engineering to become a cultural touchstone. Krueger's work is a masterclass in how a single, well‑crafted interaction can define an era.

“The red and green squiggles!? I love the red and green squiggles!” – Penn Jillette, upon learning Tony Krueger was behind the feature.

Show HN: TikZ Editor – WYSIWYG editor for figures in LaTeX
⬆ 397 pts 💬 72 HN comments

TikZ Editor is a WYSIWYG drag-and-drop editor for creating TikZ figures, a vector graphics language embedded in LaTeX widely used in academic papers. Built by DominikPeters starting in February 2026, the editor leverages AI coding agents (Codex) to handle the heavy lifting of parsing, rendering, and code generation. In total, the project consumed ~700M tokens via API, which would have cost $15k at standard rates but was achieved for ~$500 in ChatGPT subscription fees. The editor supports direct editing of TikZ code, import of existing TikZ diagrams (including from arXiv papers), and exports clean, editable TikZ code—avoiding the 'soup' of absolute coordinates that plagues many visual editors. The web version and .deb package are available, though some Linux users reported odd grid rendering.

The Hacker News discussion (397 points, 72 comments) reveals a mix of enthusiasm and critique. Many users express relief at a visual tool for TikZ, which is notoriously tedious to write manually. Comments praise the editor's potential for STEM students and researchers, and the open-source nature. However, a significant thread criticizes the generated code's overuse of absolute coordinates—a key trade-off in WYSIWYG editors. The author acknowledges this and notes the difficulty in balancing user expectations for intuitive alignment features with clean relative positioning. Comparisons arise with tools like quiver.app, draw.io, and the older Xfig. Some users call for support of Typst's Cetz alternative. The sheer scale of AI-assisted development (700M tokens) also sparks discussion about the economics of vibecoding with frontier models.

Swift Package Index joins Apple
⬆ 209 pts 💬 69 HN comments

The Swift Package Index (SPI), a community-driven package discovery service for Swift, has been acquired by Apple. SPI has been a critical tool in the Swift ecosystem, providing a centralized, searchable index of Swift packages along with compatibility badges and documentation. This acquisition demonstrates Apple's deepening commitment to the Swift language beyond its original iOS/macOS roots, particularly as Swift gains traction on servers and Linux. While the exact terms and integration plans remain undisclosed, the move positions SPI as an official Apple service, potentially leading to tighter integration with Xcode and Swift Playgrounds. However, the transition also raises questions about the future of open governance and multi-platform support that the community built.

Community reaction is sharply divided. Some developers, like Dave Verwer's collaborators, celebrate the success of a community-built tool being recognized by Apple. Others express deep skepticism, pointing to Apple's historically mixed track record with open source—notably restrictions on employee participation in open source projects and a tendency toward proprietary lock-in. A vocal contingent worries that Apple may use SPI to enforce package approval policies, mirroring the App Store model. Meanwhile, a few developers see an opportunity: with SPI now under Apple, the language could receive more robust server-side support, competing with Go and Rust. The 209-point Hacker News thread captures this tension between hope for accelerated development and fear of walled-garden dynamics.

"Not optimistic here. While I'm glad the SPI guys are getting paid, Apple is pretty bad at open source and developer services both, and they explicitly call out developer identity as a future direction, which doesn't fill me with hope." — comment by jshier
