Noise infusion banned from statistical products published by Census Bureau
⬆ 828 pts 💬 516 HN comments

The US Department of Commerce has banned "noise infusion" from all statistical products published by the Census Bureau and the Bureau of Economic Analysis, effectively outlawing differential privacy and other randomization-based disclosure avoidance techniques. This order, which mandates coarsening and suppression as preferred alternatives, threatens to undermine the privacy guarantees of the 2020 Census—the first to adopt differential privacy—and future releases. The ban ignores the fundamental privacy-utility trade-off established in statistical disclosure control literature, where noise addition is the primary lever to prevent record reconstruction attacks while preserving data usefulness. Without noise, adversaries can solve systems of equations from exact statistics to re-identify individuals, as demonstrated by the Census Bureau's own reconstruction attack on 2010 data using swapping.

The article warns that banning noise will force a choice between useless data (via heavy coarsening/suppression) or dangerously unsafe data (exact statistics vulnerable to attack). The author notes that even prior techniques like swapping and sampling inject randomness, and that the order's exclusive reliance on coarsening and suppression is incompatible with modern, high-dimensional statistical products. Commenters on Hacker News widely suspect political motives—namely, enabling gerrymandering by allowing reconstruction of individual records, or weakening data infrastructure to diminish progressive policy research. The controversy echoes historical abuses of census data, such as the compilation of lists for Japanese American internment, and highlights deep distrust in the current administration's ability to safeguard confidential data.

"future statistical releases will either be useless compared to past ones, or they will be incredibly unsafe."

Key Highlights
  • Noise infusion is critical for differential privacy and preventing record reconstruction attacks.

The experience of rendering Arabic typography and its technical debt
⬆ 236 pts 💬 60 HN comments

The article dives into the deep technical debt surrounding Arabic typography on the web, tracing a seemingly simple CSS justification bug to centuries of calligraphic tradition and modern rendering fragmentation. Arabic script is inherently cursive, requiring shaping engines to select one of four positional forms per letter depending on neighbors—a stark contrast to Latin scripts. Justification in classical Arabic relies on elongating letter strokes (kashida) rather than stretching spaces, a technique lost in modern CSS and operating systems. The author highlights how a single legacy ticket—ragged left edge instead of justified—exposes layers of infrastructure failures: from PDF generators lacking shaping engines to Unicode Presentation Forms causing search-index mismatches. The article weaves historical anecdotes, such as the vizier Ibn Muqla's development of proportional script despite mutilation, to contextualize a five-century gap between manuscript fidelity and today's tooling.

With 236 points and 60 comments, the HN community resonated strongly with the article's blend of technical depth and cultural insight. The author details how Arabic fonts are 'small programs' shipping multiple shape variants, and how the web still defaults to Latin-centric assumptions—e.g., text-align:justify stretches spaces, not letters.
