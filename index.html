Explorative modeling: Train on the best of K guesses
⬆ 102 pts 💬 25 HN comments

The article introduces Explorative Modeling (XM), a training-time objective that samples K generations per data point and backprops only through the closest one to the target. This 'best-of-K' trick shifts the loss minimizer from the conditional average of multimodal data to the actual data modes, solving the 'brown blur' problem without factorizing generation into autoregressive steps or diffusion noise-removal chains. Treating exploration as a scaling axis - generative expressivity - lets XMs be combined with existing diffusion and autoregressive models, improving efficiency and generalization. XM keeps sampling as a single forward pass; all extra compute is pushed to training. The central claim is that generation factorization and exploration are interchangeable sources of expressivity, enabling end-to-end training where inference samples identically to training. This is framed as 'Mode Forcing,' where the training objective alone controls what the best prediction is, and K determines how many distinct answers a model can commit to.

Adding exploration to the state-of-the-art RAE recipe reaches the same final performance with 6.2× less data and 4.1× fewer FLOPs, hitting 1.43 FID on ImageNet 256. Gains grow with scale: 13%→23% as models grow, 7%→36% as data grows. For robotics, an end-to-end Explorative Policy matches Diffusion Policy with one forward pass instead of 100, and a world model outperforms Diffuser with 2.3 vs 192 forward passes. Implementation is a literal for loop, which makes reproduction easy but also invites scrutiny: commenters point to prior work in IWAE, GRPO, and minibatch optimal transport, and flag extra training compute and mode-sampling bias as concerns. If the scaling trends hold, this could be an exciting new pretraining axis; if not, it is still a useful efficiency trick. Either way, the community is actively dissecting every part of the claim.

Explorative Models reach RAE's final performance with 6.2× less data and 4.1× fewer FLOPs, and match Diffusion Policy with a single forward pass instead of 100.

Key Highlights
  • Explorative Modeling uses best-of-K sampling during training to move the loss minimizer onto real data rather than their average, avoiding the 'brown blur' failure mode.
  • The method introduces a 'third pretraining axis' - generative expressivity - that can be scaled alongside parameters and data, with gains growing from 13% to 23% as models scale and 7% to 36% as data scales.
  • When added to existing diffusion and autoregressive models, it yields large improvements in data, FLOP, and parameter efficiency across images, video, and masked diffusion language models.
  • Fully end-to-end XMs can match or beat diffusion-based policies and world models with far fewer inference steps (1 vs 100 and 2.3 vs 192 forward passes).
  • Commenters question the novelty, citing related work in IWAE, GRPO, minibatch optimal transport, and discrete distribution networks, and note practical caveats such as K-1 extra forward passes and uniform mode sampling.

#4
AI financial advice is surprisingly good, especially if you ask right questions
⬆ 300 pts 💬 274 HN comments

A new MIT Sloan study by Taha Choukhmane, Weidong Lin, Matthew Akuzawa, and Tim de Silva rigorously evaluates the quality of financial advice generated by large language models. The researchers first built a life-cycle simulation modeling how income, employment, investments, and taxes evolve over decades, establishing an academic benchmark for good financial decisions. They then collected 1,000 prompts written by ordinary adults—not finance professors—and fed them to GPT-5.2, GPT-5.6, and Gemini 3 Flash, simulating what would happen if people from ages 22 to 89 followed the models' spending, saving, and investing advice. The AI consistently pushed users toward higher savings, broad stock-market participation, diversified index-style funds, and age-based de-risking after 45. However, it failed on nuanced scenarios: it recommended overly drastic spending cuts after job loss even when the simulated person had savings, and it let portfolios drift rather than actively rebalancing. A well-structured 'academic' prompt with complete financial information and explicit economic assumptions improved results significantly, but the models still leaned on simple heuristics rather than solving toward the optimal life-cycle policy.

The paper reveals a worrying equity problem: the quality of AI advice depends on the user's demographics and familiarity with the tool. Men, financially literate users, and those with prior LLM experience received advice that generated roughly 5% more retirement wealth. Specifically, women and less literate users wound up with about $50,000 (4%) less at age 60, while AI-novices lost nearly $100,000 (6%). Choukhmane attributes about two-thirds of the gender gap to different prompt content—men mention 'strategy,' 'crypto,' and 'growth'; women mention 'family,' 'grocery,' and 'pay'—and one-third to the model changing advice when the same prompt is labeled as coming from a woman, which could reflect learned bias or rational inferences about differential life expectancy and income risk. The study also finds LLMs are spontaneously recommending providers like Vanguard and iShares (Vanguard in 6% of responses) even when users never mention them, implying AI is quietly becoming the new front door for financial product discovery and challenging traditional marketing, search-based acquisition, and the fiduciary role of human advisors.

AI financial advice encouraged people to save more, invest in diversified stock funds, and reduce risk after age 45—yet women and less financially literate users ended up with roughly $50,000 (4%) less wealth at age 60 simply due to differences in how they phrased prompts.

Key Highlights
  • LLMs consistently recommend higher savings, diversified equity funds, and reduced stock exposure after 45, matching conventional life-cycle finance principles.
  • Structured 'academic' prompts yield materially better advice than casual prompts, but even then AI relies on heuristics instead of true rebalancing or shock adjustment.
  • AI advice varies by user demographics: women, less financially literate users, and AI-novices get lower equity allocations and saving rates, compounding to significant wealth gaps at retirement.
  • AI fails to adjust properly to shocks like unemployment, overreacting with excessive spending cuts even when savings buffers exist.
  • LLMs spontaneously recommend specific financial products and providers (e.g., Vanguard in 6% of responses), suggesting AI will reshape how consumers discover and compare financial products.

How Google helped destroy adoption of RSS feeds (2023)
⬆ 529 pts 💬 182 HN comments

The openrss.org post contends that Google systematically undermined RSS adoption via a textbook 'embrace, extend, extinguish' strategy. Early Chromium had a built-in RSS button that silently vanished; FeedBurner, acquired in 2007, rewrote publishers' feeds into Google-owned, ad-and-tracking-injected private feeds, with third-party APIs killed in 2012 and remaining email services gutted in 2022. The watershed was Google Reader: launched in 2005, it became the default web aggregator for millions, then was shut down in 2013 under claims of declining usage despite internal reports of persistent attempts to kill it. Google Alerts briefly lost RSS delivery, the Chrome RSS extension disappeared and returned as a 'mistake,' and Google News deprecated RSS support in December 2017. Each step severed a major on-ramp to open syndication.

The technical and cultural damage is cumulative: FeedBurner's lock-in made publishers dependent on a closed, opaque feed layer; Reader's death erased the mental model of subscribing to the open web, pushing users toward algorithmic platforms. The article is framed around Google's 2021 promise to reintroduce Chrome RSS support, which remains unfulfilled. In the HN thread (529 points, 182 comments), readers add that Mozilla also removed Firefox Live Bookmarks, while noting RSS still thrives in podcasts, YouTube's per-channel Atom feeds, and self-hosted readers like FreshRSS and NetNewsWire. The deeper lesson: RSS's decline wasn't technical failure—the protocol is free and efficient—but a strategic retreat by gatekeepers whose business models depend on controlling distribution, advertising, and attention. RSS remains a vital public utility, but its mainstream window was deliberately closed.

Google has single-handedly contributed to the reason many users who once relied on RSS feeds have stopped using them.

Key Highlights
  • Early Chromium had a built-in RSS button that was removed without notice or public reason.
  • Google acquired FeedBurner in 2007, shut down its APIs in 2012, and gutted remaining RSS email services in 2022.
  • Google Reader launched in 2005 and was killed in 2013 despite a loyal user base and internal resistance.
  • Google Alerts temporarily dropped RSS delivery and Google News deprecated RSS support entirely in December 2017.
  • A Chrome RSS extension was removed and reinstated as a 'mistake', while a 2021 promise to bring RSS back to Chrome has yet to ship.

#2
Diátaxis
⬆ 353 pts 💬 42 HN comments

Diátaxis is a documentation framework by Daniele Procida that organizes technical documentation into four distinct forms: tutorials (learning-oriented), how-to guides (task-oriented), reference (information-oriented), and explanation (understanding-oriented). It is based on the premise that users have four different needs when consulting documentation, and that each need requires a distinct rhetorical mode, content structure, and navigation architecture. The framework is deliberately lightweight, with no implementation constraints, and aims to guide documentation authors in decisions about content, style, and organization. It has been adopted by Gatsby, Cloudflare, Vonage, and many open-source projects, and is increasingly used as a shared vocabulary for restructuring documentation.

Beyond taxonomy, Diátaxis emphasizes solving practical problems for both readers and maintainers. Its 'compass' and 'workflow' sections help teams map content to the right quadrant, while 'complex hierarchies' addresses deep documentation sets. HN commenters highlight its effectiveness in handover docs and internal knowledge bases, but also note tensions: API docs under 'Reference' can add a click, and authors debate boundaries between tutorials and how-to guides. Some commenters now use Diátaxis as a prompt framework for LLM-generated documentation—'do diataxis' yields decent first-pass docs—and it is often paired with C4 and ADRs as a 'holy trinity' of documentation. Critics argue it has become overhyped relative to other models like Fabrizio's seven-action model.

“It was so clear what you were saying and what 'voice' you were writing in.” — rkangel, on using Diátaxis for a codebase handover.

Key Highlights
  • Diátaxis reduces documentation to four forms: tutorials, how-to guides, reference, and explanation.
  • The framework addresses content, style, and architecture without imposing implementation constraints.
  • Adopted by major projects including Gatsby, Cloudflare, and Vonage; validated by HN practitioners for handovers and support docs.
  • Critics point to API docs being buried under Reference and the blurred line between tutorials and how-to guides.
  • An emergent use case is Diátaxis as a prompt structure for LLM-assisted documentation generation.

Postmortem for Kernel Soundness Bug #14576
⬆ 147 pts 💬 57 HN comments

In late July 2026, the Lean proof assistant community confronted an unusual soundness bug in the kernel. Ramana Kumar published a sorry-free 'disproof' of the Collatz conjecture, generated with AI assistance; it was invalid because it exploited flaw #14576 in the kernel's handling of nested inductive types. Kiran Gopinathan reduced the exploit to a proof of False, and Leonardo de Moura's team pushed fix #14577 within an hour. The root cause: when eliminating a nested occurrence under an inductive type with phantom parameters, those parameters can disappear from the generated auxiliary type, allowing an ill-typed argument to bypass checking and yield False. The bug is only reachable via metaprogramming—the elaborator normally catches it—so it isn't a flaw in Lean's meta-theory. A separate bug in nanoda, an independent Rust checker, meant it too initially failed to catch the exploit, though that bug had been fixed a week earlier.

De Moura emphasized that restricting metaprogramming would be misguided: 'Soundness cannot depend on an untrusted component refusing to build a bad term.' Attackers can write .olean files or modify memory; the kernel must reject ill-typed declarations itself. Mario Carneiro's lean4lean, a formalization of Lean's kernel, is affected as well, though its consistency proof does not yet cover inductive types. The Lean FRO added regression tests to Kernel Arena and a follow-up PR (#14582) that forces parameters of nested occurrences to behave as parameters. With help from OpenAI's Daniel Selsam and a cybersecurity-specialist AI, the team found and fixed several more kernel bugs, all reachable only through metaprogramming and all caught by nanoda (PRs #14607–#14616, plus hardening #14621–#14632). comparator.live now runs nanoda by default. The incident illustrates the layered risk of AI-assisted proofs: a generated proof may target not just one checker, but multiple implementations, and only current, independently maintained checkers can preserve trust.

De Moura: 'Soundness cannot depend on an untrusted component refusing to build a bad term.'

Key Highlights
  • Ramana Kumar's AI-assisted 'disproof' of Collatz was actually an exploit of Lean kernel bug #14576, later reduced to a proof of False.
  • The bug involved phantom parameters in nested inductive types: eliminating a nested occurrence could let parameters escape type checking, allowing False.
  • Fix #14577 was merged within an hour; follow-up PR #14582 strengthens the kernel's parameter checks.
  • nanoda, the independent Rust checker, had its own projection-node bug fixed a week earlier; the proof targeted both old implementations.
  • The Lean FRO, aided by an OpenAI cybersecurity AI, fixed several additional kernel soundness bugs, all caught by current nanoda.

RFC 10015: Deprecating Obsolete Key Exchange Methods in TLS 1.2 and DTLS 1.2
⬆ 59 pts 💬 15 HN comments

RFC 10015, published July 2026, officially deprecates finite-field Diffie-Hellman (FFDH/FFDHE) and RSA key exchanges in TLS 1.2 and DTLS 1.2, and discourages static ECDH cipher suites. The IETF Standards Track document, authored by N. Aviram, updates 17 RFCs and modifies IANA registry entries to mark affected cipher suites as 'D' (deprecated). The rationale is deep: non-ephemeral DH lacks forward secrecy and is vulnerable to Raccoon and invalid-curve attacks; FFDHE suffers from small-subgroup attacks, interop issues with custom groups, and a weak 1024-bit default; RSA key exchange is Bleichenbacher-prone with no domain separation. The document carefully scopes prescriptions to (D)TLS 1.2, since TLS 1.0/1.1 are already deprecated and TLS 1.3 doesn't use these methods.

The RFC distinguishes MUST NOT for FFDH, DHE, and RSA suites, while static ECDH remains SHOULD NOT. It also deprecates client certificate types with fixed DH parameters. This is a pragmatic move: it preserves TLS 1.2 for legacy devices while eliminating the most dangerous algorithms. Hacker News comments reflect this tension. Sha1rholder asks why not just mark TLS 1.2 legacy and move to TLS 1.3, drawing PunchyHamster's retort about bricking devices. onion2k notes 15-year-old network stacks likely lack TLS 1.3, and mjevans says the goal is letting unupdatable devices continue. bawolff encapsulates the RFC's philosophy: 'backwards compatibility is necessary. So long as TLS 1.2 can still be used securely, its really not a big deal.' The community is split between idealist upgrade and real-world compatibility.

A single endpoint that is vulnerable to Bleichenbacher's attack would affect all endpoints sharing the same RSA key.

Key Highlights
  • Deprecates FFDH, FFDHE, and RSA key exchanges in (D)TLS 1.2.
  • Discourages static ECDH cipher suites.
  • Cites Raccoon, Bleichenbacher, invalid-curve, and small-subgroup attacks.
  • Updates 17 RFCs and IANA registry 'Recommended' column to 'D'.
  • TLS 1.3 unaffected; legacy devices can still use TLS 1.2 with secure ciphers.
