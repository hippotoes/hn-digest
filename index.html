#2
Kimi K3.1 Pro model weights are out
⬆ 605 pts 💬 173 HN comments

Moonshot AI has released the full weights of Kimi K3.1 Pro on Hugging Face, a 2.8-trillion-parameter sparse mixture-of-experts model with 104B activated parameters across 93 layers (one dense, 69 KDA plus 24 gated DeepSeek-style sparse-attention layers), 896 routed experts with top-16 routing plus two shared experts, a 160K vocabulary, a 401M-parameter MoonViT-V2 vision encoder, and a 1,048,576-token context window. The headline architectural change versus K3 is the replacement of full-attention Gated MLA layers with Gated DSA, which the model card says yields more efficient long-context modeling — and commenters like blackcat201 immediately flagged as the real story: a post-training-driven change that should collapse KV-cache pressure at long context. Weights are quantized with MXFP4/MXFP8 quantization-aware training, and the download is roughly 1.45TB. Deployment is targeted at vLLM, SGLang and TokenSpeed, with a reasoning_effort field (low/high/max) and an unusual requirement that reasoning_content be passed back verbatim across turns for multi-turn and tool use.

The benchmark table is where the controversy lives. K3.1 Pro leads or ties on Terminal-Bench 4.0 (65.7), SWE Marathon (51.7), HLE-Full with tools (70.0), BrowseComp (92.1), OfficeQA Pro v2 (64.4) and several multimodal-with-Python evals, while trailing Claude Opus 5.5 on GDPval-AA and GPT-6 Astra on Terminal-Bench-Science (38.1 versus 68.1) and ExploitBench (74.7 versus 100). The footnotes are unusually candid: Claude Code harnesses, max reasoning effort, judge models substituted (DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-1017 standing in for GPT-5.6-Terra xhigh), H20 rather than H100 GPUs for PostTrainBench, and pass@k variants for exploit tasks. HN reaction split hard between people who found it genuinely strong in day-to-day use, people who see evidence of test-set contamination, and people who simply cannot run 1.45TB of weights. The licensing compounds the friction: the Kimi K3.1 Pro License reportedly restricts model-as-a-service use above a revenue threshold.

2.8T total parameters with only 104B activated per token and a 1M-token context — but the most quoted number in the thread was the contamination claim that “K3.1 names hidden requirements in 65% of its answers, K3 in 18%.”

Key Highlights
  • 2.8T-parameter MoE with 104B activated parameters, 896 experts, top-16 routing, and a 1,048,576-token context window released as open weights
  • Gated MLA layers replaced by Gated DSA (69 KDA + 24 gated DSA) post-training to cut long-context KV-cache pressure and inference cost
  • Strong coding/agentic results (Terminal-Bench 4.0 65.7, HLE-Full w/ tools 70.0, BrowseComp 92.1) but loses badly to GPT-6 Astra on Terminal-Bench-Science (38.1 vs 68.1) and ExploitBench (74.7 vs 100)
  • Contamination allegations: a cited third-party study claims K3.1 leaks hidden instructions in 65% of answers when asked to list its instructions, versus 18% for K3
  • Serving is enterprise-only territory — ~1.45TB of weights, MXFP4/MXFP8 QAT, vLLM/SGLang/TokenSpeed recipes, and a license restricting model-as-a-service resale above a revenue threshold

#7
Ripple-65B Preview: U.S. Open-Weight SOTA on Agentic Coding
⬆ 325 pts 💬 116 HN comments

Ricursive Intelligence, a full-stack lab pursuing recursive self-improvement via AI-for-chip-design and chip-design-for-AI co-optimization, has released Ripple-65B Preview, a general-purpose variant of the proprietary model powering its internal chip design platform. The headline claim is "U.S. open-weight state-of-the-art" on agentic coding: 80.52 on TerminalBench 2.1, ahead of Reflection Beam 501B (80.1), Meta Muse Spark Open 816B (76.4), Thinking Machines Inkling 975B (63.8), and NVIDIA Nemotron 3 Ultra 550B (56.4). The architecture is the interesting part: a deliberately dense, thin-and-deep Transformer (d_model 5120, 80 layers) rather than the MoE pattern favored by nearly every other lab, tuned for NVIDIA serving including low-bandwidth devices like DGX Spark. Modifications include overprovisioned attention heads, a raised SWA-to-global layer ratio with smaller windows, FFN activation clipping, gated attention, global NoPE, and value residuals. Pretraining ran 12T tokens with up to 1M context at 40% MFU using only simple sharding — no custom kernels, no quantization.

The cost and recipe claims are the substantive contribution. Ricursive says full R&D from scratch — pretraining ladder development, data mixing, synthetic data generation, posttraining recipe work, and the final hero run — totaled $32M, roughly one-tenth the estimated cost of the prior open-weight SOTA. Instead of brute-force RL, they iterated on SFT/RL recipes using truncated importance sampling, score centering, and a linear length penalty over long-horizon synthetic environments, reaching 80.5 in only 1.4M rollouts — about 70x fewer than Reflection Beam. The pretraining ladder, seeded from AI2's Olmo 3, compounded interventions (pre+post norm, sigmoid gated attention, global NoPE, value residuals, an upsampled-code corpus) into a claimed 16x compute-efficiency gain on code at 4B scale. The model is Apache-2.0 on Hugging Face, Together, GMI Cloud, OpenRouter, and Fireworks.

$32M in total R&D and 1.4M RL rollouts — roughly 70x fewer than the prior SOTA — produced a dense 65B model that edges out 500B–975B MoE open-weight models on TerminalBench 2.1, a 0.42-point lead.

Key Highlights
  • Ripple-65B Preview claims 80.52 on TerminalBench 2.1, edging Reflection Beam 501B (80.1) for a 0.42-point 'U.S. open-weight SOTA' lead
  • Deliberately dense 65B architecture (80 layers, d_model 5120) instead of MoE, trained at 40% MFU with no custom kernels or quantization
  • Full R&D cost of $32M, ~1/10th the prior open-weight SOTA, achieved via a 1.4M-rollout RL climb (~70x fewer than Reflection Beam)
  • Olmo 3-derived model ladder with pre+post norm, gated attention, global NoPE, and value residuals yields ~16x compute efficiency on code at 4B scale
  • Released Apache-2.0 on Hugging Face, Together, GMI Cloud, OpenRouter, and Fireworks; internal variant powers Ricursive's chip design platform

Ask HN: What's your current AI agentic setup?
⬆ 465 pts 💬 563 HN comments

The Ask HN thread is less a product survey than a field map of agentic coding practice. Across 563 comments, the recurring architecture is a CLI or desktop harness—Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Pi, Hermes—with models treated as interchangeable backends routed through subscriptions, OpenRouter, DeepInfra, Bedrock, or local inference. The technical center of gravity is operational: context files such as claude.md, skill definitions, quota dispatchers, worktrees, kanban state, and message buses like nova-bus. Commenters describe agents as semi-autonomous processes that need scheduling, review gates, and isolation boundaries. The model matters, but the harness and surrounding workflow increasingly determine whether the setup is usable in production.

Specifics reveal a cost-aware, security-conscious practitioner base. One commenter runs Claude as planner, Codex as reviewer, and DeepSeek as workhorse for under $100; another uses Coder workspaces as disposable VMs with external review before merge. Isolation ranges from Docker sandboxes and LXC to dedicated Unix users and waypipe-isolated GUI apps. Subscription economics are explicit: $20, $100, and $500 tiers, with OpenCode Go and pay-as-you-go APIs as escape valves. Countering the maximalists, several users insist that vanilla Claude Code plus a good context file beats elaborate swarms. The human-agency cohort is equally visible: 'None, no, none, no, no,' one writes, while another insists, 'I am not a meat proxy.'

The thread's most repeated architectural insight is that the harness—not the model—is the durable layer: 'Less is more with these new models and harnesses... Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication.'

Key Highlights
  • Harness commoditization: Claude Code, Codex, OpenCode, Pi, and Hermes are treated as swappable front ends, with models routed via OpenRouter, DeepInfra, Bedrock, or direct APIs.
  • Isolation is a first-class concern: Docker sandboxes, disposable VMs, Coder workspaces, LXC, and dedicated Unix users appear repeatedly as ways to keep agents off personal machines.
  • Cost-aware routing is widespread: users pair $20–$100 subscriptions with pay-as-you-go DeepSeek or GLM workhorses, and some split planning, review, and implementation across different vendors.
  • A strong minimalist counter-trend rejects elaborate swarms: vanilla Claude Code with a curated claude.md and a few skills is reported to handle most work with less overhead.
  • A visible holdout cohort reports zero or near-zero agent use, citing cognitive offloading, account risk, and a preference for manual command execution and test runs.

#4
A thousand fewer paths to root
⬆ 450 pts 💬 329 HN comments

OpenAI's cyber team ran an autonomous discovery-exploitation-patching pipeline against a snapshot of the Linux kernel and reports 948 validated local privilege-escalation (LPE) vulnerabilities, each accompanied by a working exploit and a generated patch, for roughly $1.2M in API credits. The pipeline is staged: an initial agentic sweep produced 2,308 candidate findings after deduplication (that stage alone costing ~$316,549), followed by exploitation/validation and then patch synthesis. OpenAI frames the result as a "thousand fewer paths to root" — a reduction in attack surface rather than a product launch. The kernel is an ideal target precisely because it is enormous, C-based, heavily audited by humans for three decades, and yet still produces a steady stream of LPEs; anything an agent finds there is by definition something the human review pipeline missed or deferred.

The numbers are the story, and the community immediately decomposed them. Within the 10-day scan window, 61 of the reported LPEs had already been patched upstream, 43 had staging patches, and ~299 were under public discussion — suggesting substantial overlap with work already in flight rather than 948 novel revelations. OpenAI withholds full technical detail of the "new exploitation techniques" on safety grounds, which critics note is convenient when patches are already public. The framing paragraph that most commenters fixated on is the recommendation that large codebases need repeated scanning per model generation to "bottom out" latent bugs — read cynically, a business model; read charitably, an admission that vulnerability discovery is now an ongoing process cost rather than a one-time audit. Either way, it recasts kernel security from a fixed backlog into a treadmill synchronized to frontier model releases.

$1.2M in API credits produced 948 validated Linux local privilege-escalation exploits and patches — roughly $1,265 per root path, against a baseline where a single kernel LPE bounty can command far more.

Key Highlights
  • An autonomous agent pipeline found and patched 948 Linux LPEs at ~$1.2M API cost; the candidate-generation stage alone cost ~$316K for 2,308 deduplicated findings
  • 61 LPEs were already patched upstream during the 10-day scan, 43 had staging patches, ~299 under public discussion — implying significant overlap with existing human work
  • OpenAI declined to publish details of the novel exploitation techniques, citing safety, drawing accusations of unfalsifiable claims
  • Commenters argue local privilege escalation has long been treated as near-equivalent to root; the shock is volume, not category
  • Several threads call for structural fixes — Rust/rewrite-in-memory-safe-languages, seL4-style verified kernels, or reduced attack surface — rather than 948 individual patches

Images 2.6
⬆ 365 pts 💬 218 HN comments

OpenAI's Images 2.6 is an incremental but strategically broad update to its GPT Image line, rolling out globally across ChatGPT and Codex on desktop, mobile, and web for all subscription tiers. The headline features—native animated GIF output and SVG generation—are notable less for raw fidelity than for where image generation is being embedded: inside a coding assistant and a general chat product. Commenters note that animation is likely implemented via the old grid/sprite-sheet trick, conditioning each frame on a tiled layout to preserve character consistency, while SVG support is anomalous for diffusion-style raster models and may involve an LLM-assisted raster-to-vector pipeline. The release also exposes gpt-image-2.6-flare and -sunburst variants, though the web UI does not reveal which model or effort level produced an image.

The community's sharpest data point is economic: per-token rates are unchanged, but low- and medium-quality 1024×1024 images consume roughly 3.3–4.2× more tokens than 2.5, turning a $0.006 low-quality image into $0.025 and a $0.013 medium image into $0.043. One commenter calls this 'super deceptive marketing'; another reports 2.5 medium at 439 tokens/$0.014 versus 2.6 low at 824 tokens/$0.025. Quality reports are mixed: 2.6-sunburst is called 'solid,' while 2.6-flare is rolled back for broken or simplistic outputs; demos show extra limbs, splotchy chess GIFs, and stained-glass reflections that ignore light transport. With OpenAI claiming four billion images per week, the effective cost increase and automated waste become the substantive story.

Same per-token price, but low- and medium-quality images consume 3.3–4.2× more tokens than 2.5—a de facto 3–4× price increase hidden behind unchanged rate cards.

Key Highlights
  • Images 2.6 rolls out globally in ChatGPT and Codex across all subscription tiers, adding native animated GIF and SVG generation.
  • Effective pricing rises sharply: unchanged per-token rates mask 3.3–4.2× higher token usage at low and medium quality.
  • Animation appears to use a grid/sprite-sheet frame-conditioning trick, with visible consistency and noise issues.
  • Community reports split by variant: 2.6-sunburst is solid for some, while 2.6-flare regresses versus 2.5-flare.
  • Integration gaps remain: Codex CLI still uses gpt-image-2 per commenters, and the web app hides model/effort selection.

#9
Conductor is joining Meta
⬆ 203 pts 💬 212 HN comments

Conductor launched in July 2025 via a Show HN post and exited to Meta roughly four months later — a lifespan its co-founder described, with unintended precision, as "a lifetime ago, in the distant past of July 2025." The product was a macOS-native orchestration layer for parallel coding agents: multiple agents working in isolated git worktrees, cloud-hosted session VMs with webhooks and a workspace/session API, and a bring-your-own-model architecture rather than a resold inference plan. That last detail matters. As one commenter put it, "the monetization play was to charge on cloud boxes, not inference," which placed Conductor in the thin layer between commodity frontier models and developer workflow. When the intelligence underneath is rented and the interface is reproducible within an afternoon, an acquisition becomes a talent purchase: what transfers is the team's taste and the users' habits, not the codebase.

The wind-down is unusually compressed and unusually explicit: no new sign-ups from Nov 4 (free users frozen, pro users comped pro features), Cloud read-only Nov 13 with refunds, and user data deletion beginning Nov 20 — exportable via right-click "Transfer to local" or Settings → Account → Export data. Local Conductor receives new models and critical bug fixes "until early 2027," a horizon commenters treated as black comedy given the calendar. The thread's loudest signal was commercial rather than emotional: founders of T3 Code, Orca, boxes.dev, superconductor, Paseo, Juggler and aweb.ai all materialized to poach users, prompting the dry observation that "judging by the number of alternatives that are promoted by their founders here there is clearly no moat in this." One team claimed to have forked and reverse-engineered the entire app, Passport feature included, in a single afternoon with LLM assistance.

Nine days before deleting all cloud user data, the changelog was still shipping "Add your own completion sounds in Settings → General" — the perfect epitaph for the agent-harness era.

Key Highlights
  • Conductor, launched on HN in July 2025, is being acquired by Meta and shuts down Conductor Cloud in under three weeks
  • Users get three deadlines: Nov 4 sign-up freeze, Nov 13 Cloud read-only with refunds, Nov 20 data deletion
  • Local macOS app survives on life support with new models and critical bug fixes only until early 2027
  • Export paths exist — right-click 'Transfer to local' or Settings → Account → Export data — and the company reaffirms it never sold or trained on user data
  • The announcement was immediately colonized by founders of competitors (T3 Code, Orca, boxes.dev, superconductor, Paseo, Juggler, aweb.ai) pitching migrations

A new Go API for JSON
⬆ 370 pts 💬 189 HN comments

After a year as an opt-in experiment, Go 1.27 ships encoding/json/v2 and encoding/json/jsontext as stable, generally available packages — the first major rework of Go's most-used serialization surface since before Go 1.0. The redesign starts from a hard architectural split: jsontext handles pure syntax (RFC 8259 "JSON-text": streaming Encoder/Decoder over Values and opaque Tokens, no reflection), while json/v2 layers semantics (marshal/unmarshal, reflection, struct tags) on top. That separation exists because the original package's defects are not bugs to be patched but consequences of its public API — which is why the team concluded they were "practically impossible to fix within the Go 1 compatibility promise," and why MarshalV2-style naming was rejected as a parallel namespace in one package.

The concrete behavior changes are deliberate hardening: invalid UTF-8 and duplicate object member names now error by default (the latter citing CVE-2017-12635, where ambiguous duplicates were exploited), case-sensitive field matching replaces the "surprising" case-insensitive default, nil slices and maps marshal as [] and {}, and Duration serialization requires an explicit opt-in. New MarshalerTo/UnmarshalerFrom interfaces stream through an Encoder/Decoder, eliminating the validate-then-reparse penalty of MarshalJSON/UnmarshalJSON and letting options propagate down the call stack via the Options accessor — the fix for recursive UnmarshalJSON being quadratic, which the Kubernetes kube-openapi project measured as orders-of-magnitude. Benchmarks show Unmarshal up to 10x faster, Marshal roughly at parity. Critically, v1 is now implemented atop v2 using DefaultOptionsV1, so v1 and v2 types interoperate across dependency boundaries.

Unmarshal is up to 10x faster in v2 — and because v1 is now implemented in terms of v2 under the hood, even unmigrated code silently gains MarshalJSONTo support and new struct tags.

Key Highlights
  • Go 1.27 makes encoding/json/v2 and encoding/json/jsontext stable, splitting syntax (jsontext, no reflection) from semantics (json/v2, reflection-based).
  • Defaults are hardened: invalid UTF-8 and duplicate member names now error, field matching is case-sensitive, and nil slices/maps marshal as [] and {}.
  • New MarshalerTo/UnmarshalerFrom streaming interfaces remove the double-parse and allocation costs of MarshalJSON/UnmarshalJSON, with Unmarshal up to 10x faster.
  • Options are now first-class variadic arguments to Marshal/Unmarshal, plus caller-specified Marshalers/Unmarshalers that override type-defined methods.
  • v1 is reimplemented in terms of v2 with DefaultOptionsV1, enabling bidirectional interoperability and incremental, per-behavior migration.

So my ISP was hijacking my DNS
⬆ 126 pts 💬 95 HN comments

A routine homelab debugging session — a Podman container that could not reach an external site — turned into a live demonstration of on-path DNS tampering. The author's first clue was inconsistency: dig against Google, Cloudflare and other public resolvers all returned the same address, but Cloudflare's DoH endpoint returned a different one, and a whois on the first address pointed straight back at his own ISP. The mechanism is unglamorous and decades old: plaintext DNS on UDP/53 is trivially rewritable by a middlebox that can substitute A/AAAA records or sinkhole NXDOMAINs. The post's real value is its teardown of three folk remedies. A self-hosted Unbound recursive resolver still walks root → TLD → authoritative over plaintext UDP unless it is configured with a forward-zone pointing at a DoT-capable upstream. DNSSEC supplies authenticity via signatures, not confidentiality, and only for signed domains with an intact chain. And DoH/DoT simply relocate trust to the resolver operator — encryption in transit, not integrity of the answer.

The quantitative hook is a census figure: per the October 2026 Defaults.Exposed dataset, only 7.03% of graded domains have a valid, fully validating DNSSEC chain, so DNSSEC would catch tampering for roughly one domain in fourteen — and would return SERVFAIL rather than a bogus IP anyway. TLS SNI remains a side channel regardless, which is why the author layers Mullvad via Tailscale exit nodes. His production setup is defensive rather than dogmatic: AdGuard Home for filtering, dnscrypt-proxy speaking DNSCrypt upstream, Cloudflare as a fallback only for containers where DNS failure is unacceptable — deliberately keeping some containers off local DNS so a resolver rebuild during updates cannot cascade. Commenters supply the missing context: a UK ISP operator noting many hijacks are legally mandated for age-gating and censorship, while another commenter identifies the ISP as ACT Fibernet and dryly notes the blocked target was a torrent site. DNS is, in effect, a policy enforcement point.
