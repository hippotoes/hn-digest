How to build a diffusion language model
⬆ 158 pts 💬 19 HN comments

Diffusion language models represent a paradigm shift in text generation, trading the left-to-right token-by-token autoregressive approach for iterative refinement of a full sequence. The article explains how masked diffusion works: the forward process randomly masks a fraction of tokens (with the unmasking probability governed by a schedule α_t), and a bidirectional transformer is trained to reconstruct the original tokens — essentially a generative BERT with a randomized masking rate. This process defines a latent-variable model whose ELBO simplifies to a weighted BERT loss, enabling principled sampling and, importantly, direct perplexity comparison with autoregressive models. The authors show how simple masked diffusion closed much of the perplexity gap on LM1B. However, production-ready systems require extensions: block diffusion for variable-length generation, encoder-decoder architectures to separate representation from denoising, and distillation for faster sampling.

The article traces the evolution from basic MDLM to modern open-weights systems like Mercury 2, Gemma Diffusion, and Nemotron Diffusion. It highlights how block diffusion enables KV caching and flexible-length generation by diffusing chunks conditioned on previous tokens, while iterative refinement provides built-in error correction. The HN comments reflect a polarized field: practitioners like gdiamos report that diffusion Gemma is 'very fast on GPUs in output token/sec' and 'usable as a local model,' while skeptics such as mmastrac and ux266478 warn of catastrophic error propagation — a mis-generated token can 'spread everywhere like a blood infection superhighway' — and weak chain-of-thought abilities. Others debate adoption economics (why don't more labs ship this?) and propose alternative formulations like image-based diffusion with OCR. The article presents diffusion LLMs as a serious, still-maturing alternative to autoregression.

A diffusion LLM starts from a rough, full-length draft and refines every position in parallel — rewriting the whole sequence at each step rather than emitting a single token.

Key Highlights
  • Masked diffusion treats text generation as iterative unmasking: train a bidirectional transformer to fill in randomly masked tokens.
  • The ELBO for masked diffusion is a BERT loss averaged over all masking rates, enabling perplexity comparison with autoregressive models.
  • Block diffusion extends MDLM to variable-length generation with KV caching by diffusing chunks conditioned on previous tokens.
  • Encoder-decoder and distillation techniques address computational cost and sampling speed in production systems.
  • Modern diffusion LLMs (Mercury 2, Gemma Diffusion, Nemotron Diffusion) are competitive, but HN commenters debate error recovery and adoption economics.

“I just chose words carefully”
⬆ 1088 pts 💬 299 HN comments

Marcin Wichary's latest post 'I just chose words carefully' is a masterclass in the hidden aesthetics of monospace text. It opens with the small agonies of typesetting in a fixed-width font: left alignment is fine, right alignment demands counting spaces, centering is impossible without a half-space, and full justification creates rivers of ugly white space. Hyphenation works in proportional type but feels jarring in monospace. The unlikely hero is rs1n, who wrote a 17,000-word guide to Super Metroid in the late 1990s where every single line ends flush against the right margin, achieved purely by word choice. In the FAQ, rs1n writes: 'None. I just chose words carefully so that everything lined up on the right hand side. Everything was done with an ASCII editor.' Wichary frames this as a cousin to physical-book rewrites for widows/orphans and to UI strings massaged into a fixed button width.

Hacker News immediately recognized the practice in their own craft. Laravel's famous three-line comment blocks, where each line is three characters shorter than the previous one, are cited multiple times; a top maintainer fully justifies his commit messages; Project Gutenberg founder Michael S. Hart wrote email updates with equal line lengths. One commenter adds the legend that X-Files writer Chris Carter formatted dialogue to avoid widows, shaping the show's cadence. A separate analytical thread draws an unexpected parallel to LLM watermarking: choosing words to satisfy a verifiable statistical constraint rather than a visible margin. But the aesthetic is contested. Several readers find justified monospace harder to read, with one admitting 'wow that's really impressive and I hate it.' The debate ultimately asks whether arbitrary constraints are a creative engine or a harmful distraction.

"What program did you use to justify the text? None. I just chose words carefully so that everything lined up on the right hand side." — rs1n, Super Metroid guide author

Key Highlights
  • rs1n's 17,000+ word Super Metroid guide is not justified by software; every line is manually word-wrapped in an ASCII editor.
  • The post connects monospace typography hard-mode to UI string fitting, a universal developer pain point.
  • HN users cite Laravel's 3-line descending comments, fully justified commit messages, and Michael Hart's equal-width email updates.
  • A major thread compares the technique to LLM watermarking—choosing words for a statistical pattern rather than visible margin.
  • The community is divided: some see constraints as creative fuel, others as a readability and grammar hazard.
