Apertus – Open Foundation Model for Sovereign AI
⬆ 456 pts 💬 153 HN comments

Apertus is a fully open foundation model developed by the Swiss AI Initiative, a collaboration between EPFL, ETH Zurich, and CSCS. It distinguishes itself by releasing not only model weights but also the full training data, code, methods, and alignment principles, making it one of the most transparent large language models to date. The model targets sovereign AI use cases, particularly for EU entities requiring compliance with the AI Act, and features built-in mechanisms for opt-outs, PII removal, memorization prevention, and multilingual support across 1000+ languages. It was trained on the Alps supercomputer, a cluster of over 10,000 NVIDIA Grace-Hopper GPUs housed at CSCS, and claims competitive performance with top open models at both 8B and 70B parameter scales. The project emphasizes scientific reproducibility and aims to serve as a public infrastructure for AI development, akin to what Linux did for open source software. However, the model's knowledge cutoff is March 2024, and its actual benchmark performance has been questioned by the community, with many noting that earlier versions (V1) lagged behind contemporary closed and open models.

Despite its ambitious goals, Apertus has received mixed reactions from the Hacker News community. Some users appreciate its openness and sovereign AI narrative, particularly in light of growing concerns about US data safety and political interference, such as the recent 'Fable' incident. One commenter noted that 'by far the most impactful product of the Apertus project are the people,' highlighting the team's inexperience relative to other LLM providers. Others have found practical utility in specific domains; for instance, one user reported success using Apertus for RAG systems, while another praised its translation capabilities despite wanting chain-of-thought training. However, significant criticism centers on reliability: a user reported that the model 'hallucinates words that do not exist' for basic translation queries, and several commented that earlier versions were 'sub-par' and 'not very competitive.' Comparisons with other open models like Allen AI's OLMo and Nvidia's Nemotron were common, with some arguing that Apertus's value lies more in its sovereignty pitch than raw performance. The license's handling of personal data and opt-outs also drew scrutiny over enforcement legitimacy.

Competitive with top open models at an equivalent scale of 8B and 70B parameters, trained on 1000+ languages.

Key Highlights
  • Apertus is a fully open foundation model with released weights, data, code, and training methods.
  • Developed by Swiss AI Initiative (EPFL, ETH Zurich, CSCS) on the Alps supercomputer with 10,000+ Grace-Hopper GPUs.
  • Designed for EU AI Act compliance with opt-out, PII removal, and memorization prevention.
  • Multilingual support across 1000+ languages, but users report hallucinations for linguistic queries.
  • Performance questioned: earlier versions lag behind competitors, though some find utility in RAG and translation.

Good results fine tuning a local LLM like Qwen 3:0.6B to categorize questions
⬆ 171 pts 💬 33 HN comments

The article details a practical experiment fine-tuning Qwen 3:0.6B, a 600M parameter local LLM, to categorize household questions into metadata labels (e.g., pool, hvac) for RAG-based chatbot retrieval. The author hypothesized that a tiny model could be fine-tuned into a reliable classifier, reducing vector search space by filtering on category. Baseline prompting achieved only 10% accuracy, with the model inventing categories and overusing broad labels. First fine-tuning attempt using Unsloth and QLoRA raised accuracy to 79%, but with issues like partial category names (e.g., 'ac' for 'hvac'). The key insight came in the second attempt: mapping categories to opaque two-character codes (e.g., AA, KK) to eliminate semantic overlap, which boosted accuracy to 92%. This demonstrates that careful output encoding can significantly improve small models' classification reliability.

The experiment used ~850 training samples split 70/15/15, with 131 held-out integration tests for evaluation. The author noted that fine-tuning parameters mattered less than dataset quality. Remaining failures (11 errors) mostly involved 'water heater' misclassified as 'pool', indicating residual semantic confusion. The HN community largely debated the necessity of LLMs for this task: many suggested simpler alternatives like SGDClassifier on n-grams or BERT-based encoders, with one commenter reporting ModernBERT outperformed Gemma 1b on a binary task. Others proposed constrained decoding to enforce output grammar, a technique the author didn't explore. The article positions fine-tuning tiny LLMs as a middle ground between rule-based systems and larger models, highlighting prompt engineering tricks to overcome architectural limitations.

Accuracy jumped from 10% to 92% by fine-tuning a 600M parameter model and switching to opaque two-character codes.

Key Highlights
  • Baseline Qwen 0.6B with prompting only achieved 10% accuracy on question categorization.
  • First fine-tuning attempt (Unsloth + QLoRA) improved accuracy to 79% but had partial label issues.
  • Second attempt used opaque two-letter codes (e.g., AA, BB) instead of category names, reaching 92% accuracy.
  • Remaining 8% errors were primarily water heater vs. pool confusion due to semantic overlap.
  • Author emphasizes that dataset quality outweighs hyperparameter tuning for small model fine-tuning.

Everything is logarithms
⬆ 252 pts 💬 54 HN comments

In his essay 'Everything Is Logarithms,' Alex Kritchevsky explores the deep structural parallels between logarithms, vectors, dimensions, and other mathematical concepts by introducing the notion of a 'baseless logarithm' — an abstract, unit-free object log N that gains numerical meaning only when expressed relative to a chosen base (e.g., log 2 for bits). This perspective recasts the change-of-base formula as a unit conversion, analogous to treating vectors as coordinate-free geometric entities that become numeric when projected onto basis vectors. The article systematically draws analogies: logarithms behave like vectors in a vector space, with prime factorization acting as a basis and p-adic valuation serving as a projection operator. Kritchevsky also links logarithms to translation operators in differential geometry and to the limit definition of the natural logarithm, framing it as a derivative of x^y at y=0.

The essay extends the analogy to the dimension operator in linear algebra, showing that dim satisfies similar identities to log (e.g., dim(U ⊕ V) = dim U + dim V). The author suggests that these patterns hint at a unifying theory, though he stops short of formalizing it. Commenters on Hacker News engaged with the material from several angles: some recognized the 'baseless log' as a torsor (a mathematical structure of principal homogeneous spaces), while others criticized the terminology as nonsensical or demanded a more rigorous type system. Historical enthusiasts reminisced about log tables and slide rules, and a debate emerged over whether the essay represents valuable pattern matching or merely notational play. Despite the mixed reception, the piece resonated with many engineers and mathematicians who appreciate seeing familiar concepts reframed in a coordinate-free, abstract light.

The 'baseless logarithm' transforms the change-of-base formula into a unit conversion, exactly like expressing a vector in different coordinate systems.

Key Highlights
  • Baseless logarithm as an abstract, unit-free object analogous to a geometric vector.
  • Change-of-base formula reinterpreted as unit conversion, akin to vector coordinate transforms.
  • p-adic valuation and order of vanishing as logarithmic projection operators.
  • Natural logarithm expressed as a derivative limit: ln x = lim_{a→0} (x^a - 1)/a.
  • Dimension in linear algebra satisfies identical algebraic identities as logarithms.

The method, documented in Psychology of Music, starts with two chords and gradually introduces more, with new chords added only after the child achieves 100% accuracy on current ones. The app employs an adaptive weighting algorithm to increase the frequency of harder chords. It is a rewrite of Paul Ganssle's CIM Trainer, rebuilt with modern tooling (Node.js, TypeScript, Android). The developer emphasizes short, frequent practice sessions: five times a day for two to three minutes each.

The chord progression includes nine white-key chords covering intervals like F/C and C/E, followed by five black-key chords. The app tracks accuracy per user profile and uses colored flags for responses. However, the effectiveness is debated. Paul Ganssle, the original author, notes that his son could identify chords perfectly but was worse than chance at identifying single notes, suggesting the method may train chord recognition rather than true absolute pitch. Other commenters question whether perfect pitch is inherently useful; many argue that relative pitch is far more important for musicians, and that absolute pitch can become a hindrance as it shifts with age or tuning standards. Some cite studies suggesting adults can acquire it under certain conditions (e.g., with psilocybin), but consensus remains mixed. The project's open-source nature and clear documentation invite community experimentation and criticism.

Young children can acquire absolute (perfect) pitch — but adults cannot. The window closes around age 6.

PowerFox is a secure, modern, open-source web browser aimed at breathing new life into aging PowerPC and Intel Macs running Mac OS X 10.4 Tiger, 10.5 Leopard, or 10.6 Snow Leopard. Developed by Jazzzny, it is built on the Goanna web engine (a fork of Firefox's Gecko) derived from the Basilisk browser project. PowerFox brings modern security features—TLS 1.3, up-to-date cipher suites, and regular patches—to operating systems long abandoned by Apple. It also supports WebGL, an updated JavaScript engine, color emoji, and NPAPI plugins. The project actively reuses code from the defunct TenFourFox browser, with collaboration from its original maintainer. The goal is to make the modern web accessible on vintage hardware, preserving the nostalgic aesthetics of the early Mac era.

The Hacker News community responded with a mix of nostalgia and technical scrutiny. Comments highlight the joy of the aqua scrollbar ('Ishamr') and memories of Camino/Chimera. Others see PowerFox as a lightweight alternative for thin clients or retro testing, with mentions of using old Macs for dashboards. The maintainer of TenFourFox, classichasclass, advises on JIT porting, indicating community continuity. Practical advice includes using Linux on older hardware as an alternative browser strategy.

Japanese verb conjugation the simple hard way

Dan Abramov's article "Japanese Verb Conjugation the Simple Hard Way" presents an alternative mental model for conjugating Japanese verbs, aimed at programmers and logical thinkers. Instead of the traditional approach that categorizes verbs into -ru and -u groups with seemingly arbitrary suffix rules, Abramov introduces a stem-based system where the verb's stem may contain a wildcard vowel (e.g., nom*) that interacts with suffixes containing a secret vowel. This system reduces conjugation to concatenation with a single rule: if the stem ends with a wildcard, the suffix's secret vowel is revealed; otherwise, it is discarded. The article emphasizes that this approach is not meant to be a complete pedagogical method but a conceptual framework that makes the underlying phonetics and structure clearer, especially for those who struggle with traditional rote memorization. He also addresses the pros and cons of using romaji, noting that while it aids phonetic intuition, it can mislead due to irregularities like shi and chi that arise from the hiragana syllabary.

The Hacker News discussion reveals a polarized community. Many experienced Japanese learners and speakers criticize the article for overcomplicating a topic that is better learned through exposure and pattern recognition, with some calling it a "beginner's headcanon" that could hinder progress. Supporters appreciate the systematic explanation and see it as a useful bridge for those with engineering backgrounds, noting that it helped demystify conjugation for them. A recurring theme is the debate over romaji: some argue it's a crutch that delays learning kana, while others find it useful for initial phonetic understanding. The comment thread also surfaces alternative resources like Cure Dolly's videos and the 80/20 Japanese book, and highlights the broader challenge of language learning communities being filled with optimization theorycrafting rather than practical advice. Overall, the response underscores the difficulty of teaching a complex linguistic system to an audience that seeks a simple, rule-based explanation.

Despite the article's title claiming a 'simple hard way,' the Hacker News community is sharply divided: some call it a helpful conceptual framework, while fluent speakers dismiss it as an 'overwrought headcanon' that obscures the natural patterns of the language.
