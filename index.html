GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra produces proof of the Cycle Double Cover Conjecture [pdf]
⬆ 450 pts 💬 363 HN comments

In a landmark demonstration of AI's expanding capability in formal reasoning, OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol Ultra has produced a proof of the Cycle Double Cover (CDC) conjecture—a 50-year-old open problem in graph theory. The proof, released as a PDF, is remarkably concise (fewer than 10 pages) and exploits clever combinatorial constructions, including parallel-edge 2-cycles and multiset counting, to show that every bridgeless graph admits a 2-cover of even subgraphs. The model was prompted with extensive instructions to reject status reports and vague optimism, and to persist for at least eight hours; it completed the proof in under one hour. The prompt itself, released alongside, reveals that only 1/5 of the text described the problem, with the remainder devoted to cajoling the model into sustained deep reasoning—highlighting current limitations in autonomous goal-keeping and time awareness.

The proof has not yet been formally verified, though early analyses by mathematicians and even the model itself (when asked) deem it sound. The HN comments reflect a mix of awe and critical reflection: users note the survivorship bias (many failed attempts likely precede this success), the heavy prompt engineering required, and the irony that the model's own verification is circular. The result also rekindles debates about AI's role in mathematics—whether it can truly build new theories or merely manipulate existing syntax—and the displacement of human mathematicians. Notably, the proof's elegance and conciseness suggest a genuinely new insight, not just a brute-force search. This development positions AI as a powerful tool for exploration, even as it underscores the continued need for human oversight and interpretation.

QuadRF can spot drones and see WiFi through my wall
⬆ 578 pts 💬 192 HN comments

The QuadRF, a compact phased-array radio built around a Raspberry Pi 5 and an FPGA, represents a significant leap in accessible software-defined radio (SDR) technology. Developed by Martin McCormick (formerly of SpaceX's Dishy team), it leverages the Pi's MIPI lanes to stream I/Q data at over 5 Gbps, enabling low-latency, high-bandwidth signal processing. Operating in the 4.9–6 GHz C-band, it can visualize WiFi networks through walls and track drones in flight using an augmented reality overlay. The device is priced at $499 for a basic kit on Crowd Supply, and its open-source nature invites customization. Despite a clunky UI, early testing by Jeff Geerling confirmed impressive capabilities, such as identifying individual WiFi channels and tracing drone movements.

The QuadRF's use of MIPI for RF streaming is a novel approach that frees up the PCIe slot and offers a simpler alternative to USB, though it required reverse-engineering the Raspberry Pi 5's RP1 chip. The device can be daisy-chained for larger arrays, aiming for up to 1.15 MW EIRP in moon-scale experiments. However, the narrow frequency range (4.9–6 GHz) limits its immediate utility for sub-GHz or 2.4 GHz bands. The crowdfunding campaign has exceeded expectations, with plans for injection-molded enclosures. The article highlights the democratization of SDR but also warns that governments have long possessed such tools. The open-source community's ability to replicate this tech raises both excitement and privacy concerns, echoing themes of accessible surveillance.

"If the open source community can come up with something like this, just imagine what governments are capable of." — Jeff Geerling

#7
Einstein's relativity rules chemical bonds in heavy elements, new research shows
⬆ 239 pts 💬 84 HN comments

Brown University chemists have provided the first direct experimental evidence that Einstein's theory of relativity fundamentally alters the nature of triple chemical bonds in heavy elements, challenging textbook models that have stood for decades. The study, published in Science, used photoelectron spectroscopy on bismuth-carbon molecules cooled near absolute zero to show that the traditional separation between sigma and pi bonds breaks down under relativistic effects. In heavy elements like bismuth, electrons orbit at a significant fraction of the speed of light, causing spin-orbit coupling that hybridizes bond types—creating a smeared structure that is neither pure sigma nor pure pi. This directly validates predictions from Dirac's relativistic quantum mechanics and represents a milestone in experimental chemistry.

Led by Professor Lai-Sheng Wang and Ph.D. students Deniz Kahraman and Jie Hui, the team observed that the carbon-bismuth triple bond actually consists of one pi bond and two hybrid sigma-pi bonds, rather than the textbook sigma+2pi model. Wang noted, 'The boundary between a sigma bond and a pi bond is now sort of smeared.' The research has immediate implications for bismuth's growing role in next-generation solar cells and quantum materials, where understanding relativistic bonding is crucial. It also underscores how relativity governs chemistry at the bottom of the periodic table, a realm where many industrial processes (e.g., lead-acid batteries) already rely on such effects without full theoretical understanding.

Direct spectroscopic evidence shows that in heavy elements like bismuth, the textbook triple bond structure of one sigma and two pi bonds collapses into hybrid relativistic bonds due to spin-orbit coupling.

Key Highlights
  • First direct experimental evidence of relativistic bond hybridization in heavy elements.
  • Challenges textbook model of sigma/pi bond separation for elements beyond carbon.
  • Uses photoelectron spectroscopy on bismuth-carbon molecules at near absolute zero.
  • Validates Dirac's relativistic quantum mechanics for chemical bonding.
