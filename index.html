#1
Microsoft's ONNX Runtime has disappeared from GitHub
⬆ 429 pts 💬 90 HN comments

Microsoft's ONNX Runtime — the de facto cross-framework inference engine that sits beneath a large fraction of production ML: Hugging Face serving, Windows ML, Azure endpoints, TensorRT/OpenVINO/DirectML execution providers, and countless PyTorch-to-ONNX export pipelines — returned a bare HTTP 404 from its github.com/microsoft/onnxruntime home, a repository with roughly 22,000 stars and no advance notice, no deprecation banner, and no archival redirect. The disappearance coincided with the equally abrupt vanishing of microsoft/agent-framework, itself freshly tied to a reported PyPI supply-chain compromise, which converted a routine repository move into something closer to an infrastructure incident. Critically, the distribution surface did not go dark with the source: onnxruntime.ai remained reachable and the wheels and JARs persisted on PyPI and Maven Central, a reminder that modern package ecosystems decouple artifact availability from source provenance — meaning builds and CI pipelines keep working while auditability, issue history, and patch review silently disappear.

The comment thread is dominated by the epistemics of an information vacuum. magnio invoked Occam's razor: "Without any official communications... we can assume at least one admin account in Microsoft GitHub org got compromised," a claim CodeCompost rebutted by pointing to Microsoft Ignite and routine "consolidating repos." nvader supplied the concrete failure mode — "evals started failing in our pipelines this morning" — while spdustin and vrighter surfaced Phrack issue 73's backdoor-attacks-against-ML-models paper, whose disclosure timeline (bug found 2026-03-07, patches merged in May, shipped in v1.27.0 on 2026-06-12) coffee_am noted does not line up with a November takedown. Practitioners immediately rerouted to intel/onnxruntime with OpenVINO patches, a syntax-syndicate mirror validated against web.archive.org commit hashes, and a SourceForge tarball mirror, while others weighed Tract, RTen, Burn/burn-onnx, WONNX and GoMLX.

With 22,000 stars and no official statement, a 404 is the loudest possible announcement that source availability is not a guarantee — and neither is the fork you just made.

"Does anyone know why or how a repository with 22k stars just went dark?" — nvader, reporting failed evaluation pipelines, while Microsoft stayed silent and onnxruntime.ai stayed up.

Key Highlights
  • microsoft/onnxruntime (≈22k stars) returns 404 with no notice, deprecation banner, or redirect; microsoft/agent-framework vanished in the same window
  • PyPI wheels, Maven Central artifacts and onnxruntime.ai remained live, so downstream builds kept working while issue history and patch review became unauditable
  • Leading theory is GitHub org compromise, reinforced by a reported PyPI supply-chain compromise in microsoft/agent-framework
  • Counter-theory: Microsoft Ignite coincided with the takedown and the org may simply be consolidating repositories
  • Community fallbacks emerged fast: intel/onnxruntime (OpenVINO fork), a syntax-syndicate mirror cross-checked against Wayback commit hashes, and a SourceForge mirror

#2
Harvard researchers find AI coding agents shifted the bottleneck to code review
⬆ 297 pts 💬 382 HN comments

A Harvard job-market paper by Ph.D. candidates Fiona Chen and James Stratton puts hard numbers on what most working engineers already suspect: AI coding agents accelerate drafting but relocate the constraint downstream. Using a proprietary Jellyfish engineering-analytics dataset joined to Revelio Labs employment records — 445 million work events, 68.3 million Jira issues, 200.9 million commits, 49.1 million pull requests across 718 firms — the authors run a staggered difference-in-differences comparison of adopters against not-yet-adopters from January 2021 to March 2026. The identification argument rests on adoption timing being driven by when firms learned about the tools and how long procurement, legal, and security approvals took — one firm approved in a day, another reported six to twelve months. That is a defensible but not airtight exclusion restriction, and the paper concedes larger firms adopted earlier (controlled via firm size) while arguing any residual bias would flatter AI, making the flat output result generous. It is an unreviewed working paper with a data partner that sells 'AI Impact' analytics, a caveat worth carrying throughout.

The measured asymmetry is stark. Lines of code added per worker-month rose 1,494 against a 5,017 baseline (~30%), commits 20%, pull requests 23% — yet resolved-and-deployed Jira issues moved just 0.12 on a 3.67 baseline with a standard error of 0.17, a confidence interval ruling out anything above ~12%. The shortfall surfaces in review: pull-request submission-to-merge time climbed 3.45 days on a 7.03-day baseline (+49%), formal change requests jumped ~12 percentage points from a 13% base, review comments per PR rose 35%, and the share of workers doing any review rose 14% from 29%. Crucially, AI review tooling did not clear it: nearly 80% of firms had adopted some by March 2026, yet AI produced only 23.3% of review comments and touched just 10.8% of PRs. The authors' two-channel model distinguishes a fixed-reviewer-pool volume problem (fixable by shifting labor) from a draft-quality problem (fixable only by better models) — a distinction with direct budget consequences. An NBER paper by Demirer, Musolff and Yang finds a congruent 240%-to-30% commit-to-release falloff across 500,000+ GitHub developers.

Engineers wrote ~30% more code, but time from pull request to merge rose 49% — from 7.03 to 10.48 days — while resolved-and-deployed Jira issues stayed statistically indistinguishable from zero (+0.12, SE 0.17), and AI review tools generating only 23.3% of review comments failed to clear the queue.

Key Highlights
  • Output gains from agents are entirely upstream: +30% lines of code, +20% commits, +23% PRs, but +0.12 resolved Jira issues per worker-month (SE 0.17) — statistically zero against a 3.67 baseline.
  • PR submission-to-merge time rose 3.45 days on a 7.03-day baseline (+49%), formal change requests nearly doubled (13% → ~25%), and review comments per PR rose 35%.
  • AI code-review tools did not relieve the bottleneck: ~80% of firms used one by March 2026, yet AI authored only 23.3% of review comments and only 10.8% of PRs received even one.
  • The paper's two-channel model separates a fixed-reviewer-pool volume problem (labor reallocation helps) from a draft-quality problem (only better models help) — buyers should know which dominates.
  • Provenance is contested: unreviewed working paper, Jellyfish (an 'AI Impact Platform') supplied the data and previously published a rosier 100k-engineer version claiming code quality wasn't suffering.

Archive Team is archiving the Apple Support Community
⬆ 253 pts 💬 101 HN comments

ArchiveTeam is racing to preserve the Apple Support Community before Apple permanently closes it on November 20, 2026, following a November 10 announcement that the site would stop accepting new posts and become read-only. The project is technically interesting because it uses ArchiveTeam’s Distributed Preservation of Service model: volunteers run Warrior containers or VMs that pull work units from a central tracker and scrape the forum’s multilingual regional domains. This is not mere nostalgia. The community is a large long-tail corpus of device-specific troubleshooting, version regressions, obscure workarounds, and negative evidence—material that search engines and, increasingly, LLMs have consumed. A centralized shutdown converts a queryable public archive into dead links, so the race is against both a hard deadline and link rot.

The Hacker News thread exposes a sharp tension between preservation ethics and content quality. ArchiveTeam’s tracker reportedly showed 2.68 million items remaining with three days to go, and commenters urged others to fire up a Warrior. Yet the dominant reaction was scorn: the forum was “horrible,” full of “100s of threads with nonanswers,” dominated by “unpaid community leaders” copy-pasting templates, and routinely answered with “reset the PRAM and SMC.” Defenders counter that deciding what is junk is subjective, archiving everything is cheaper than curation, and even unanswered threads prove a problem is not isolated. Others argue AI now provides better one-shot fixes, while a further cohort insists Apple itself should have archived the corpus rather than leaving volunteers to preserve corporate knowledge.

ArchiveTeam’s tracker showed 2.68 million items still queued with only three days left—preservation at the scale of a distributed race against a hard corporate shutdown.

Key Highlights
  • Apple announced the Apple Support Community would close permanently on November 20, 2026, after becoming read-only on November 10.
  • ArchiveTeam is using its DPoS/Warrior infrastructure to scrape the main forum and multiple language-specific Apple community sites.
  • The project tracker had roughly 2.68 million items remaining with three days left, prompting calls for volunteer compute.
  • HN commenters overwhelmingly described the forum as low-quality, full of template answers, misinformation, “me too” replies, and nonanswers.
  • The debate centers on whether noisy corpora are worth preserving wholesale, whether Apple had a duty to archive, and whether AI has made forums obsolete.
