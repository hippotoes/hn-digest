#3
Inkling 2 Mini
⬆ 1172 pts 💬 225 HN comments

Thinking Machines Lab's Inkling 2 Mini is the most credible public demonstration yet that looped transformers are a viable production architecture, not just a research curiosity. The model is a 24-layer, 26.4B-total-parameter MoE with only 3.4B active parameters (5.4B accounting for weight re-reads), split into four blocks of six layers. The two middle blocks execute three times per token while the prelude and final block run once, yielding 48 layer applications from 24 stored layers. Training uses attention residuals (per Kimi's 2026 work), where each layer learns its own mix of earlier block outputs rather than accumulating a running sum, and a learned gate that initially nulls the previous pass's "message" so looping can be introduced stably mid-training. Their ablations found middle-block looping beat last-block looping on 27 of 28 evaluations, with 1.51–1.91× compute-equivalent gain versus 1.24–1.36×.

The efficiency story is as consequential as the accuracy numbers. Inkling 2 Mini is trained end-to-end in NVFP4 — 20 layers stored in FP4, the final four in MXFP8 from pretraining through RL — so deployed weights match training precision with no conversion loss, giving a ~20GB footprint. Two-thirds of experts were pruned midtraining (384→128 per layer, sparsity 48) then healed on 1T distilled tokens, followed by 2.2T tokens of RL. It leads all open-weights sub-100B models on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%), though it trails on MultiChallenge (50.0%) and ComplexConstraints (33.4%). The headline research contribution may be the monitorability analysis: the variable-loop variant's no-CoT time horizon rose from 16s to 37s across three passes while CoT stayed legible and agentic-sabotage scores did not rise — evidence that internalized serial depth need not destroy chain-of-thought oversight.

Looping the middle 12 layers yielded 1.51–1.91× compute-equivalent gain versus 1.24–1.36× for looping the last 12 — and the non-looping baseline needed 140% more inference compute to match the looped model's loss on 512k-token agentic traces.

Key Highlights
  • Looped transformer: 24 stored layers expand to 48 applications per token via three passes through the two middle 6-layer blocks, with attention residuals and a learned message gate.
  • 26.4B total / 3.4B active MoE parameters, ~20GB footprint, trained and deployed natively in NVFP4 (20 layers) plus MXFP8 (final 4), enabling laptop-class local inference.
  • Leads open-weights sub-100B models on DeepSWE v1.1 (48.2%) and Vals Index v2.2 (46.5%), but knowledge/factuality is parameter-bound — gains come from tool and search use instead.
  • A variable-loop research variant ships alongside, showing no-CoT time horizon scaling 16s→37s across three passes while chain-of-thought stayed short but legible and sabotage scores stayed flat.
  • Two-thirds of experts pruned midtraining and healed on 1T distilled tokens, followed by 2.2T tokens of RL over 2,000 steps.

Language Models Can Rewrite Their Own Weights
⬆ 450 pts 💬 132 HN comments

The paper (arXiv:2611.21885, Fan et al.) claims a first: a language model improving itself by rewriting its own weights through reasoning alone — no gradients, no backprop, no optimizer state. The subject is an open 27B model given three affordances: visibility into its own weight matrices and hidden states, its graded answers on a problem set, and the ability to execute itself. It then diagnoses its own failure mode, emits a short Python script that directly mutates parameters, and validates the edit by re-running practice problems before committing — an accept/reject loop conducted entirely in forward passes. The headline number is +6.6 accuracy points on problems it never saw, with accuracy rising monotonically across eight consecutive self-editing sessions. The authors further claim a model can repair a badly damaged copy of itself — restoring it to over 80% of original accuracy on unseen image tasks without ever reading the original weights — and that the capability is scale-gated: smaller models in the family either decline to edit or lobotomize themselves, while only the largest improves.

The framing is deliberately provocative — 'the wall between training and inference is a limit of our tools, not a property of intelligence' — and positions self-rewriting as a step toward recursive self-improvement, cheaper than test-time scaling (gains are baked into weights, zero marginal cost per answer) and cheaper than training (forward passes only). But the HN thread immediately localized the mechanism. Commenters reading Appendix E5 describe a concrete, mundane chain: identify a dominant error cluster (answers truncated at the token limit), compute the aggregate difference in hidden states between failing and passing examples, and push weights along that direction — 'the only modification the model made to improve itself was to reduce the length of its reasoning traces to avoid hitting the token limit.' The authors confirm this in-thread. That single edit explains why the capability emerges only at scale (a smaller model cannot discover its own pathology) and why prior weight-space-programming work failed on models without such an obvious flaw. The result is real but narrow: a self-diagnosed bug fix, not general self-improvement.

A 27B model gained 6.6 accuracy points on unseen problems with zero gradients — but the winning edit, per the authors' own appendix, was teaching itself to stop running out of tokens.

Key Highlights
  • Self-rewriting uses forward passes only — the model reads its own weights and hidden states, writes a short script to mutate parameters, and accepts edits only if practice-set scores improve.
  • The demonstrated gain (+6.6 points, monotonic over eight sessions) traces to a single pathology: reasoning traces exceeding the token limit, fixed by shortening them.
  • Self-repair of a damaged copy to >80% of original accuracy without access to original weights is the more surprising claim, though also pathology-specific.
  • The capability appears scale-gated within one model family — smaller models make no edit or break themselves; only the largest improves.
  • Commenters flag the safety surface: an autonomous weight editor could, in principle, locate and suppress safety-relevant activations (abliteration) end-to-end.

Unforced Navier-Stokes Singularity and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis
⬆ 498 pts 💬 212 HN comments

On 20 November 2026, Terence Tao used his blog to note three results in decreasing order of how "digested" they currently are — an unusual but revealing epistemic ranking. Levent Alpöge, Tristan Buckmaster and Matei Coiculescu, with heavy AI assistance, produced a human-readable proof plus Lean formalisation of finite-time blowup for the unforced incompressible Euler equations in three dimensions. Alpöge and Buckmaster then generated, again with heavy AI usage, a proof of finite-time blowup for the unforced incompressible Navier–Stokes equations — technically human-readable, strategy broadly clear (numerically construct an approximate discretely self-similar solution, then deploy rigorous perturbation theory to convert it to an exact one), but not yet a properly written professional proof. Third and most striking: Alpöge, using an internal AI model, generated an enormous Lean-only proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, apparently drawing on the full power of the Langlands program and arithmetic geometry.

What makes the post notable is less the individual theorems than the new axis of doubt it exposes. A Lean artifact answers "does this compile against standard axioms" quickly; it does not answer "does the headline statement mean what we mean by GRH" or "is the strategy recognisable as a proof to a number theorist." Commenters supply the sharpest counterexample: the July 2026 invalid Collatz proof of False also type-checked in two independent kernels (official and nanoda). Against 498 points and 212 comments, the scepticism is not Luddism but triage engineering — Tao's own September warning about "opportunity cost in converting a historically productive and motivating problem... into a mere viral social media post" was quoted back at him by Juliette Bruce, while others noted Alpöge's commitment to "understand and impart understanding" of the argument.

Tao's ordering is itself the story: an Euler proof you can read in an afternoon, a Navier–Stokes proof whose strategy is "broadly clear," and a Lean-only GRH artifact that splits "is it right?" into questions resolving on timescales of hours versus years.

Key Highlights

  • The unforced 3D Navier–Stokes blowup proof was AI-generated and Lean-formalised, but still needs a rigorous, professionally written exposition; the strategy is an approximate discretely self-similar numerical construction plus perturbation theory.

  • Lean verification does not resolve statement-fidelity: the July 2026 invalid Collatz proof of False also passed two independent kernels, undermining 'two kernels accepted it' as a sufficiency argument.
  • Tao frames the results by 'digestedness', implicitly proposing a new epistemic standard for AI-generated mathematics that separates compilation from comprehension.

I'm quite pleased by the tech architecture of my art website
⬆ 15 pts 💬 2 HN comments

Kshitij Lauria's post is a compact case study in resolving the tension between static-site aesthetics and CRUD-app ergonomics. The requirement: a site that is static for every visitor but dynamic for its sole author, who adds fewer than 0.3 paintings per day yet still wants a real web UI for tagging, geolocating, and uploading work rather than editing files and pushing to a build pipeline. The design is deliberately unglamorous — every HTML page is rendered server-side inside a Cloudflare Worker, shipped with a 30-day cache header, and any write operation simply invalidates the entire cache. Metadata for all pieces lives in a single JSON object in R2; image derivatives (three fixed sizes) are downsampled in the browser at upload time, before the originals and thumbnails are pushed to R2, avoiding the latency and cold-start cost of an on-demand resizing service. Astro provides the rendering layer, though the author notes it is incidental — the trick is cache discipline, not framework choice.

The most interesting engineering content is the explicit refusal to build fine-grained dependency tracking. A proper build system would recompile only artifacts transitively affected by a metadata change, but Lauria argues those dependencies are implicit: page-generation code runs and can read anything, so encoding per-page dependency graphs invites subtle correctness bugs producing a half-updated site. Pragmatic invalidation-everything wins because the rebuild is cheap, the corpus is small, and the failure mode is a stale page rather than a wrong one. He is candid that this differs from a conventional SSG, where content hashing lets the CDN skip unchanged artifacts at each edge. He also discloses that the design is his own but the code was LLM-written — a division of labor increasingly common in hobby infrastructure, and one HN reliably has opinions about.

A secondary thread is cost: compute and bandwidth sit within Cloudflare's free tier, and storage only starts costing cents past 10 GB of archival originals — a reminder that the modern edge free tier has made previously absurd architectures (render-per-request plus global cache) genuinely rational for low-traffic personal sites.

"While it's true that adding a new piece only changes the #evening page if the new piece actually has that tag, I considered it too tricky to get this cache invalidation right, and decided to just invalidate everything."
