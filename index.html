Fable 5 vs. GPT-5.6 Sol on an NP-Hard Problem: Does /goal help?
⬆ 240 pts 💬 116 HN comments

The article evaluates two leading AI coding agents—Anthropic's Claude Fable 5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.6 Sol—on an unpublished NP-hard optimization problem called KIRO, a fiber-network design task with a known human baseline. The core question is whether the '/goal' mode, available in both Claude Code and Codex, improves performance by forcing the model to persist until an explicit objective is met. Results reveal that Fable 5 is substantially stronger, producing the best overall solution (31,934 cable length units) with remarkable consistency across runs, while Sol's performance varied widely. However, '/goal' proved to be a double-edged sword: although it helped in 4 out of 6 trials by sustaining productive solver strategies, it occasionally locked the model into suboptimal approaches, causing large regressions. The author concludes that '/goal' can win most runs while making average performance worse—a cautionary tale for relying on persistence features without understanding their interaction with the problem's search landscape.

The deep dive into '/goal' implementations highlights key architectural differences: Claude Code uses a separate lightweight evaluator (Haiku) that inspects the transcript for completion evidence, while Codex persists the goal as thread state and provides the working model with tools to declare completion. This makes Claude's evaluator 'blind' to file state, whereas Codex's model grades its own progress—a potential source of bias. The benchmark also emphasizes that raw model intelligence, not the control loop, was the decisive factor: Fable plain mode beat Sol plain by 1,875 points on average, and Fable's plain runs were tightly clustered (319-point range) compared to Sol's 1,958-point spread. Community comments reflect divided opinions: some praise '/goal' as a replacement for plan mode, others caution that it can amplify bad initial decisions, and several users report that context compaction undermines long sessions. One memorable comment notes that 'the quality of the loop matters less than the quality of what the loop keeps doing.'

Fable 5's plain mode achieved a narrow 319-point spread across three runs, while GPT-5.6 Sol's plain results varied by nearly 2,000 points—demonstrating that raw model stability, not persistence features, determined success on this NP-hard benchmark.

Key Highlights
  • Fable 5 outperformed GPT-5.6 Sol overall, with a plain mean 1,875 points better and narrower variance.
  • /goal mode won 4 of 6 trials but made mean performance worse due to occasional large regressions.
  • Claude Code's /goal uses an independent evaluator (Haiku) that only sees the transcript; Codex's /goal uses persisted state with self-evaluation tools.
  • The KIRO problem is a fiber-network design task with a 10^1223 lower-bound search space, far beyond exhaustive search.
  • The author warns against treating /goal as a generic 'try harder' switch without understanding its effect on the optimization loop.

GPT-5.6 used a prompt to close a 30-year gap in convex optimization
⬆ 562 pts 💬 359 HN comments

GPT-5.6 (via OpenAI's Sol Pro) has reportedly closed a 30-year gap in convex optimization by proving a lower bound on the minimal number of subgradient evaluations required to optimize Lipschitz, convex functions over a spherical domain—a core problem in optimization theory. The model, operating under a 10-page expert-crafted prompt, produced a Lean-verified proof in 148 minutes. The result advances understanding of information-theoretic limits in first-order optimization, where the known lower bound of O(d) (Nemirovsky & Yudin, 1979) left a gap for higher-order smoothness assumptions. The proof leverages techniques from convex geometry and information theory, synthesizing decades of partial results into a coherent argument. The author, Phillip Kerger, notes that the prompt encoded a year of prior failed attempts with weaker models, effectively framing the problem within a structured search space that GPT-5.6 could navigate.

The breakthrough is emblematic of a new paradigm: AI acting as a highly capable research assistant that can integrate and extend existing methodologies when given precise, domain-specific scaffolding. Kerger's approach—feeding all previous failed strategies and explicit constraints—mirrors how a human mentor might guide a promising student. The Lean formalization ensures the proof's correctness, sidestepping the hallucination risk that plagues less structured outputs. Community reaction is polarized: some hail it as a genuine contribution (the problem is more niche than OpenAI's CDC, but still significant), while others emphasize the human effort behind the prompt, arguing the model merely mechanized a well-defined search. This case underscores that AI's research utility scales with the quality of human input, and raises questions about the future of low-hanging fruit in mathematics—now potentially automated.

148 minutes to close a 30-year gap, but the prompt encoded a year of human research—raising the question: how much of the credit belongs to the model, and how much to the expert who guided it?

The guide reflects a growing trend among power users to create dedicated AI agent workstations, driven by Claude Code's ability to autonomously perform research, development, and system administration tasks. Gleam Is Now on Tangled
⬆ 234 pts 💬 146 HN comments

Gleam, a statically typed language for the BEAM virtual machine known for its friendly syntax and focus on scalability, has mirrored its repository on Tangled, a decentralized Git forge built on the AT Protocol (ATProto)—the same protocol underpinning Bluesky. This move is more symbolic than a migration: the canonical repository remains on GitHub, but Tangled offers a decentralized mirror that leverages ATProto’s federation model, where each user controls their own data via Personal Data Servers (PDS). The Gleam project’s decision highlights the growing interest in decentralized code hosting, particularly among communities that value protocol-level interoperability and resistance to corporate gatekeeping. Tangled, still in alpha, aims to provide a GitHub-like experience with decentralized identity and repository management, though it currently lacks features like private repos and mature CI/CD.

The announcement sparked a polarized reaction on Hacker News. Enthusiasts praised Gleam’s philosophy of minimalism and careful design, with one commenter noting the team’s hand-animated talk transitions as emblematic of their attention to detail. Tangled itself drew mixed reviews: some lauded its potential to break GitHub’s lock-in, while others were frustrated by a buggy sign-up flow, 404 errors on repos, and a confusing authentication system that forces users to learn ATProto handles. Critics questioned the wisdom of adopting a VC-funded platform (Tangled Labs, based in Finland) over established FOSS forges like Codeberg. The core Gleam team member ‘lpil’ responded, acknowledging funding concerns but emphasizing belief in ATProto’s transformative potential. This incident underscores the tension between ideological purity and pragmatic adoption in the push for decentralized infrastructure.

“While the way that Tangled is funded is not my preference, I see great potential in atproto for improving the internet.” — Gleam core team member ‘lpil’
