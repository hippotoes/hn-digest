Kimi K3 Architecture Overview and Notes
⬆ 433 pts 💬 92 HN comments

Sebastian Raschka's analysis of Kimi K3 reveals a 2.8 trillion parameter open-weight model that pushes architectural boundaries while prioritizing inference efficiency. Building on last year's Kimi Linear, K3 introduces LatentMoE (compressing linear layers via down-projection), multi-head latent attention, and Kimi Delta Attention (KDA) — a linear-time recurrent alternative to standard attention. Notably, K3 eliminates all RoPE layers in favor of NoPE (No Positional Embeddings), becoming the first frontier-scale model to do so. Attention residuals improve gradient flow across layers at modest 4% training cost overhead, while native multimodal support extends its applicability. The architecture reflects a broader trend toward efficiency-tweaked components, similar to DeepSeek V4's manifold-constrained Hyper-Connections, rejecting computationally expensive heuristics in favor of simpler, empirically validated designs.

K3's release sparks technical discourse around reproducibility, cost, and model degradation. Community members note KDA's update rule mirrors online linear regression, suggesting potential for continuous learning. However, users report performance inconsistencies: while some find K3 on par with Claude Opus 4.8, others cite failed tool calls and endless thought loops, attributing this to compute pressure or aggressive quantization. The $100/month subscription draws comparisons to $200 Claude plans, but token consumption during complex prompts raises concerns about actual cost-effectiveness. Notably, K3's open-weight nature challenges Western labs' distillation narratives, with commenters arguing Chinese labs introduce genuine innovations like NoPE and KDA. The architecture's reliance on linear attention (KDA) over standard attention raises questions about information loss, though causal masking alone appears sufficient to learn positional relationships at scale.

Kimi K3 is the first frontier-level model to eliminate positional embeddings entirely (NoPE), relying solely on causal masking and linear attention for positional understanding.

Key Highlights
  • 2.8T parameter open-weight model with LatentMoE, KDA attention, and NoPE.
  • First frontier model to remove all RoPE layers, using causal masking for position.
  • Attention residuals improve gradient flow with only 4% training overhead.
  • Mixed user reports: strong coding performance but tool call failures and cost surprises.
  • Community debates reproducibility, distillation narratives, and Chinese labs' innovation.

Discovering Cryptographic Weaknesses with Claude
⬆ 219 pts 💬 160 HN comments

Anthropic's Claude Mythos Preview has demonstrated a leap in AI-driven cryptanalysis by discovering significant weaknesses in two cryptographic primitives. The first attack targets HAWK, a post-quantum digital signature scheme under NIST consideration, halving its effective key strength by finding a nontrivial automorphism in the underlying lattice. The second improves a meet-in-the-middle attack on 7-round AES (out of 10) by a factor of 200-800× through a novel 'Möbius Bridge' technique. Both results, costing approximately $100,000 in API compute each, were achieved with minimal human guidance—one researcher for HAWK over 60 hours, and a mostly autonomous scaffold for AES that produced over a billion tokens. Crucially, neither attack breaks full deployed systems: HAWK is not yet standardized, and the AES attack applies only to a reduced-round variant under a chosen-plaintext model. The work underscores both the potential of AI to stress-test cryptographic algorithms and the verification challenges that follow from machine-generated discoveries.

The HAWK attack demonstrates that Claude could identify a previously unexploited symmetry that prior work had hypothesized but not found. By automating literature review, mathematical reasoning, and computational verification, Claude cut the expected security of HAWK-256 from 2^64 to 2^38 operations. For AES, Claude overcame initial reluctance ('it's impossible') by responding to human prompts that reframed the goal, eventually devising the Möbius Bridge to eliminate a 256-value guess and optimize downstream steps. However, verification required researchers to spend hundreds of hours learning cryptography to validate correctness. The results have broader implications: Anthropic released CryptanalysisBench for evaluating LLM cryptanalytic capability, and the company coordinated disclosure with the HAWK authors and US government. As AI models grow more powerful, the line between beneficial stress-testing and catastrophic disclosure of real-world vulnerabilities becomes thinner, prompting calls for new norms in cryptography research.

Each of the results cost roughly $100,000 in API cost to develop. — Anthropic, on the HAWK and AES attack costs

  • An improved attack on 7-round AES (200-800× faster) was discovered autonomously via a 'Möbius Bridge' technique, though it does not break full AES.
  • Both attacks required significant human verification: hundreds of hours to validate correctness and prepare papers.

Substack writers, you need a website
⬆ 549 pts 💬 282 HN comments

Elizabeth Tai's article warns writers against ceding control of their content to platforms like Substack, framing the choice as one between digital homeownership and tenant status. She argues that although Substack offers convenience and built-in distribution, writers who rely solely on the platform are vulnerable to policy changes, algorithm shifts, and loss of audience. She advocates for the POSSE (Publish on Own Site, Syndicate Elsewhere) model, where writers maintain a canonical website under their own domain and use Substack only as a distribution channel (e.g., email newsletters). Tai cites John Scalzi's 28-year-old independent blog as a gold standard of digital sovereignty, and warns that platforms like Substack can change terms abruptly, referencing past shifts at Twitter, Medium, and Reddit. The article underscores that long-term visibility and ownership require a dedicated home base, not rented space.

The Hacker News discussion (549 points, 282 comments) reveals a nuanced debate. Many commenters acknowledge the theoretical appeal of owning one's platform but highlight practical challenges: discoverability is hard without network effects, RSS remains niche, and Substack solves payment processing and email distribution cheaply (skippyfish notes Substack's push mechanism is valuable). Simon Willison shares his pragmatic approach: he publishes on his own blog first, then copy-pastes to Substack for email to 66,000 subscribers, using a custom tool. Others counter that even Substack's network effects are weak for most writers (peteforde: 'very low traffic'), and that platforms like Ghost or open protocols (AT Protocol-based Leaflet) offer independence without sacrificing distribution. The community also debates the cost of hosting and technical overhead, with some favoring static sites (Astro, Hugo) and others acknowledging Substack's simplicity as a trade-off. Overall, the thread mirrors the article's core tension: autonomy versus ease of reaching an audience.

"When you build your audience entirely on someone else’s platform, you aren’t a homeowner. You are a tenant. Or worse, a digital sharecropper."

Key Highlights
  • Own your domain and use platforms only for distribution (POSSE model).

  • Substack provides crucial distribution and payment infrastructure but at the cost of control.

Zig's Incremental Compilation Internals
⬆ 247 pts 💬 175 HN comments

Zig's incremental compilation represents a significant leap in compiler technology, enabling rebuilds in tens of milliseconds by caching and selectively recompiling only changed code. The compiler splits analysis into independent units (layout, type, value, body) and tracks dependencies between them via a graph, reducing re-analysis scope. This design, detailed by Zig core team member mlugg, leverages data-oriented patterns and pure functions for file processing, making the pipeline embarrassingly parallel. The linker employs a custom memory-mapped file abstraction (MappedFile) to incrementally patch machine code into the output binary, handling relocations and section growth without full re-linking. This approach contrasts sharply with traditional toolchains, where even small changes trigger full rebuilds.

The demo shows initial builds taking ~5 seconds and subsequent rebuilds completing in 50-70ms for a pixel editor. The article notes that Zig's language design was intentionally tweaked for incremental compilation, e.g., by limiting cross-function dependencies. This has sparked discussion on Hacker News: Rust developers note Rust's slower incremental compilation despite similar sophistication, attributing it to Rust's richer type system and dependency tracking complexity. Some question the method's reliability, citing potential binary corruption during patching, while others praise the focus on developer iteration speed as a long-overdue industry priority. The comments reflect a community deeply interested in toolchain performance tradeoffs.

Using Zig’s incremental compilation, you can make changes to real, complex applications in a matter of milliseconds — rebuilds complete in 50–70ms.

Key Highlights
  • Incremental compilation caches intermediate representations (ZIR, AIR, MIR) and tracks dependencies between analysis units.
  • Dependency graph includes source code hashes and four unit types: layout, type, value, and body of runtime functions.
  • Code generation and file processing are embarrassingly parallel; linking uses a custom MappedFile for patching.
  • Rebuild times drop to milliseconds after initial build, demonstrated with a pixel editor (5s initial, 50-70ms incremental).
  • Language design (e.g., limited cross-function dependencies) was intentionally chosen to enable this capability.
