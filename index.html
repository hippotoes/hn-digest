#3
Yes, and
⬆ 583 pts 💬 218 HN comments

Carson Gross, creator of htmx and computer science professor at Montana State University, publishes an essay titled "Yes, and..." addressing the anxious question from students and relatives: given AI's rapid advance, should one still become a programmer? His answer is a qualified yes. He grounds programming in two fundamentals: problem-solving with computers and controlling complexity. While AI can generate code, he warns that junior programmers who skip writing code rob themselves of the visceral understanding needed to read and reason about code. Drawing a sharp distinction from the common "assembly to high-level languages" analogy, Gross argues compilers are deterministic and maintain formal precision, whereas LLMs are not, often introducing accidental complexity. He advocates using AI as a teaching assistant rather than a code generator, sharing an AGENTS.md configuration to steer agents toward that pedagogical role.

The essay, which drew 583 points and 218 comments on Hacker News, offers concrete job-seeking advice: the "four F's" (family, friends, family of friends) over online lotteries, and programming skills layered onto non-tech roles (e.g., an accountant who codes). Gross predicts raw coding loses relative value while communication, business understanding, and software architecture gain. Commenters split sharply. Some, like johsole, report a 30% speed increase in feature delivery but fear fewer devs will be needed, and samstress argues learning to code is a "bad allocation of your time." Others, like vips7L, still write everything by hand, while layer8 and fxwin debate whether "deterministic" is the right term, suggesting "chaotic" instead. The networking advice drew criticism from smcg for excluding those without connections. The thread reflects a community grappling with AI's real but contested impact on their craft.

"If you can’t read the code you are going to fall into The Sorcerer’s Apprentice Trap, creating systems you don’t understand and can’t control." — Carson Gross

Key Highlights
  • Carson Gross argues programming remains viable but juniors must write code to develop intuitions.
  • AI should be used as a teaching assistant, not a code generator, to avoid the Sorcerer's Apprentice Trap.
  • The compiler-to-LLM analogy is flawed due to non-determinism and accidental complexity.
  • Job market is cyclical; networking (the 4 Fs) is more effective than online applications.
  • HN commenters debate AI's impact on job availability, with some predicting massive displacement and others defending fundamentals.

Whistle: Speech to Text in 16.9 MB
⬆ 837 pts 💬 168 HN comments

Cactus Compute's Whistle is a 16.9 MB speech-recognition model that ships as a single .cact file, runs on CPU with zero dependencies, and loads into the same C++ inference engine as their Needle foundation model. Architecturally it reuses Needle's blocks rather than copying them: an 8-block non-causal encoder (mHC residual lanes, Monarch Hadamard MLP, 128-channel conv stem that downsamples 3,000 log-mel frames to 375 frames at 80 ms), and a similarly-structured 8-block decoder at width 512 with GQA (8q:2kv), 3-tap causal conv, engram lookups, and a gated cross-attention per layer whose K/V projections are computed once per clip — so 5 beams cost 5 transcript caches, not 5 audio passes. Decoder depth is a runtime ladder (`--audio-depth`), a rare touch: every depth from 2 layers up was trained independently, while the encoder always runs whole.

The headline numbers are aggressive: 11.1 ms time-to-first-token and 1,319 decode tokens/s on an M4 Pro CPU versus Whisper base's 73.2 ms / 266 tok/s at 145.3 MB, with lower WER on LibriSpeech test-clean/test-other, SPGISpeech, Earnings-22 and FLEURS across seven languages, though Whisper wins TED-LIUM, AMI and MLS. Whistle adds word timestamps from decoder attention, per-80ms speech embeddings, Aho-Corasick keyword biasing, silence gating, and a combined mode where one binary transcribes a clip and emits tool calls as JSON (`audio_text`, `audio_language`). It ships prebuilt for 17 targets including WASI, RISC-V, MIPS and watchOS, with a Vosk-like attention to battery-scale deployment — and a 168-comment HN thread that splits sharply between benchmark admiration and on-the-ground accuracy complaints.

11.1 ms to first token, 16.9 MB on disk, 1,319 tokens/s on an M4 Pro — yet one HN tester reports Whistle recognized 70 of 170 utterances where a 1.7B Qwen ASR got 168 right, and others caught it hallucinating "cannons" for "guns" in a *A Few Good Men* clip.

Key Highlights
  • 16.9 MB single-file STT model sharing Needle's C++ engine, quantization, and attention blocks (mHC lanes, Monarch Hadamard MLP)
  • Reports 11.1 ms time-to-first-token and 1,319 tok/s on Apple M4 Pro, beating Whisper base (145.3 MB) on LibriSpeech, SPGISpeech, Earnings-22 and FLEURS but losing on TED-LIUM, AMI and MLS
  • Three capabilities from one model: transcription (7 languages), word timestamps aligned from decoder attention, and per-80 ms speech embeddings
  • Gated cross-attention per decoder layer with clip-level K/V projection, so 5-beam search reuses one encoder pass; keyword biasing via Aho-Corasick; `--audio-depth` runtime decoder ladder
  • HN reaction splits between benchmark praise and accuracy complaints: hallucinated filler on silence, weak out-of-domain context handling, and no Chinese or Nordic language support

OpenAI, the Partition Principle, and Mathematics
⬆ 160 pts 💬 240 HN comments

OpenAI released a Lean-formalized proof that the Partition Principle does not imply the Axiom of Choice — a question open for over a century in choiceless set theory — and Asaf Karagila, one of the small number of researchers who works directly on this problem, responded with a pointed refusal to engage. His objection is not primarily to correctness. Skimming the preprint, he finds it 'unclear, muddled,' with terminology 'a bit off,' unexpected lemmas stated strangely, and citations to at least three unpublished, unrefereed, non-arXiv'd lecture notes — in his judgment a desk rejection. The deeper technical tension: a formal artifact of 233,891 lines of Lean may be machine-checkable and therefore true, while remaining epistemically inert for humans, because verification has been decoupled from comprehension. Karagila frames mass, unreadable output as a Denial-of-Service on expert attention.

The public-facing numbers matter: 160 points and 240 comments on HN, a standing whisky bounty Karagila declines to pay, and field-wide precedents already in motion — arXiv rate limiting, mandatory AI-disclosure statements, open questions about whether chat logs must be shown to reviewers and editors. The comment thread splits sharply. One camp calls the preprint a 'treasure trove of mathematical results ready for the taking' and accuses experts of refusing to engage with breakthroughs; another says we have simply 'reinvented cranks sending unsolicited, poorly written putative proofs,' or, more bluntly, 'industrial scale scooping.' Karagila's chef metaphor — 'replacing the chef with the kitchen tools' — captures the policy risk: funders and policymakers who cannot distinguish a verified artifact from a scientific contribution may conclude mathematicians are replaceable.

A 233,891-line Lean proof can be verifiably correct and still unusable: as one commenter puts it, 'Lean is making the results more likely to be correct, but I think making them harder to understand.'

Key Highlights
  • Karagila argues OpenAI's Partition Principle preprint would earn a desk rejection on quality grounds: muddled structure, off terminology, odd lemmas, and citations to unpublished lecture notes rather than published literature.
  • The formalization/correctness question is orthogonal to the accessibility question: a machine-checked Lean proof of ~234k lines can be valid yet functionally unreadable, severing verification from human understanding.
  • Karagila characterizes bulk release of incomprehensible 'solutions' as a Denial-of-Service attack on scarce expert attention, not a scholarly contribution.
  • Commenters debate whether researchers owe engagement: some invoke a 'treasure trove' of results and younger mathematicians willing to wade in; others note that PhD candidates and postdocs are facing the worst job market in a decade.
  • The strategic risk is downstream: funders, policymakers, and media treating 'solutions' as proof that mathematicians can be replaced, alongside practical questions of AI-disclosure norms, arXiv rate limits, and reviewer access to chat logs.

Beauty in DVD Menus
⬆ 324 pts 💬 170 HN comments

The essay 'Beauty in DVD Menus' is a technical and cultural retrospective on the DVD menu as a brief flowering of constrained interactive design. It situates menus within the format's mid-1990s foundations: MPEG-2 video, 720x480 NTSC or 720x576 PAL raster inherited from Rec. 601/D-1, and separate storage vs. display aspect ratios. Interactivity was primitive by modern standards - subpictures limited to 2-bit color (four colors from a 16-color palette), 16 general parameter registers (GPRMs) and 24 read-only system parameter registers (SPRMs) - yet authors produced animated, diegetic interfaces, hidden easter eggs, and even playable games. The piece traces how 4:3 safe areas persisted as a design habit, and how menus became a selling point over VHS/LaserDisc.

Concrete examples anchor the analysis: Disney's FastPlay auto-played trailers before films, EasyFind sought consistent navigation, and menus for Scooby-Doo 2, Harry Potter and the Prisoner of Azkaban, Shrek 2, The Rocky Horror Picture Show, Wayne's World, and Doctor Who exploited the DVD VM's tiny state machine. HN commenters split sharply. One notes, 'The only beauty in DVD Menus was that watching them meant we actually owned the content and nobody could remotely pull the plug on it.' Others recall Memento's backward-playback easter egg, Shrek's Revoice Studio, or Monty Python's 'Menu Reader for the Hard of Hearing.' The counterweight: 'I hated DVD menus,' and unskippable ads, slow navigation, and buggy players. Blu-ray's complex, proprietary Java authoring and RAM limits help explain the decline; streaming's sterile interfaces completed it.

A HN commenter distilled the era's real appeal: 'The only beauty in DVD Menus was that watching them meant we actually owned the content and nobody could remotely pull the plug on it.'

Key Highlights
  • DVD menus relied on MPEG-2 video, 2-bit subpictures (4 colors from 16-color palette), GPRMs/SPRMs, and a primitive DVD VM state machine.
  • Aspect ratio handling - 720x480 NTSC / 720x576 PAL, SAR vs. DAR/PAR, and 4:3 safe areas - shaped menu design long after widescreen adoption.
  • Memorable menus (Scooby-Doo 2, Shrek 2, Rocky Horror, Wayne's World, Doctor Who) included games, easter eggs, and diegetic interfaces.
  • Disney's FastPlay and EasyFind standardized navigation but also became emblematic of unskippable trailers and corporate control.
  • Modern Blu-ray/streaming menus rarely match this creativity; Blu-ray authoring complexity, proprietary Java, RAM limits, and streaming's generic UIs are cited as causes.

The value of not getting to the point (2015)
Ken Arneson's 2015 essay — resurfaced on Hacker News to 198 points and 70 comments — makes a structural rather than sentimental claim: small talk is infrastructure, not ornament. The opening observation, that shared food and drink 'spare us from the burden of needing to have something to say,' recasts conversation as a protocol whose preamble exists to absorb the cost of silence. Arneson then generalizes: language is lossy compression of feeling, our rationalizations of desire are frequently incoherent, and we intuit that incoherence well enough to be cautious. Speech therefore carries inherent vulnerability, and cultural rituals — chit-chat, coffee, lunch — function as trust-establishment handshakes that lower the perceived penalty of saying the wrong thing to the wrong person. His confessed fifty-year blind spot is framed, half-jokingly, through the Dunning-Kruger effect. The concrete case study is his college-freshman daughter, who texted 'I want to talk'; when he asked 'what about?' she was annoyed, then asked to 'just talk about something stupid.' Protocol Handshake Framing This cohort reframes small talk as link-layer negotiation rather than content. nine_k's modem analogy is the thread's most-quoted: two modems 'send to each other signals that are intended to assess how the line behaves, how well the other side hears you, and what it supports,' concluding that skipping it 'may result in a badly botched communication attempt.' asdfman123 extends it to feline ethology ('letting a cat sniff your hand'), and vaultdweller101 cites Robin Dunbar's argument that speech evolved as a cheaper substitute for grooming. veunes adds the sharpest refinement: 'Being trustworthy and being perceived as trustworthy aren't the same thing' — the other party hasn't had a chance to establish the former yet. Efficiency Absolutists A vocal minority rejects the essay's premise outright. jwpapi calls the article and its commentariat 'cope,' stating plainly that small talk leaves him feeling he 'wasted my time' and ranking it alongside scrolling television. charcircuit labels the daughter's half-hour warmup 'a waste of time,' asking why a conversation that could finish 'within a single minute' should take thirty. artyom admits the framing is 'kind of embarrassing' for spectrum-adjacent fathers, but insists on 'a very strong reason' to endure 'such long pointless conversation.' u8080 reports fatigue from mutual performance-of-interest, describing small talk as 'people pretending to be interested in you while you pretend to be interested in them.' Emotional Maturity Reading paimapi offers the thread's most-cited reframe: this is less 'a practice of rhetoric' than 'a practice of emotional maturity,' since you cannot know a counterpart's current emotional state, formative pressures, or shifting moral commitments. solatic supplies a negative case — a boss who allowed 'absolutely no room for nuance' and was the worst he ever worked for. rexpop is openly angry at charcircuit: 'People are not vending machines!' and demands 'haven't you ever loved someone enough to give them a half hour of simply sharing their mind.' momojo credits the piece's humility, noting it likely would have been mocked on today's platforms. vigilantpuma adds that any parent should be glad a college freshman wants thirty minutes. (~16 users)
Platform & Community Deficit Rather than blaming brevity, this group blames absent community. tyg13 argues online spaces lack continuity: 'Why would I spend a bunch of time idly having Seinfeldian conversations with strangers with whom I have no rapport?' — years of commenting yielded 'precisely zero lasting relationships.' dale_glass counters that it worked for him via IRC channels and Discord servers with 'the same dozen regulars.' SecretDreams asks how the 2015 author would read today's internet. tonymet indicts contemporary rhetoric for preferring passive-aggressive 'I don't understand' when the speaker means 'I disagree.' sarreph notes the positive inverse: suspense and build-up make a point land memorably.
