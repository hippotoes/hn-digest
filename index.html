Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Mythos 5.1
⬆ 1306 pts 💬 1227 HN comments

Today Anthropic released Claude Fable 5.1 and its restricted twin Claude Mythos 5.1. While both are the same underlying model, Fable ships with standard safeguards and Mythos is reserved for trusted-access work in cybersecurity and the life sciences. The headline engineering change is a new Enterprise Frontier Safeguard (EFS) that stores all data in customer-controlled cloud infrastructure, eliminating Anthropic-side retention—a direct answer to enterprise privacy concerns. Pricing falls by 25–45% on typical or highly agentic workloads by slashing cache-read prices from $1/M to $0.25/M. On benchmarks the model shows clear advances: Terminal-Bench-Science jumps to 52.6% from Fable 5's 24.7%, OSWorld strict reaches 41.7%, and Mythos 5.1 hits 60.9% on Agentic Terminal-Bench 4.0. The model is more apt at long-horizon, multi-step work, finding a rare crash that had eluded Millennium's engineers for years.

However, the community is split. With speed and cost of open-weight models improving, Anthropic's frontier narrative is under pressure to show real-world value, not just leaderboard supremacy.

Fable 5.1 solves a crash no engineer at Millennium could explain for four years—and Anthropic says it costs up to 45% less on agentic workloads.

The efficient frontier of LLM inference
⬆ 132 pts 💬 37 HN comments

Baseten's Philip Kiely frames LLM inference optimization through the lens of an efficient frontier, distinguishing between techniques that exert a latency-throughput tradeoff and those that shift the frontier outward entirely. The former category includes batch sizing, parallelism strategy (TP, EP, ADP), and quantization—the latter of which introduces its own jagged quality-versus-efficiency frontier that is often unintuitive and needs empirical sweeps. Tradeoff-controlling knobs let operators target a specific point on cost-latency-throughput space, but they do not yield universal gains. By contrast, kernel/runtime optimization, modern speculative decoding (EAGLE-3, DSpark, DFlash), and P/D disaggregation push the entire frontier outward, compounding gains and allowing reallocation of new efficiency toward either lower latency or higher throughput. The article is explicitly written for inference practitioners running large models like GLM-5.3 or Kimi K3 in agentic coding workloads.

Kiely emphasizes that the efficient frontier in inference is not a smooth curve but jagged, with disproportionate shifts near cutoff points; these can only be discovered by systematic testing. Speculative decoding, once only practical at small batch sizes due to high speculation cost, has matured to the point of being a default in major open-source engines, especially for predictable code generation. Disaggregation is highlighted as still underappreciated, splitting prefill (compute-bound) from decode (memory-bound) onto dedicated workers to tactically absorb traffic patterns. The HN comment section sharpens these claims: one engineer notes that many techniques have not fundamentally changed and that architecture-level choices dominate efficiency, while another pushes back that P/D disaggregation and new speculative-decoding variants are genuinely net-new. The article closes with a practical bent, but the commenters add nuance about hardware realism, Pareto vs. efficient-frontier terminology, and security concerns from speculative execution.

Speculative decoding, once limited to small batch sizes, now yields skipped forward passes and higher tokens-per-second per user — a clear example of pushing the inference efficiency frontier outward rather than merely trading latency for throughput.

Key Highlights
  • Inference optimization splits into tradeoff-targeting techniques (batch size, parallelism, quantization) vs. frontier-shifting techniques (kernel tuning, speculative decoding, disaggregation).
  • The inference frontier is jagged and must be discovered empirically through sweeps, not modeled as a smooth mathematical line.
  • Quantization pushes out the serving-efficiency frontier but adds a separate quality tradeoff, with MXFP4/NVFP4 formats minimizing quality loss.
  • Speculative decoding has matured from a costly niche technique to a broadly adopted default, especially for code generation.
  • P/D disaggregation separates compute-bound prefill from memory-bound decode, allowing better ratio tuning per workload.

The ChatGPT/Codex app bundles a full copy of LibreOffice
⬆ 440 pts 💬 202 HN comments

Simon Willison discovered that the ChatGPT/Codex desktop app, when installed, stores a 1.7GB `codex-primary-runtime` under `~/.cache`. Inside it, OpenAI bundles not just a Python runtime and a Node.js runtime, but also native binaries for Poppler, git, and the LibreOffice office suite. A `plugins/documents` directory includes skills that explicitly teach the agent where and how to invoke those binaries. This matters because language-model agents are increasingly asked to touch `.docx`, `.xlsx`, and `.pptx` files. Office formats are notoriously labyrinthine: they combine XML, legacy OLE2 containers, and decades of compatibility quirks. Rather than trying to make an LLM reimplement that complexity in token space, OpenAI delegates document conversion and rendering to a mature open-source component. That is the classic engineering pattern of using a trusted subprocess instead of rebuilding the world: the model orchestrates, and LibreOffice does the dirty file-format work.

Hacker News commenters split sharply. Some developers independently bundle LibreOffice in their own tools because legacy `xls` files are unwieldy; as one put it, nothing else gives confidence that it will "just read anything I give it." Others see Microsoft-sized bloat in a chat application: one user wrote that the hard drive "just let out an audible sigh," while another compared the situation to Anthropic's Claude app silently installing a 10GB VM. A more philosophical thread asks whether OpenAI needing LibreOffice means LLMs are still worse than human developers at handling document formats; the counter-argument is that it would be "dumb as hell" to have a model rewrite Word from scratch. Several users also questioned whether the MPL-2.0 license text is properly shipped. The result is a small but revealing snapshot of the real runtime dependencies hiding behind ChatGPT's UI.

Even a company whose core product is AI coding agents ships a full copy of LibreOffice—an illustration that decades-old document formats remain harder for models than code.

Ambient CSS v3 – Blender meets CSS
⬆ 285 pts 💬 82 HN comments

Ambient CSS v3 is presented as a “physics-based lighting system for CSS,” a front-end demo that brings a 3D renderer’s mental model into the DOM. It layers directional light, elevation, material surfaces, and shadows over ordinary HTML components; a small control panel lets users alter lighting direction and tweak materials in real time.
