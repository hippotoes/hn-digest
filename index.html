Show HN: Kakehashi – Experimental userspace to run macOS binaries on Linux ARM
⬆ 223 pts 💬 56 HN comments

Kakehashi is an experimental userspace translation layer that lets Linux aarch64 machines run unmodified macOS ARM64 command-line binaries. Rather than emulating instructions, the Rust project parses Darwin Mach-O files, maps them into memory, and substitutes a freestanding libSystem.B.dylib; BSD syscalls are intercepted in user space and translated for Linux. The architecture is split into kh-loader (Mach-O load/execute), kh-runtime (memory, traps, bottle), and kh-libsystem (the guest dylib). The authors explicitly state 'This project is not derived from Darling,' though HN immediately drew comparisons. On UTM/Docker, real Darwin binaries—7-Zip 7zz, curl, and Xcode Command Line Tools git—already pass non-trivial gates, including multi-threaded 7zz compression on a ~8k-file tree and over 200 curl option combinations.

The headline trade-off is performance versus CI cost. Kakehashi runs guest code natively, so overhead comes from syscall-boundary work—TLS switches, alt stacks, NEON save/restore. A large 7z archive measured ~5.2x slower than native Linux, yet the README argues the billable math can still favor Linux arm64 runners: macOS hosted minutes are roughly 10-12x more expensive per minute. That framing resonated with commenters wanting iOS builds on Linux or AU audio plugins, while others questioned whether the project will outlive the 'Darling curse' of mac-only software demand. The author disclosed ~6 days of active development using Grok 4.5, sparking a clean-room/AI-derivation debate, and the classic HN naming controversy over 'Kakehashi' drew cultural pushback.

At ~5.2x slower than native Linux, Kakehashi can still win on cost: Linux arm64 runners at $0.005/min vs macOS at $0.062/min make a 5x-slower job roughly 40% cheaper.

Key Highlights
  • User-space, no-JIT approach: Mach-O loader + freestanding libSystem + BSD syscall translation on Linux aarch64.
  • Real Darwin binaries pass: multi-threaded 7zz, 200+ curl options, basic git.
  • ~5.2x slowdown on large multi-file 7z, dominated by syscall boundary not emulation.
  • CI economics could favor Linux arm64 runners despite slowdown due to 10-12x lower per-minute cost.
  • Community debates Darling lineage, LLM-derived 'light-gray room' clean-room status, and niche demand viability.

SwiftUI After 7 Years
⬆ 206 pts 💬 186 HN comments

The article 'SwiftUI After 7 Years' (ykvm.com) argues that Apple's declarative UI framework has remained a 'story of mediocrity' despite seven years of releases. The author contends that SwiftUI's reactive data flow is unpredictable, that it often ignores relevant state changes and responds to irrelevant ones, and that core components like NavigationStack, GeometryReader, and list rendering have only recently matured—or remain inadequate for complex desktop apps. Because the original article was inaccessible, this summary is constructed primarily from 186 HN comments, which reveal a community split between those who view SwiftUI as an unfinished, ever-beta product and those who believe its perceived shortcomings stem from developer inexperience or outdated iOS deployment targets.

The discussion escalated into broader questions about Apple's engineering culture. Critics pointed to AppKit's superiority, Swift's complexity, and the fact that Apple's own apps (Settings, Spotlight) built with SwiftUI feel worse. Defenders noted that SwiftUI is now used by hundreds of thousands of production apps and that iOS 26/27 closed much of the performance gap. Significant sub-threads compared SwiftUI to WPF, UWP, Compose, and Flutter, debating whether declarative reactive UI is fundamentally flawed for complex applications. A notable exchange featured Miguel de Icaza replying 'skills issue,' citing his own 727-file SwiftUI codebase as evidence that mastery overcomes friction. The conversation reflects a deeper anxiety about the future of native development and AI's role in scaffolding UI code.

SwiftUI is the type of framework that makes the easy things easier to accomplish but the harder things harder.

Key Highlights
  • Article claims SwiftUI's reactive data flow is unpredictable and that complex apps require dropping down to UIKit/AppKit.
  • Commenters criticized SwiftUI's maturity, calling it a 'seven-year beta' and comparing it to WPF/UWP.
  • Defenders argue SwiftUI is production-ready, with 100% SwiftUI apps in the App Store and significant improvements in iOS 26+.
  • The thread debated declarative-reactive vs imperative UI paradigms, with some claiming Autolayout/Cocoa remains superior.
  • Broader criticism of Apple's developer experience: poor docs, video-only tutorials, and Swift's complexity; AI is seen as both mitigating and exacerbating these issues.

The myth of Snow Leopard
⬆ 106 pts 💬 84 HN comments

Rubenerd's essay dismantles the popular myth that Mac OS X 10.6 'Snow Leopard' was a perfectly stable, zero-feature bug-fix release. Apple marketed it as having '0 new features,' but the author reminds readers that he personally downgraded twice due to severe Finder crashes, broken FireWire 800 ExpressCard support, and iMovie HD plugin issues. The post links to Jeff Johnson's catalog of Snow Leopard bugs and notes the release actually introduced huge architectural changes such as Grand Central Dispatch. The myth persists, Rubenerd argues, not because Snow Leopard was truly exemplary, but because its memory has been weaponized as a proxy for the industry's collective craving for stability and quality-of-life improvements amid the 'enshittification' of software. This framing gives the essay its resonance and turns a niche Apple history lesson into a broader commentary on modern development priorities.

The Hacker News thread confirms a deep split in collective memory. Former Apple engineer LegNeato says he 'personally ran 10.6 security updates' and triaged every bug, yet notes it was an 'architecturally huge release' that rewrote Mail and introduced GCD. Meanwhile vor_ points to an infamous data loss bug in guest accounts, and lisper linked to his own contemporaneous complaint calling Snow Leopard a disaster. Users like gord288 reconcile this by arguing the OS only became 'rock-solid' by the final 10.6.8 point release, implying people compare the mature, battle-hardened version against brand-new releases. The thread also shows the broader takeaway: comments longing for a 'Snow Leopard' of Linux or Android, and criticisms of annual OS upgrade cadence and AI features, reveal an industry-wide desire for refinement over novelty.

Apple marketed Snow Leopard as having '0 new features', yet it rewrote Mail, added Grand Central Dispatch, and shipped an infamous guest-account data-loss bug—a legend built on the final 10.6.8 point release, not 10.6.0.

Key Highlights
  • Snow Leopard was marketed as having '0 new features' but actually contained major architectural changes like Grand Central Dispatch and a rewritten Mail.
  • The article author downgraded twice because of severe Finder, FireWire, and iMovie instability, contradicting the perfect-stability legend.
  • Former Apple engineers confirm it was a huge architectural release, but also acknowledge real bugs and the influence of iOS resource shifts.
  • The 'legend' was largely formed after the final 10.6.8 point release, which was far more stable than the initial 10.6.0 launch.
  • The term 'Snow Leopard' has become a broader metaphor for users craving stability and polish in an industry obsessed with new features and enshittification.
